# Foreword
## Britain, in Private

This book began as a map, and the map began as an argument I lost.

I had said — over dinner, with the confidence of a man who has not checked — that Britain has no erotic history worth the name. That we are a nation of raised eyebrows and separate bedrooms, and that if you want the good stuff you go to Venice, or Paris, or anywhere with better weather and a looser grip on the concept of shame. Somebody disagreed. Somebody produced, with the air of a card player who has been waiting all evening, a single fact about a Dorset hillside. And that was the end of my dinner and the beginning of this book.

So I started putting pins in a map. Not metaphorical pins — actual coordinates, actual streets, actual buildings you can stand outside and feel faintly odd about. And within about a month a pattern emerged that I have not been able to unsee since: the pins do not fall randomly. They cluster. And they cluster, with an almost comic regularity, along edges.

They gather on the wrong bank of rivers. They pile up on the far side of city walls, just past the gate where the corporation's writ ran out. They collect eight miles over the Scottish border, in a village whose entire economy was built on the fact that English law stopped at a small stream. They accumulate in basements, on islands, on moorland, in the unlit end of a pleasure garden — and in one instance in a wood on the edge of Hampstead long associated with clandestine encounters. Britain's erotic history is not merely a history. It is a geography problem. Tell me where the authorities' jurisdiction ended, and I will tell you where everybody went.

This turns out to be the key to the whole thing, and it explains the great national contradiction that visitors have been remarking on for four hundred years — that we are simultaneously among the most repressed and the most inventive people in Europe. Both are true. The repression is real; the debates are there in Hansard and the laws in the statute book, drearily, century after century. But so is the ingenuity, and the ingenuity is what this book is actually about. Every wall built in this country to keep desire out has had, within about a fortnight, a door cut in it by somebody enterprising — and quite often that somebody was charging admission.

The evidence, I should warn you, is strange stuff, and the Introduction that follows explains why. What I noticed first was simply how much of it I was reading over somebody's shoulder — a constable's, a magistrate's, a blackmailer's. Very little of this was written down by the people it happened to.

This means that erotic history is inseparable from every other kind of British history — and that is one of the pleasures of the thing. You cannot tell it without the coal boom that built a port, or the railway that built a resort, or the Reformation, the Restoration, the Blitz and the Pill. A bishop's rent roll helps explain a red-light district. A change in marriage law creates an industry. A fashion for sea-bathing produces a moral panic, a magistrate and eventually a postcard. Follow desire through these islands and you find yourself, almost by accident, being told the story of the place — its class system, its lawyers, its landladies, its genius for the licensing committee — from an angle nobody teaches at school.

And what it reveals about us is, I think, rather flattering, though not in the way one might hope.

We are not, as a people, given to abandon. Let us be honest about that. Other nations own that story and are welcome to it. What we are is discreet — and discretion is not the same thing as shame, though the two have been confused by every foreign visitor since the sixteenth century. Shame hides a thing because it believes the thing is wrong. Discretion hides it because it believes the thing is nobody else's business — which is a completely different proposition, and a far more robust one. It is the instinct that puts the inscription on the inside of the ring. It is the small door with no sign on it. It is the shop that wraps the parcel without comment and asks after your mother.

The censors, meanwhile, are the comic relief, and I make no apology for enjoying them. A gentleman's pornography collection was accepted by a great national museum, catalogued and locked in a cupboard, where it survived precisely because it had been locked in a cupboard. This is a country that has rarely managed to suppress anything but has developed world-beating expertise in filing it.

The arc, though — and this is the part I did not expect when I started sticking in pins — bends. It genuinely does. You begin with women working in licensed houses from which a bishop drew rent, and you end within living memory with the same trade conducted by women, for women, on their own terms and in their own front rooms. Somewhere between those points it came out of the shadows, stopped being furtive and — this is the part I care about — started being good. Well made. Beautiful, even.

At which point I must declare an interest, and I would rather do it here than have you find it in a footnote. I sell things — beautiful things, for private use, from a shop in Mayfair. Whether that gives me any business writing this book is a question I return to at the end, when you will be in a position to judge it. For now it is enough that you know.

One last thing, and then I will get out of your way.

Everything here is sourced, and the book tells you where certainty ends. This subject has been written about for centuries by pornographers, moralists and people with an axe to grind, which means that a great deal of what “everyone knows” turns out to have been invented by a Victorian with a theory and a subscription list. Where the record is solid, I say so. Where it is folklore, I say that too.

Take a coat. Some of this is outdoors.

*The Marquis de Mayfair*  
*Mayfair, London*
