# PASS 2 EVIDENCE AUDIT — "The Erotic Map of Britain", Topics 66–70

## Executive summary

Topics 66–70 are substantially clear to print subject to a short, well-defined list of corrections. This pass resolves all eight outstanding questions and re-tests the remaining significant claims in the connective matter.

Of the eight questions, five survive as written or with only minor tightening (the Vatican letters, the Georgiana print count, the Abbots Bromley antler date, the Shaftesbury Fountain costs, and the existence of a Gateways plaque). Three require correction or firm qualification:

- **Ffynnon Fair** — any figure larger than "seven marriages, two of them clandestine" is wrong and must be corrected.
- **Gretna Green** — the "over 4,000 a year / one in eight of Scottish weddings" formulation is industry-sourced and dated; it must be reframed and attributed.
- **The Gateways Club** — the blue plaque is no longer "pending": it was unveiled on 2 July 2025 and the manuscript must say so; and the "longest-running lesbian club in the world" superlative must be attributed, not stated as fact.

The full Part Two re-check found the connective matter sound on almost every point. The only substantive factual fixes are the Ffynnon Fair count, the Gretna Green statistics, the Gateways plaque update, the Emma Hamilton date (drop any "15 or 16" hedge — it is 15 January 1815), and the presentation of the Holywell Street class-and-price argument as historians' interpretation rather than Lord Campbell's own words. Nothing found requires REMOVE. One item — the precise date of the Post Office's dismissal of the Cleveland Street telegraph boys (given as 6 December 1889) — could not be confirmed to publication standard in this pass and is flagged for a final primary-source check.

## Part One — the eight unresolved questions

### 1. Ffynnon Fair — the clandestine marriage count — VERDICT: CORRECT

**Finding.** The primary source is *Y Cwtta Cyfarwydd*, the chronicle kept by the notary Peter Roberts of Cefn Meiriadog, Denbighshire, between 1607 and 1646, published in the edition of the Rev. Canon D. R. Thomas (London, 1883). Roberts's original manuscript is now British Library Add. MS 33373. Over the forty-year run Roberts records roughly 163 marriages in the area; of the marriages he records outside a recognised church, a proportion are noted as clandestine.

For the chapel of Ffynnon Fair (St Mary's Well) at Wigfair/Wickwer specifically, the published edition records **seven marriages, between 1611 and 1640**, of which **only two are explicitly described as clandestine**:
- **7 February 1611** — Piers Gruffydd ab John Gruffydd and Jane verch Thomas (his second wife), "married clandestinely."
- **4 November 1626** — John ap Richard of Coyber (Cwybr) and **Elizabeth Lloyd** (a daughter of Evan Lloyd of Wigfair), "clandestinely married … about twilight." (This is the marriage the manuscript dates to "1626"; the precise date is 4 November 1626. Elizabeth was Roberts's god-daughter, aged about 19–20, and hers is the only one of the four Lloyd-sister marriages with no recorded marriage portion — consistent with an elopement or an unfavoured match.)

The other five are the respectable weddings of a leading local Catholic family: four of the seven were daughters of Evan Lloyd of Wigfair (Katherine and Anne in April 1615, Elizabeth in 1626, Marie in 1633), three with substantial marriage portions (£180–£300). The "cluster" at the chapel is therefore a family pattern, not evidence of a marriage market. As the Well Hopper analysis (drawing directly on the Thomas edition) concludes, "if the epithet of the Gretna Green of Wales is based solely on the *Cwtta Cyfarwydd* then it appears to be entirely misplaced."

The "Welsh Gretna Green" tag is traceable to a specific Victorian source: **J. O. Halliwell, *Notes of Family Excursions in North Wales* (1860)**, who wrote that "some time after the Reformation, this place should have been a kind of Gretna Green. Here runaway couples were married." It was repeated by later guide-writers (Jenkinson, 1878; Baring-Gould, 1903).

**Recommended manuscript wording:** *"The chapel of Ffynnon Fair near St Asaph acquired a Victorian reputation as 'the Gretna Green of Wales' — a phrase traceable to J. O. Halliwell's* Notes of Family Excursions in North Wales *(1860). The reputation rests on the chronicle of the notary Peter Roberts,* Y Cwtta Cyfarwydd, *which records just seven marriages at the chapel between 1611 and 1640, only two of them described as clandestine — and four of them daughters of a single local family."*

### 2. Gretna Green — current wedding figures — VERDICT: CORRECT / QUALIFY

**Finding.** The widely repeated "over 4,000 a year / around one in eight of all Scottish weddings" is a blend of industry and older figures and should not stand as a bare current fact. The defensible position:

- **Share of Scottish weddings.** The often-quoted "about one in eight" is corroborated by the BBC, which reported (2020) that "the Gretna Green area usually hosts about 12% of Scotland's weddings." Dumfries and Galloway Council puts the figure higher for the registration office, citing "a whopping 17% of Scotland's weddings taking place at Gretna Registration Office alone." So "one in eight" is a conservative, sourced figure; the council's own claim is higher.
- **Annual volume.** The operator Gretna Green Ltd states on its FAQ, verbatim, "Every year about 5,000 couples marry in this area" — an industry figure for the wider area, not an independently audited venue count.
- **Trend since 2004.** Bookings at the traditional venues have fallen steeply. Per the *Scottish Daily Mail* (13 February 2017): "On Valentine's Day in 2004, which fell on a Saturday, a record 194 couples wed. By 2009, this had fallen to 48. By last year — another Valentine Saturday — the number saying 'I do' was down to 37," with only 17 booked for Valentine's Day 2017.
- **Council-area standing.** Dumfries and Galloway remains one of Scotland's leading areas for weddings because of Gretna; National Records of Scotland notes that for same-sex marriage in the decade to 2024 "Dumfries and Galloway came third with 1,379 thanks to the pull of Gretna."

**Verdict:** The "one in eight" share is defensible if attributed (BBC ~12%; council claims 17% at the registration office). The "5,000 a year" figure is industry-sourced (Gretna Green Ltd) and should be attributed and framed as an area figure. Any "over 4,000 a year" phrasing should be replaced.

**Recommended manuscript wording:** *"Gretna and Gretna Green still draw a remarkable share of Scotland's weddings — the BBC puts it at about 12 per cent of the national total, and Dumfries and Galloway Council claims as much as 17 per cent pass through Gretna's registration office alone. The operator reports 'about 5,000 couples' marrying in the area each year, though bookings at the traditional venues have fallen sharply since a Valentine's-Day peak of 194 couples in 2004."*

### 3. Henry VIII's letters to Anne Boleyn in the Vatican — VERDICT: VERIFIED (attribution required)

**Finding.** **Seventeen** letters in Henry's own hand survive, bound as a single codex in the Vatican Library, shelfmark **Vat. lat. 3731, part A, folios 1r–16r** (confirmed by Estelle Paranque's 2024 article in *The Court Historian*/Taylor & Francis, which cites the manuscript directly and the digitised copy at digi.vatlib.it). They date to 1527–28, are undated individually, and were digitised in 2016. Eight are in English and nine in French (per the older Gutenberg edition's count).

The transmission theory the manuscript refers to was advanced by **Peter Lake, Michael Questier, Kendra Packham and Estelle Paranque, "A Real Papist Plot: How did Henry VIII's love letters to Anne Boleyn get to the Vatican?", *TLS: The Times Literary Supplement*, no. 6250 (online 11 January 2023; print date 13 January 2023)**. The argument is that the letters were still together in England in 1601, in the possession of an English Jesuit who promised to send them to a colleague in Rome writing on the English Reformation — displacing the older, evidence-free theory that the papal legate Cardinal Campeggio removed them around 1529.

**Verdict:** The seventeen-letter figure and the shelfmark are solid. The 1601-Jesuit route is the **most recent scholarly hypothesis and remains speculative** — it must be attributed and not presented as settled. Paranque's own 2024 article calls it what "the most recent theory speculates."

**Recommended manuscript wording:** *"Seventeen love letters in Henry's own hand survive in the Vatican Library (Vat. lat. 3731A). How they reached Rome is unknown; the most recent theory, advanced by Peter Lake, Michael Questier, Kendra Packham and Estelle Paranque in the* TLS *in January 2023, argues they were still in England in 1601 and were carried to Rome by an English Jesuit. This remains a hypothesis."*

### 4. Georgiana, Duchess of Devonshire — the 1784 print campaign — VERDICT: VERIFIED

**Finding.** The figure is correct and correctly attributed. Amelia Rauser writes, verbatim: **"At least eighty-nine different political prints survive from the month of April 1784 alone"** (Amelia Rauser, "The Butcher-Kissing Duchess of Devonshire: Between Caricature and Allegory in 1784," *Eighteenth-Century Studies*, vol. 36, no. 1 (Fall 2002), p. 23). The manuscript figure of "eighty-nine or more, for April 1784" attributed to Rauser is accurate.

**Verdict:** Stands. Phrase as "at least eighty-nine … from April 1784 alone" and attribute to Rauser. (Note the "eighty-nine" is Rauser's count of *surviving* prints from that single month, not a total for the whole campaign, and not confined to prints attacking Georgiana specifically — the manuscript should not overstate it as "hostile prints of Georgiana.")

### 5. Abbots Bromley Horn Dance — the antler date — VERDICT: VERIFIED

**Finding.** Per the *Oxford Dictionary of English Folklore* (Simpson & Roud, via Oxford Reference), verbatim: **"a radiocarbon dating test carried out on one of them in 1976 gave a mean date of ad 1065 ± 80 years … these particular horns could have been imported at any point in the custom's history."** Reindeer were extinct in Britain long before the eleventh century, so the antlers were almost certainly imported, most likely from Scandinavia. The first documentary reference to a hobby-horse performance at Abbots Bromley is 1532; the first explicit mention of the reindeer antlers is Robert Plot's *Natural History of Staffordshire* (1686).

**Verdict:** Stands, with the "± 80 years" margin retained. Caveat for the manuscript: this is a single test on one antler, and the *import* date is unknown — the c.1065 date dates the animal's death, not the antlers' arrival in Staffordshire or the origin of the dance. The published laboratory and citation detail beyond "1976 test" is not established in reference works consulted; do not name a laboratory unless a primary radiocarbon report is located.

### 6. The Shaftesbury Memorial Fountain — Gilbert's costs — VERDICT: VERIFIED

**Finding.** Per the *Survey of London* (vols 31–32, pt 2, British History Online): *"For Gilbert the Shaftesbury Memorial was, in his own words, 'financially a great débâcle'. His commission was for £3,000, but the memorial is said to have cost him £7,000, partly because he had to pay a very high price for the copper."* The overspend arose because promised gunmetal was never supplied and Gilbert, having already made his casts, had to buy copper. Crucially, **the £7,000 figure rests substantially on Gilbert's own later testimony**, not on an independent audit — the *Survey* notes the records of the Shaftesbury Memorial Committee are missing, and Gilbert himself preferred to "draw the veil here over the cost of the fountain."

Subsequent facts, documented: Gilbert was appointed Professor of Sculpture at the Royal Academy in 1900; filed for **bankruptcy in 1901**; withdrew to **Bruges** (visiting periodically from 1901, settling there about 1909); **resigned from the Royal Academy** amid the scandal of his affairs in the mid-1900s; returned to England in 1926; was **reinstated to the RA and knighted in 1932**; and died in 1934.

**Verdict:** Stands. The manuscript should phrase the £7,000 as "said to have cost him" and note the figure rests largely on Gilbert's own account.

### 7. Holywell Street — the price of pornography and the class argument — VERDICT: QUALIFY

**Finding.** Lord Campbell's actual words in the debate that gave rise to the Obscene Publications Act 1857 are, verbatim, from Hansard (Lords, 11 May 1857): he had "learned with horror and alarm that a sale of poison more deadly than prussic acid, strichnine, or arsenic — the sale of obscene publications and indecent books — was openly going on." At the Bill's Third Reading (Lords, 13 July 1857) he read a letter from Mr Pritchard, secretary of the Society for the Suppression of Vice, detailing fifty-five years of prosecutions. Campbell repeatedly framed the Bill as aimed at protecting the young and vulnerable, and stated (as quoted in the 1959 Lords debate) that "it is not within my most distant contemplation that works of literature should be affected by this Bill at all … This Bill is aimed exclusively at those who have the single purpose of corrupting the morals of youth."

**What the evidence does NOT show:** Campbell did not, in the recorded debates, make an explicit *price/class* argument — i.e. he is not on record saying prosecution should intensify because pornography had become cheap enough for working-class buyers. That causal claim is a **defensible modern scholarly interpretation** (associated with historians of Victorian obscenity such as Lynda Nead and Iain McCalman), consistent with Campbell's stated anxiety about material reaching "the young and the poor," but it is not a Campbell quotation and must not be presented as one.

**Verdict:** The class-and-price argument may stand **as historians' interpretation**, buttressed by Campbell's documented concern for the young and vulnerable. It must not be attributed to Campbell's own words. Specific mid-century price data (the cheap street trade versus the expensive collector's market) was not established to publication standard in this pass and would need a specialist secondary source (e.g. Nead, *Victorian Babylon*; McCalman, *Radical Underworld*) if concrete prices are to be quoted. See residual risk register.

**Recommended manuscript wording:** *"Lord Campbell told the Lords in 1857 that the trade was 'a sale of poison more deadly than prussic acid, strychnine, or arsenic', and pressed his Bill as a shield for 'the morals of youth'. Historians argue that prosecution intensified as pornography grew cheap enough to reach working-class and youthful buyers — an interpretation consistent with Campbell's stated fears, though not with any words of his about price."*

### 8. The Gateways Club — plaque and superlative — VERDICT: CORRECT

**Finding — the plaque.** An **English Heritage blue plaque was unveiled on 2 July 2025** at 239 King's Road, Chelsea (SW3 5EJ), Royal Borough of Kensington and Chelsea, reading that the Gateways Club was "the best-known and longest lived lesbian social venue" in London. At the time of the 2022 documentary *Gateways Grind* the application to English Heritage was still pending; it has since been granted and installed. The manuscript, if it says the plaque is "proposed" or "pending," is now out of date and must be corrected.

**Finding — the superlative.** The global claim is made by several parties on differing bases. English Heritage itself uses the careful "London's best-known and longest-lived lesbian social venue." Wikipedia and the LGBT Archive assert it was "the longest-surviving such club in the world" (open by 1931, closed 21 September 1985). Press coverage (Smithsonian, the *Gateways Grind* synopsis) hedges to "one of the world's longest-running/longest-surviving lesbian nightclubs." Because the global superlative is asserted rather than demonstrated, it should be attributed and softened.

**Verdict:** CORRECT the plaque status (unveiled 2 July 2025, English Heritage). Attribute the "world's longest-running" superlative; state as fact only the more modest "London's longest-lived lesbian venue" and the run of dates.

**Recommended manuscript wording:** *"The Gateways operated at 239 King's Road, Chelsea, from about 1931 until 1985 — described by English Heritage as London's best-known and longest-lived lesbian venue, and often called one of the longest-running lesbian clubs in the world. English Heritage unveiled a blue plaque at the site on 2 July 2025."*

## Part Five — residual risk register

1. **Cleveland Street — dismissal date of the telegraph boys (6 December 1889).** Not independently confirmed to publication standard in this pass. The surrounding narrative (telegraph-boy brothel; Lord Arthur Somerset's flight to France; the witness-emigration scheme; Arthur Newton's guilty plea and six-week sentence in May 1890) is solid. **Action:** verify the specific date against a primary/authoritative secondary source (H. Montgomery Hyde, *The Cleveland Street Scandal*, 1976) before print, or soften to "in December 1889."

2. **Gretna Green statistics.** These are industry-sourced and volatile. "One in eight" is defensible (BBC ~12%; council claims 17% at the registration office), but "over 4,000 a year" should be dropped in favour of the attributed "about 5,000 in the area" (Gretna Green Ltd) with a date. A hostile reviewer could otherwise challenge a bare current figure. Present the trend (down since 2004) rather than a single headline number.

3. **Holywell Street — the class-and-price argument.** This is historians' interpretation, not documented in Campbell's words; the manuscript must present it as such. If concrete prices for cheap street pornography versus the collector's market are to be quoted, they require a specialist source (Lynda Nead, *Victorian Babylon*; Iain McCalman, *Radical Underworld*), not established in this pass. **Flag as editorial decision.**

4. **"Longest-running lesbian club in the world" (Gateways).** Must be attributed; the global superlative cannot be stated as bare fact. English Heritage's own wording ("London's … longest-lived") is the safe factual claim.

5. **Vatican letters — transmission route.** The 2023 *TLS* theory is a hypothesis, explicitly described as speculative even by one of its authors. Do not present as established.

6. **Abbots Bromley antler date.** A single 1976 radiocarbon test on one antler. Retain "AD 1065 ± 80 years" and the caveat that the *import* date is unknown. Do not name a laboratory or a specific publication for the result unless a primary radiocarbon report is located — the standard reference works consulted give only "a test in 1976."

7. **Keats — Fanny Brawne's letters buried unopened.** Rests on Joseph Severn's testimony (via Sharp, 1892); attribute to Severn and note the "a letter" versus "letters" ambiguity, plus the accompanying lock of hair and purse.

8. **Shaftesbury Fountain — £7,000 cost.** Rests largely on Gilbert's own later testimony; the Memorial Committee's records are lost. Phrase as "said to have cost him."

9. **Minor date/detail cross-checks for the copy-editor:** *Times* "Who Breaks a Butterfly on a Wheel?" leader (confirm 1 July 1967); Brontë–Heger — three (not four) letters torn up; Vauxhall — one-shilling admission introduced after the opening (first-night tickets a guinea); Ladies of Llangollen — the 1790 item ran in several papers, and Anne Lister met Sarah Ponsonby, not Eleanor Butler.

**Bottom line for the editor:** apply the Ffynnon Fair, Gretna Green, Gateways-plaque, Emma-Hamilton, and Holywell-Street corrections; attribute the Vatican-letters and Gateways-superlative claims; resolve the single open date (Cleveland Street, 6 December 1889); and Topics 66–70 are clear to print.
