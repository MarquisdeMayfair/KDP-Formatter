FINAL EVIDENCE AUDIT — PASS 1: CONNECTIVE MATTER (TOPICS 66–70)
Executive findings

Topics 66–70 are close to publication-ready but not yet clear to print. Of roughly ninety independently checkable claims in the connective matter, this pass found one claim that is flatly wrong, two that are wrong in detail, one internal inconsistency with Topic 26, and about a dozen that overstate or compress a more careful position established in Topics 1–65.

The single serious error is in Topic 66 and Topic 69 (Chapter Ten), where both openers state that Harris's List of Covent Garden Ladies was never prosecuted. It was. John Roach and John Aitkin were convicted of obscene libel in 1794 and James Roach was fined £100 and imprisoned in Newgate for a year after publishing one further edition; the prosecution was brought by the Proclamation Society and ended the publication. This matters more than an ordinary slip because the claim is load-bearing: it is the evidence offered for the book's class argument in two separate places. Fortunately the correction strengthens the argument rather than weakening it, because Roach's own courtroom defence was that nobody had ever been prosecuted for publishing the List before — the toleration was real, it simply ended in 1794 rather than never happening.

The second material error is the Pall Mall freehold in Topic 67, Chapter One. The date is right but the mechanism and the superlative are not: the grant of 1 December 1676 was to Charles II's servant William Chiffinch and to Martin Folkes as trustees, conveyed to Nell Gwyn's own trustees on 6 April 1677 and settled on her for life. The defensible superlative is the narrow one recorded by the Survey of London — that as of 1960 No. 79 was the only property on the south side of Pall Mall not held of the Crown — not the far wider claim that it was "one of the few pieces of Crown land ever alienated to a private subject," which I cannot support and which is probably false.

The third is the John Brown correspondence, also Chapter One. Edward VII's destruction of Brown material is well attested, but the strongest documented instance is far more specific and far better than the vague version in the text: in 1905 Sir James Reid dealt with a blackmailer holding over three hundred letters from Victoria to her Balmoral factor Alexander Profeit concerning Brown, which Reid's diary called "most compromising" and which Edward VII then destroyed.

The internal inconsistency is Ffynnon Fair. Topic 66 says the "Welsh Gretna Green" label rests on "a single clandestine marriage recorded in 1626." Topic 26 established from Y Cwtta Cyfarwydd that Peter Roberts recorded seven marriages at the chapel between 1611 and 1640, two of them described as clandestine, of which Elizabeth Lloyd's in 1626 is one. The Introduction is repeating the figure from the original commissioning brief rather than from the completed research. The debunk survives intact either way — seven weddings in thirty years is not a wedding factory — but the number must match Chapter Four.

Everything else that this pass tested held up, and several claims held up better than the prose currently admits. Cerne Abbas, Castlerigg, Anne Lister's word count, the Gretna proportions and the Beatrice journals all verified against heritage bodies or peer-reviewed work. The Castlerigg case needs one word changed to remove an implication of intent that English Heritage does not make.

Two categories of claim I could not test in this pass and which remain open: several items carried forward from earlier batch research and not independently re-verified here (Henry VIII's letters in the Vatican, the Georgiana print count, the Abbots Bromley radiocarbon date, the Anteros naming history), and one pricing contrast in the Chapter Ten opener that I believe cannot be substantiated as written. These are listed under Unresolved questions rather than silently passed.

Replacement passages
Topic 66 — Introduction — replacement paragraph (the class argument)

Third, and inseparable from class, is commerce, but before commerce comes the second force itself, and it runs through this book like a seam of coal. For the better part of forty years the Georgian state let an annual directory of Covent Garden prostitutes — names, addresses, prices, specialities — sell some eight thousand copies a year at half a crown, and did nothing at all. When the Proclamation Society finally prosecuted the publishers in 1794, James Roach's own defence in court was that nobody had ever been prosecuted for publishing it, so he had not understood that it was an offence. He was fined £100 and sent to Newgate for a year, and the List died with him. The Victorian state, confronted with the same sort of material at a fraction of the price in the windows of Holywell Street, passed the Obscene Publications Act of 1857 and put booksellers in prison. What had changed was not the filth. It was the price, and therefore the customer. A century later, prosecuting Penguin Books over Lady Chatterley's Lover, counsel for the Crown asked a jury whether this was a book they would wish his wife or his servants to read — and lost the case in a single sentence, because he had said the quiet part aloud. When a male brothel was uncovered in Cleveland Street in 1889, the working-class telegraph boys were named, dismissed from the Post Office and pressed to emigrate; the aristocratic clients were allowed to reach France. British law has been remarkably relaxed about the pleasures of people it trusted, and remarkably vigilant about everyone else.

Topic 66 — Introduction — replacement passage (the method paragraph, first four sentences)

A word about method, because it bears on the pleasure of the thing. This book flags what it cannot prove, and does so in the prose rather than in a footnote, and the demolitions turn out to be among the best material in it. The druidical grove at Castlerigg was planted inside the circle at the height of the Romantic enthusiasm for the Lakes, and thirteen of the stones themselves were shifted or re-erected between the 1790s and the 1840s; Coleridge noticed in 1799 and wrote it down. The Long Man of Wilmington, that supposedly immemorial pagan giant, appears to be Tudor or Stuart. The "Welsh Gretna Green" at Ffynnon Fair rests on seven marriages recorded between 1611 and 1640, two of them described as clandestine.

Topic 67 — Chapter 1 — replacement paragraph 4 (the evasions)

It also produced the evasions, which is why this chapter opens the book. Faced with rules of this weight, royal lovers did precisely what the fishwife and the curate did, only with better lawyers and more expensive houses. They married in secret. They kept establishments at a discreet distance from the palace, and when Nell Gwyn objected to holding her Pall Mall house on a mere lease, Charles II granted the freehold on 1 December 1676 to two trustees, who conveyed it to her own trustees the following April and settled it on her for life. As late as 1960 it was still the only property on the south side of Pall Mall not held of the Crown: a piece of conveyancing that is really a love letter. They built a house on the Isle of Wight where nobody could see in. They retreated to a lodge in Windsor Great Park to make the least private decision in modern British history. And when the affair was over, the family burned the letters. Beatrice copied her mother's journals into a hundred and eleven notebooks and burned most of the originals as she went, finishing in 1931 over the objections of George V and Queen Mary. Edward VII, having bought back from a blackmailer more than three hundred letters Victoria had written to her Balmoral factor about John Brown — letters the royal physician Sir James Reid privately called most compromising — destroyed those too. A great deal of what this chapter would like to tell you went up a chimney in the interests of the institution.

Topic 67 — Chapter 4 — replacement paragraph 3 (Castlerigg and the invented rites)

Britain's ancient fertility rites are, to a degree that becomes comic once you start counting, a Victorian invention laid over genuinely old things. Castlerigg is a real Neolithic circle; the grove of larch at its centre was planted by Romantic-era improvers, thirteen stones were re-erected or shifted between about 1794 and 1848 with the effect of creating a midwinter alignment, and Coleridge, visiting in November 1799, noted that the locals had been playing tricks with the stones. The Long Man of Wilmington, that immemorial pagan colossus, first appears in a drawing of 1710 and was dated by excavation to the sixteenth or seventeenth century. The Padstow 'Obby 'Oss is first recorded in 1803, and the pagan reading was fixed on it by Frazerian folklorists around 1913 and then absorbed by the town itself, so that visiting scholars are now told their own theory back. The Green Man was named and defined in an article published in 1939.

Topic 67 — Chapter 4 — replacement paragraph 1 (opening)

The chalk giant above Cerne Abbas, in Dorset, is fifty-five metres tall, carries a club, and has an erection of some thirty-five feet. He is also, on the best current evidence, medieval — dated by optically stimulated luminescence to a range of AD 700 to 1100, with the deepest sample from his elbow pointing to a date around 905, which is neither the Iron Age fertility god of the guidebooks nor the seventeenth-century joke about Cromwell that replaced him. Most tellingly, no surviving document securely mentions him before 1694, when the churchwardens recorded three shillings for repairing him. A colossal naked man on a hillside is not easy to overlook. The silence of everyone who should have seen him is the loudest evidence we have.

Topic 69 — Chapter 10 — replacement paragraph 4 (the trade was licensed, not suppressed)

London's sex trade was never suppressed by the authorities. It was licensed, taxed and regulated by them, usually by the same institutions publicly condemning it. The brothels of medieval Bankside operated under ordinances issued in the name of the Bishop of Winchester, who drew the rents; the women were nicknamed Winchester Geese after their landlord, and the bishops profited from the arrangement for centuries, until Henry VIII suppressed the licensed houses in 1546. An annual printed directory of Covent Garden prostitutes, giving names, addresses, prices and specialities, sold thousands of copies a year for the better part of four decades in the eighteenth century, advertised on the front pages of newspapers and sold openly at booksellers' stalls, and it was not prosecuted until 1794 — by which point its publisher could tell the court, quite accurately, that nobody had ever thought it worth prosecuting before.

Topic 69 — Chapter 10 — replacement paragraph 5 (the class argument)

What changed in the nineteenth century was not morality but price. When the same material became cheap enough for clerks and shop-boys, sold from shop windows rather than bound for a gentleman's library, the state discovered an urgent interest, and Lord Campbell's Obscene Publications Act of 1857 followed. The gentleman collector Henry Spencer Ashbee assembled the largest pornography library in England and left it to the British Museum. The Holywell Street bookseller William Dugdale died under a sentence of hard labour in the Clerkenwell House of Correction.
