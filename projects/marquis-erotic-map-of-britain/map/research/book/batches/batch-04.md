## 16 — The Gay Delavals: Northumberland's most exhausting family
*Seaton Delaval Hall · Seaton Sluice, Northumberland, England · 1718–1822*

### BOOK VERSION
Fifteen hundred pounds. That is what Sir Francis Blake Delaval spent, in a single evening, to hire the Theatre Royal, Drury Lane, so that he and his brothers and sisters could act out Shakespeare's *Othello* to a fashionable London audience — with themselves in every leading part. In the money of the day it was a small fortune; today the sum runs into six figures. The House of Commons adjourned early to let members attend. The footmen's gallery, Horace Walpole recorded, was "strung with blue ribands." The Prince of Wales came. Sir Francis played the Moor; his brother John played Iago; a sister, Mrs Quon, played Desdemona. Walpole, who watched the whole thing with the delight of a man collecting other people's absurdities, wrote that they acted so well "that it is astonishing they should not have had sense enough not to act at all."

That was the Delavals. They did nothing by halves, and they did almost nothing sensibly.

Their house still stands on the Northumberland coast a mile inland from Seaton Sluice, and it is one of the most theatrical buildings in England. Sir John Vanbrugh — playwright turned architect, the man behind Blenheim and Castle Howard — designed it between 1718 and 1728 for Admiral George Delaval, a naval officer who had made his money capturing enemy ships. Neither man lived to see it finished. The Admiral fell from his horse in 1723; Vanbrugh died in 1726. The estate passed to a nephew, and it was the nephew's children who turned the place into a byword for extravagance and earned the family its nickname: the Gay Delavals, gay in the eighteenth-century sense of merry, spendthrift and scandalous.

The stories that cling to the house are mostly about beds. Guests, it is said, were shown to bedrooms whose walls could be hauled up on pulleys in the small hours, exposing a sleeper to the room next door; or whose four-poster could be winched aloft and lowered, occupant and all, into a tank of cold water; or whose entire contents had been turned upside down and fixed to the ceiling. Geese were hidden in the bedclothes. These tales are wonderful and they are told everywhere, so it is worth being clear about the evidence. The National Trust, which owns the Hall, is candid: the family's reputation for pranks is genuine and contemporary, but the mechanical beds and sliding walls belong largely to Victorian retelling, much of it traceable to the *Monthly Chronicle of North Country Lore and Legend* in the 1880s. The Trust's own displays include an upside-down room, described as "inspired by one of the most well known anecdotes" — which is a heritage body's honest way of saying: lovely story, don't take it to the bank. What is solidly documented is the appetite for spectacle. They took acting lessons from the professionals Charles Macklin and Samuel Foote. They staged plays and pantomimes at home. They hired a London theatre. The practical jokes are the froth on top; the theatre is the real thing.

And under the froth is a good deal of ordinary human wreckage. Sir Francis (1727–1771) is described in the *Oxford Dictionary of National Biography*, flatly, as a rake. He kept a long-term mistress, an illegitimate cousin named Betty Roach, who bore him two children; the National Trust reckons he left at least five illegitimate children in all. To clear his debts the family arranged, in March 1750, his marriage to a wealthy widow, Lady Isabella, daughter of the Earl of Thanet — a woman decades his senior whose fortune turned out to be smaller than advertised, and whose money he promptly spent on Betty Roach anyway. The marriage was a transaction to keep the estate afloat, and everyone at the time understood it as one. Sir Francis died in 1771, aged forty-four, an alcoholic and, by most accounts, largely alone.

The building, too, came to grief. In 1822 fire tore through the central block; the heat was so fierce that glass ran like water. The core of the house has stood as a roofless shell ever since, statues of the Muses still ranged along bare interior walls open to the Northumberland sky. The National Trust acquired the Hall in 2009 and opened it the following year, choosing — rightly — not to pretend the fire never happened. The shell stays a shell.

Set the Delavals beside the other pleasure-seekers of this chapter and something clarifies. The Hellfire men dressed as monks and told each other they were blaspheming; Byron kept a bear at Cambridge and a skull for a wine cup at Newstead. All of it was performance, and the performance was almost always more elaborate than the sin underneath. The Delavals are that principle in its purest, silliest, most domestic form. Their transgression was, in the end, a cold bath and a goose in the bed. They spent themselves into ruin staging plays about themselves, and they built a house so theatrical it eventually caught fire and became the best set of all. Nobody was corrupted. Everybody was exhausted. It is the most English scandal imaginable: enormous effort, enormous expense, and the joke, when you get to the end of it, is a wet aristocrat climbing out of a tank in his nightshirt.

### WEB VERSION — seaton-delaval-hall
Fifteen hundred pounds — a small fortune — was what Sir Francis Blake Delaval spent hiring Drury Lane for one night in 1751, so he and his siblings could star in their own *Othello*. The Commons adjourned early to attend; the Prince of Wales came; Horace Walpole noted the footmen's gallery "strung with blue ribands." That was the Gay Delavals — gay meaning merry, extravagant and scandalous — the family who made Seaton Delaval Hall the loudest house in Georgian Northumberland.

Vanbrugh designed the Baroque masterpiece between 1718 and 1728 for Admiral George Delaval. Neither architect nor admiral lived to see it finished. It was the next generation who spent liberally, gambled, staged plays and pantomimes at home, and acquired a reputation for tormenting house guests. The famous pranks — beds winched up on pulleys and dropped into cold-water tanks, walls that slid back to expose sleepers, rooms turned upside down, geese smuggled into the bedclothes — should be handled with care. The National Trust confirms the pranking reputation is genuine but notes the elaborate mechanical versions are largely Victorian embroidery, much of it from the 1880s. The solid fact is the theatrical mania: lessons from professional actors, home performances, and that ruinous night at Drury Lane.

Sir Francis (1727–1771) — described in the *Oxford Dictionary of National Biography* as a rake — kept a mistress, Betty Roach, and left at least five illegitimate children. In 1750 his family married him off to a wealthy elderly widow to clear his debts; he spent her money on his mistress. He died at forty-four, alcoholic and largely alone.

Fire gutted the central block in 1822, so fiercely that glass melted. It has stood as a roofless shell ever since. The Trust took it on in 2009 and wisely left the shell as a shell.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**Starlight Castle, built for a bet.** Sir Francis's brother John raised a small folly, Starlight Castle, overlooking Holywell Dene — reputedly thrown up in a single day to win a wager. Little survives but the name and the story, which is fitting: the Delavals specialised in enormous effort expended on things that did not, strictly, need doing.

**A third foot in the family portrait.** During conservation, curators found that a painting of the Delaval sisters had been altered — one girl appears to have three feet, and the canvas bears what looks like damage from a champagne cork. Even the family's art records the wear and tear of relentless partying.

**The bats moved in.** After the 1822 fire the gutted central hall was left open to the elements, and it became the largest maternity roost of common pipistrelle bats in the region. The grandest room the Delavals ever built now belongs entirely to the wildlife.

## 17 — Bloomsbury: they stopped performing
*46 Gordon Square & Charleston Farmhouse · London WC1 and Firle, East Sussex · 1904–1978*

### BOOK VERSION
A spring evening, a drawing room in Bloomsbury, two young women sitting after dinner. The door opens and Lytton Strachey — long, cadaverous, unmistakable — stands on the threshold. He points at a stain on Vanessa Bell's dress and says a single word: "Semen?"

Virginia Woolf set the scene down decades later in a paper called "Old Bloomsbury," read to friends and never meant for the public. "With that one word," she wrote, "all barriers of reticence and reserve went down." It has become the founding anecdote of the whole group, and like most founding anecdotes it has been polished by retelling. It comes to us only through Woolf, only in memoir, only in old age; the exact year is uncertain. But the point she was making is the real one. She was describing the moment when a group of clever, well-bred Victorians decided that sex was a thing you could simply talk about at the dinner table, in mixed company, without dying of it.

The room was 46 Gordon Square. The four Stephen siblings — Vanessa, Virginia, their brothers Thoby and Adrian — had moved there in 1904 after the death of their father, Sir Leslie Stephen, escaping the heavy respectability of Kensington for the unfashionable squares near the British Museum. Thoby's Cambridge friends began coming on Thursday evenings: Strachey, Clive Bell, Leonard Woolf, the economist Maynard Keynes, the painter Duncan Grant. They talked about art and the nature of the good and, eventually, about who was sleeping with whom, which in this company was a large subject.

Because the striking thing about Bloomsbury is not that they had affairs. Everyone in this book has affairs. It is that they arranged their actual households around desire, in the open, and kept doing so for decades. The clearest case is a farmhouse under the South Downs.

In 1916 Vanessa Bell — married to Clive Bell, though the marriage had quietly become a friendship — took the lease of Charleston, near Firle in East Sussex, from the Gage estate. She was in love with Duncan Grant. Grant was a conscientious objector, and so was his lover, the writer David Garnett, always called Bunny; under the terms of Grant's exemption the two men needed farm work, and Charleston was near a farm. So the household that moved in consisted of Vanessa, her two young sons by Clive, the man Vanessa loved, and the man that man loved. Clive came at weekends. Everyone painted.

Set out plainly it sounds like arithmetic, and Bloomsbury has a famous piece of arithmetic attached to it: they "lived in squares and loved in triangles." The line is nearly always credited to the American wit Dorothy Parker, but the attribution is shaky — scholars tracing it have found no solid source in Parker and an earlier candidate in the novelist Margaret Irwin, writing in 1928. Best to enjoy it as an anonymous summary rather than anyone's coinage.

On Christmas Day 1918 Vanessa gave birth at Charleston to a daughter, Angelica, whose father was Duncan Grant. Maynard Keynes was staying in the house at the time; so was Bunny Garnett. Within weeks Garnett wrote to Lytton Strachey about the newborn a sentence that has never stopped being quoted with a shudder: he thought he might marry her when she grew up. He was twenty-six. She was days old. In 1942 he did exactly that. He was fifty; she was twenty-three; her parents were appalled and did not attend the wedding — and neither Vanessa nor Duncan told Angelica the other half of it, which was that her new husband had once been her father's lover.

That is the part of the story that stops the laughter. Angelica was raised believing Clive Bell was her father, and was told the truth only in 1937, when she was eighteen. She wrote about all of it in a memoir, *Deceived with Kindness* (1984), and the title is the whole argument. The adults around her were tolerant, artistic, unshockable, kind — and they organised her childhood around a set of secrets that were an open joke to everyone except the child at the centre of them. Bloomsbury's freedom was real. The bill for it was itemised, and a good deal of it was sent to Angelica.

The men were freer still, and better documented, because Keynes wrote everything down. He kept, alongside his ledgers of expenditure and his golf scores, a private tally of his sexual partners — some by initials (GLS for Strachey, DG for Grant), some by nickname, some, in the anonymous years around 1911, by pure category: "Lift boy of Vauxhall." He counted, quarter by quarter, as methodically as he counted anything. This was a man who would later shape the economies of nations, keeping a spreadsheet of his lovers before spreadsheets existed, at a time when what he was recording could have sent him to prison. He married the Russian ballerina Lydia Lopokova in 1925, to the disappointment of Grant and the astonishment of nearly everyone, and by all accounts loved her.

Charleston is now a museum, its walls and doors and furniture painted all over in the Omega Workshops manner, the red dog Duncan painted still guarding the head of Vanessa's bed. Angelica Garnett helped found the trust that saved it. Bloomsbury's other threads run on from here — Vanessa's friend Virginia would fall, later, for Vita Sackville-West, which is a story for the queer chapter and another set of great houses. But Charleston is where the argument of this chapter turns. Every rake and monk and clubman before them performed transgression and went home to a conventional bed. The Bloomsbury household stopped performing. They simply rebuilt the bed to the shape they wanted, and lived in it in the daylight, and let a child grow up inside the consequences.

One spring evening at 46 Gordon Square, Lytton Strachey pointed to a stain on Vanessa Bell's dress and asked, "Semen?" — and, as Virginia Woolf recalled decades later in her memoir "Old Bloomsbury," "all barriers of reticence and reserve went down." The anecdote survives only through Woolf, written down long afterwards, but it captures the exact hinge on which a group of clever Victorians turned into moderns.

**charleston-farmhouse-bloomsbury**
In 1916 Vanessa Bell took Charleston, a farmhouse below the Downs, so that Duncan Grant — the man she loved — and his lover David "Bunny" Garnett could work as conscientious objectors on a nearby farm. Vanessa's husband Clive visited at weekends. The household was frank, complicated and permanent. On Christmas Day 1918 Vanessa bore Duncan's daughter, Angelica; Keynes and Garnett were both staying in the house. Days later Garnett wrote that he might marry the baby when she grew up — and in 1942, aged fifty, he married the twenty-three-year-old Angelica, who had been told only at eighteen that Duncan, not Clive, was her father, and was never told that her husband had been her father's lover. Her memoir *Deceived with Kindness* (1984) counts the cost. The house itself is a total work of art — every surface painted by Bell and Grant in the Omega manner — and is now open to the public, saved by a trust Angelica helped found.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**Keynes's private ledger.** The economist John Maynard Keynes kept a coded tally of his sexual life the way he kept his golf scores — partners listed by initials, nicknames, or bald category ("Lift boy of Vauxhall," 1911). He recorded it methodically in years when such acts were criminal, then married ballerina Lydia Lopokova in 1925 in a genuine love match that stunned his friends.

**The open marriage that stayed friendly.** Vanessa and Clive Bell never divorced and never quite separated. After their marriage cooled she loved Roger Fry, then Duncan Grant; Clive kept his own mistresses; all remained close. When Fry died, Vanessa decorated his coffin. The Bloomsbury talent was less for love than for staying friends afterwards.

**A dog painted for protection.** Above Vanessa Bell's bed at Charleston, Duncan Grant painted a large red dog, meant to watch over her. It is still there. Amid all the tangled affairs, it is the plainest surviving love token in the house.

## 18 — The salon and the saloon bar
*Garsington Manor & the Fitzroy Tavern · Oxfordshire and Fitzrovia, London · 1915–1950s*

### BOOK VERSION
An ordinary corner house on Charlotte Street gave a whole London district its name, and what went on inside it between the wars is now almost impossible to picture from the pavement. To understand the Fitzroy Tavern at all you have to start forty miles away, in an Oxfordshire manor painted the colours of a peacock.

Garsington Manor was bought in 1915 by Lady Ottoline Morrell and her husband Philip, a Liberal MP. Ottoline was six feet tall, dressed like a stained-glass window, and had a genius for gathering interesting people and almost no talent for keeping their loyalty. She had the Tudor house done out in her own extraordinary taste — peacock-blue panelling, red lacquer, Spanish leather on the walls — laid out an Italianate pond and a loggia, and during the First World War threw the place open as a refuge for conscientious objectors, many of them from Bloomsbury. They ate her food, worked notionally on her land, and repaid her by turning her into a figure of fun the moment they were out of earshot.

Her private life was as unhidden as anything in this chapter. From 1911 to 1916 she conducted a passionate, non-exclusive affair with the philosopher Bertrand Russell; it began one night in 1911 when Philip was away. The two were prodigious correspondents even by the standards of the age — the Harry Ransom Center in Texas holds over 1,900 letters from Russell to Ottoline, and the Bertrand Russell Archives at McMaster University in Canada hold roughly 1,500 the other way. Later, in 1920, a young village stonemason named Lionel Gomme came to Garsington to cut plinths for her garden statues; she called him "Tiger," and by 1921, in her late forties, she had — her biographers agree — found real physical happiness for the first time, with a working man half her class and age. Some critics have suggested Gomme fed into the gamekeeper Mellors in D. H. Lawrence's *Lady Chatterley's Lover*, though that is literary speculation rather than fact.

Philip was no better behaved, and considerably clumsier about it. In 1917 the Morrells' marriage hit a genuine crisis when it emerged that he had got two women pregnant at once — his wife's maid and his own secretary. Ottoline, who tolerated a great deal, was for once badly shaken.

The cruelty came from the guests. Lawrence, who had sheltered under her roof, put her into *Women in Love* (1920) as the awful Hermione Roddice; Aldous Huxley, another beneficiary, satirised her in *Crome Yellow* (1921). Both portraits were recognisable and both wounded her deeply, which tells you something about the etiquette of the clever set: you accepted the woman's hospitality and then sold her to your publisher. Garsington is a private house now, and the opera company that borrowed its name has long since moved elsewhere.

The Fitzroy was the other end of the same world — bohemia with the country-house filter removed. The building had been the Fitzroy Coffee House in 1883 and a pub called the Hundred Marks by 1887; a former Savile Row tailor named Judah Kleinfeld took over the licence and, in March 1919, renamed it the Fitzroy Tavern. Everyone called him Pop. He devised the "pennies from heaven" trick — customers threw coins wrapped on darts up into the ceiling for a children's charity — and he presided over the most mixed room in London. Augustus John called it the Clapham Junction of the world, meaning everyone passed through sooner or later. Dylan Thomas scribbled poems on beer mats and gave them to women at the bar; Aleister Crowley mixed cocktails; the painter Nina Hamnett held court; Betty May, the "Tiger Woman," lapped drink from a saucer on all fours.

It was also, crucially, one of the places where gay and bisexual Londoners could drink in relative peace decades before the law allowed it. Sex between men remained a criminal offence until 1967, and the period's raids were real and ruinous — a queer gathering in nearby Fitzroy Square was raided in 1927, the Caravan Club off Covent Garden in 1934, its members photographed and prosecuted. The Fitzroy escaped that fate for years, largely, one regular reckoned, because it was too well known and too public to raid quietly. Tolerance here was not a right; it was a fragile local arrangement that could have collapsed at any time, and everyone drinking there knew it.

Nina Hamnett is the figure who binds the two halves together, because she moved through both and paid for it. A gifted painter from Tenby, the self-styled Queen of Bohemia, she published a memoir, *Laughing Torso*, in 1932. In it she wrote that Crowley had practised black magic at his "abbey" in Sicily. Crowley — who had discovered that suing people was a living — took her and her publisher to court for libel in 1934. He lost, spectacularly. The judge, Mr Justice Swift, told the jury he had never in over forty years on the bench heard "such dreadful, horrible, blasphemous and abominable stuff" as Crowley's evidence. Hamnett won and was ruined anyway; drink took the rest of her, and she died in 1956 after a fall from her window.

The salon and the saloon bar look like opposites — the peacock panelling and the spit-and-sawdust. They were the same impulse, at different altitudes: rooms where the ordinary rules were suspended, at a price, and where the people who made them possible were usually the ones who suffered for it.

Lady Ottoline Morrell bought Garsington Manor in 1915 and painted it the colours of her own imagination — peacock-blue panelling, red lacquer, Spanish leather — with an Italianate pond and loggia outside. During the First World War she made it a refuge for conscientious objectors, many from Bloomsbury, who accepted her hospitality and then mocked her behind her back. In 1920 she took up with "Tiger" — Lionel Gomme, a village stonemason cutting her garden plinths — and found, at last, real happiness. Her husband Philip caused a family crisis in 1917 by fathering children on the maid and the secretary at once. D. H. Lawrence repaid her in *Women in Love* (1920) and Aldous Huxley in *Crome Yellow* (1921); both portraits hurt her. Garsington is now a private house.

**fitzroy-tavern**
The Fitzroy Tavern at 16 Charlotte Street gave Fitzrovia its name. A former coffee house, it became the Fitzroy under landlord Judah "Pop" Kleinfeld, who renamed it in March 1919 and invented the "pennies from heaven" darts charity. From the 1920s to the 1950s it was London's bohemian crossroads — Augustus John called it "the Clapham Junction of the world." Dylan Thomas handed out beer-mat poems; Aleister Crowley mixed drinks; Nina Hamnett reigned as Queen of Bohemia. It was also a rare place where gay and bisexual people could drink relatively openly at a time when sex between men was illegal — a tolerance that was fragile, not guaranteed, with raids on other venues (Fitzroy Square 1927, the Caravan Club 1934) a constant reminder. Hamnett's 1932 memoir *Laughing Torso* led Crowley to sue her for libel in 1934; he lost, and the judge denounced his evidence from the bench.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**Pennies from heaven.** Pop Kleinfeld turned charity into a bar game: drinkers wrapped coins around darts and threw them at the Fitzroy's ceiling, where they stuck. Once a year the money was taken down and spent sending poor local children to the seaside. Nina Hamnett, generous and usually broke, was said to miss the ceiling but never the point.

**Sassoon told to walk out of the war.** Among the conscientious objectors Ottoline sheltered at Garsington was the war poet Siegfried Sassoon, recuperating from wounds. There he was encouraged to make his public protest against the conduct of the war — one of the more consequential things ever hatched in a country-house drawing room.

**The Tiger Woman and the cat.** Betty May, a Fitzroy regular nicknamed the Tiger Woman for drinking from a saucer on all fours, became the star witness in Crowley's 1934 libel case. She testified that at Crowley's Sicilian abbey her late husband had been made to kill a cat and drink its blood — evidence that helped sink Crowley's suit.

## 19 — Coteries at the edge: Mount Stewart and Portmeirion
*Mount Stewart & Portmeirion · County Down and Gwynedd · 1915–1941*

### BOOK VERSION
The oddest thing in the gardens of Mount Stewart is a terrace populated by concrete animals — dodos, a griffin, a boar, apes holding pots, a whole ark of creatures moulded by a local craftsman, Thomas Beattie, around the late 1920s. They are not decoration. They are a guest list. Each beast stands for a member of the Ark, the private club that Edith, Marchioness of Londonderry, ran through and after the First World War, and whose members took animal nicknames, usually a pun on their own. Winston Churchill was Winston the Warlock. Edith's husband Charles, whose infidelities were an open secret, was Charley the Cheetah — tall, sleek and prowling. Ramsay MacDonald, the first Labour Prime Minister, was Hamish the Hart. And Edith herself, the woman who summoned them all and held them in place, was Circe the Sorceress, the enchantress from Homer who turned men into swine.

Edith was one of the great political hostesses of the age, and her club — founded in 1915 and meeting at Londonderry House on Park Lane — was where the powerful were charmed into her orbit. The most discussed of her enchantments was MacDonald. A Tory marchioness and a Labour prime minister formed, across the 1920s and especially after his break with his own party in 1931, an intense friendship that scandalised both their camps and generated a large and affectionate correspondence. Here it is worth being honest about the evidence, because the temptation to tidy it into a love affair is strong. What the record supports is deep emotional intimacy and real political influence. The sexual question is unresolved; it is generally held to have been platonic, and gossip is not proof. Edith mattered to MacDonald enormously, and he to her, and beyond that the sources go quiet.

She was also, it turns out, tattooed. Beneath the marchioness there was a serpent: a snake coiling up her left leg from the ankle, hidden for decades and, according to the tattoo historian Matthew Lodder, exposed to general view around 1938 when hemlines rose, causing something of a sensation and prompting some to conclude she was "a fast woman." The National Trust, which now cares for Mount Stewart, records the tattoo too. It was not a family eccentricity but a fashion — the late-Victorian and Edwardian aristocracy went in for tattoos, Lady Randolph Churchill among them with a serpent round her wrist. Where and by whom Edith's snake was done is not documented; the suggestion that she was tattooed in Japan around 1900 rests on a single scholarly source and should be taken lightly. The gardens she made from 1921, of which the Dodo Terrace is one corner, she gave to the nation in 1957.

Four hundred miles south-west, on a wooded Welsh estuary, another powerful and unconventional person was building a private world with its own rules. Portmeirion is the fantasy Italianate village that the architect Clough Williams-Ellis began in 1925 on the coast of Gwynedd, and it is where, in the spring of 1941, Noël Coward wrote the play that would become his greatest hit.

He had good reason to be out of London. The Blitz had destroyed his office and his flat. He arrived at Portmeirion in early May and stayed, by the best account — his own — in the Fountain suite, though the village's folklore also attaches the story to the Watch House, and it is one of those pleasant local disputes that will never be settled. What is certain is the speed. Coward sat down on the morning of the second day and wrote for six days flat, morning and afternoon, propping up a wobbling table leg with a wedge, and by the evening of 9 May 1941 he had finished *Blithe Spirit* — the ghost comedy about a novelist haunted by his dead first wife. He took the title from Shelley. He made almost no corrections; only two lines of the original script were ever cut. It opened at the Piccadilly Theatre that July and ran for 1,997 performances, a record for a non-musical play in the West End that stood until Agatha Christie's *The Mousetrap* overtook it in 1957.

The thing the play could not say out loud is the thing that shaped Coward's whole career. He was homosexual, at a time when that was a criminal matter throughout his working life, and he never publicly acknowledged it. His discretion was total and it was armour: the wit, the dressing gown, the clipped delivery, the drawing-room comedies about heterosexual couples behaving badly, all of it built a public Noël Coward so polished that no interviewer could find the private one. There is a real joke buried in *Blithe Spirit*, and it is not on any of the characters. It is on a legal system so blunt that it drove one of the most celebrated Englishmen of the century to hide in plain sight, and never noticed him doing it. Portmeirion went on to a second fame in the 1960s as the set of *The Prisoner*, and now draws well over two hundred thousand visitors a year, most of whom have no idea a masterpiece was hammered out here in under a week.

Two places, then, at the geographical edges of these islands, made by two people who could not or would not live by the centre's rules — and who each built, out of concrete animals or Italianate towers, a world in which they got to set the terms. Circe kept her sorcery to the end. Coward kept his secret. The buildings kept both.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**A boiler-suited marchioness.** Edith Londonderry directed the making of her own gardens in a boiler suit, setting demobilised ex-servicemen to work after 1919 and driving the project herself. Guests who signed the Mount Stewart visitors' book were liable to be handed a wheelbarrow. The hostess who charmed prime ministers also chopped her own wood.

**Circe and the Sirens.** The painter Edmond Brock captured Edith in 1925 in a group portrait with her three youngest daughters, titled "Circe and the Sirens" — a direct nod to her Ark nickname. The mythological joke ran right through her world, from the dining club to the garden herms carved with Circe's face and the swine's snouts of Odysseus's crew.

**The village that played a prison.** In the 1960s Portmeirion became "the Village" in the cult television series *The Prisoner*, starring Patrick McGoohan. Clough Williams-Ellis's dreamlike resort, built to prove that development could enhance rather than spoil a landscape, found lasting fame as the set for a story about a man who cannot escape.

## 20 — De Quincey's opium marriage
*Dove Cottage & Nab Cottage · Grasmere and Rydal, Cumbria · 1802–1859*

### BOOK VERSION
Consider first the girl, because he did. In the winter of 1802 a runaway schoolboy of seventeen was starving on the streets of London, sleeping in doorways and empty houses, and the person who kept him alive was a teenage prostitute he calls only Ann. She was about sixteen. One night, when he fainted from hunger on Oxford Street, she spent her own small money on hot spiced wine to revive him — a poor girl feeding a poorer boy. He never forgot it. Years later, in his *Confessions of an English Opium-Eater*, Thomas De Quincey wrote of "this poor friendless girl" with a tenderness he gave almost nothing else, and refused to file her among the "outcasts": "let me not class thee, oh! noble-minded Ann." When his luck turned and he came back to find her, she had vanished, and though he searched Oxford Street for years he never saw her again. It is one of the most humane passages any Englishman wrote in that century, and it is about a sixteen-year-old sex worker who once bought a hungry boy a drink and disappeared.

De Quincey (1785–1859) is remembered for the drug and ought to be remembered for the honesty. Born in Manchester, he ran from his school, wandered Wales, and fetched up destitute in London before his family recovered him and sent him to Oxford. He worshipped the poet William Wordsworth from a distance, so violently that when he first travelled to the Lakes to meet his hero he lost his nerve on the ridge above the house and turned back. He got there in the end, in 1807, and in 1809 — the year after the Wordsworths outgrew their famous little house at Town End and moved on — he took over the tenancy of Dove Cottage in Grasmere and stayed for eleven years.

The opium had started in 1804. He had crippling pains in the face and head — neuralgia, a bad tooth — and a fellow student recommended laudanum, the ordinary tincture of opium in alcohol that any apothecary sold across the counter, no questions asked. It worked; and then it kept working, and then he could not stop. He measured it, as addicts do, and the figures climb through his own account and his biographers': from drops, to glasses, to a reported ten wineglasses of laudanum a day by around 1813, with a peak that later writers put as high as some twelve thousand drops daily during his worst years — a figure to treat with caution, but a horrifying one even discounted. His great book turns on the split he knew from the inside: "The Pleasures of Opium" and, then, "The Pains." He called it, in one of the phrases everyone remembers, "just, subtle, and mighty opium."

The marriage sits right in the middle of the habit. Near Rydal Water stood a small farm called the Nab, and there lived Margaret Simpson, the daughter of an independent farmer — a "statesman," in the local word. De Quincey courted her, walking out to see her late at night, and Dorothy Wordsworth, who missed nothing and forgave less, noted it with a spinster's tartness: "At the up-rousing of the Bats and the Owls he regularly went thither." The consequence, she added, was that "Peggy Simpson presented him with a son ten weeks ago." The son, William, was born in November 1816; De Quincey married Margaret at Grasmere church in February 1817. The Wordsworths disapproved, and the disapproval was about class — Margaret was a farmer's daughter, beneath the poet's exalted circle — and it was cold enough to help end the friendship. That, and the fact that De Quincey had taken to ripping out the cottage garden his hero had planted.

They should be allowed their own verdict on the marriage, not the Wordsworths'. By every honest account it was happy. Margaret bore him eight children and nursed him through the opium nights, and he wrote of her as his comfort in the worst of them — the wife who sat up with the addict while the visions came. She died in 1837, worn out, and he outlived her by more than twenty years.

The *Confessions* came out first in the *London Magazine* in 1821 and made him famous almost overnight. It is, by common consent, the first modern drug memoir — the book that taught Baudelaire and half the writers who came after that you could turn addiction itself into literature. It did not cure him and it did not make him solvent; he spent his later years dodging creditors, at one point taking refuge in the debtors' sanctuary at Holyrood, and he died in Edinburgh in December 1859.

What survives is the building. Dove Cottage at Grasmere, where Wordsworth wrote his best poetry and De Quincey ate his opium, is now cared for by Wordsworth Grasmere and open to visitors; the Nab, Margaret's family farm at Rydal, still stands as Nab Cottage. Readers of this book will have met the Lake District already — the Maid of Buttermere fending off her seducer a valley away, Wicked Jimmy Lowther terrorising the same landscape a generation earlier — and will meet Dove Cottage again in the chapter on the Lakes poets, because it is the same small whitewashed house. Two poets, one drug, one kind farmer's daughter and one nameless girl on Oxford Street: the erotic map does not always lead to a scandal. Sometimes it leads to a man being decent about the two women who saved him, in a century that told him not to mention either.

### WEB VERSION — de-quincey-opium-marriage
Thomas De Quincey (1785–1859) is remembered for the opium and deserves to be remembered for the honesty. As a starving runaway of seventeen in 1802, he was kept alive on the London streets by a teenage prostitute he calls only Ann — about sixteen, who spent her own money on hot wine to revive him when he fainted from hunger. He wrote of her with rare tenderness in his *Confessions of an English Opium-Eater* (1821) and searched Oxford Street for her, in vain, for years. His opium habit began in 1804 as a remedy for facial neuralgia; laudanum was legal and sold over the counter. It became a lifelong addiction, measured in drops and later in wineglasses — up to ten a day by around 1813. In 1809 he took over the tenancy of Dove Cottage in Grasmere, the house the Wordsworths had just left, and lived there until 1820. He courted Margaret Simpson, a farmer's daughter from the Nab near Rydal; their son William was born in November 1816, and they married in February 1817. The Wordsworths disapproved on grounds of class — Dorothy Wordsworth recorded the courtship with icy wit — but the marriage was happy, and Margaret nursed him through his worst opium years. The *Confessions*, first serialised in 1821, was the first modern drug memoir. He died, debt-ridden, in Edinburgh in 1859.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**The fan who lost his nerve.** De Quincey idolised Wordsworth so intensely that on two early trips to the Lakes, in 1805 and 1806, he got within sight of Dove Cottage, stood on the ridge staring at its white walls, and fled without knocking. He finally managed an introduction in 1807 — by first befriending Coleridge and giving him £300.

**The garden that ended a friendship.** When De Quincey took over Dove Cottage he altered the garden the Wordsworths had lovingly planted, ordering shrubbery torn out. The poet's family took it as a violation of trust. Between that and their snobbery about his marriage, the worship curdled into a lasting estrangement.

**A hero who slept on a grave.** When Wordsworth's little daughter Catharine died in 1812, De Quincey — who had adored the child — was so undone by grief that he lay for weeks on her grave in Grasmere churchyard. His deepening opium use in these years was, in part, an attempt to blunt that loss.
