## 51 — The Sunday They Came for the Butterfly
*Redlands, West Wittering, West Sussex & Eel Pie Island, Twickenham, Middlesex · 1956–1971*

### BOOK VERSION

Just after eight o'clock on the evening of 12 February 1967, a face appeared at the window of a thatched Elizabethan house near West Wittering, in West Sussex. Inside, eight or nine people were coming down gently from a long day on LSD, listening to Bob Dylan's "Rainy Day Women #12 & 35" go round and round. One of the party thought the face at the glass must belong to a fan. It belonged, in fact, to one of eighteen police officers — two of them women, brought along in case a female body-search was needed — waiting outside Keith Richards's country house, Redlands, with a warrant. Keith got up and opened the door. What followed was not, whatever the newspapers would shortly claim, an orgy. In the words of one guest present that night, the art dealer Christopher Gibbs, it was a scene of pure domesticity.

That phrase is the whole tragedy in miniature, because domesticity was the one thing nobody wanted to hear.

The bust at Redlands is one of the two great establishment-versus-the-young scandals of the decade — the other being Profumo, four years earlier — and like Profumo it was never really about what it said it was about. The police found very little. Four amphetamine tablets in a green velvet jacket, which Mick Jagger said were his: they had in fact been bought, perfectly legally, over a counter in Italy by his girlfriend, Marianne Faithfull. A lump of hashish, and some heroin belonging to the art dealer Robert Fraser. The officers confiscated a stash of Keith's hotel soaps, apparently unsure what they were, and failed to recognise other substances entirely. The tip-off, it is widely believed, came via the *News of the World*, which Jagger was then suing for libel and which had every commercial reason to see him disgraced.

And then there is the Mars bar.

Let us be very precise, because for the rest of her life other people were imprecise on Marianne Faithfull's behalf. When the police entered, Faithfull — who was twenty — had just had a bath and was wrapped in a large fur rug, and nothing else. That is the entire factual basis of the legend. There was no chocolate. The story that the police interrupted Jagger performing a sexual act involving a Mars bar and Faithfull's body appeared in no charge, in no piece of court evidence, and in no newspaper report at the time. It seems to have spread by word of mouth after the trial — quite possibly seeded by police-canteen gossip — and it attached itself to her like a burr. She was still denying it decades later, and denying it with real weariness. She called it a dirty old man's fantasy, a policeman's idea of what people got up to on acid. Keith Richards, asked about it, said simply that he had no idea how the Mars bar got into the story, and that it showed you what was in people's minds.

Notice what the rumour does. It makes the men outlaws and the woman a punchline. Jagger and Richards became folk heroes, their sentences a *cause célèbre*; Faithfull became "the naked girl in the fur rug", and would say later that a male drug-user in that situation was glamorised, while a woman in the same room became a slut and a bad mother. The record here goes quiet in exactly the way the record usually goes quiet about women: through an invention repeated so often it hardens into fact.

The trial came at Chichester in late June 1967, before Judge Leslie Block. Faithfull recalled one small mercy — that the men were beautifully dressed, Jagger in a green velvet suit, Richards in silk — which helped make them look romantic rather than depraved. Fraser pleaded guilty to the heroin. Jagger got three months for his four tablets; Richards got a year for allowing cannabis to be smoked on his premises. They were sent down. For a first offence involving a handful of pep pills, the sentences were preposterous, and the country knew it.

What saved them was the least likely rescuer imaginable: William Rees-Mogg, the impeccably conservative editor of *The Times*, who on 1 July 1967 ran a leader under a borrowed line from Alexander Pope — "Who Breaks a Butterfly on a Wheel?" His argument, put plainly, was that Jagger had received a heavier sentence than any anonymous young man would have done, and that this was not what British justice was for. It is a measure of how far the thing had swung that the Establishment's own paper thought the Establishment had lost its head. On appeal at the end of July, Richards's conviction was quashed and Jagger's reduced to a conditional discharge. Between them they had spent a couple of nights inside.

Faithfull was not so lucky, because there is no appeal against a rumour. Within three years her life came apart. The relationship with Jagger ended in 1970; she lost custody of her son; and she spent roughly two years addicted to heroin and homeless on the streets of Soho. She came back — *Broken English*, in 1979, is one of the great comeback records — and she lived, garlanded at last, until 2025. But the girl in the fur rug was thrown to the wolves while the men she was with became legends, and the story that did it to her was never true. She deserves the same care this book has tried to give Christine Keeler, because it is the same crime, committed the same way, four years on.

A few miles upriver, and a few years earlier, the sixties were being invented on a sprung dance floor. Eel Pie Island is a small ait in the Thames at Twickenham, in Middlesex, reachable in those days only by a chain ferry or, from 1957, a footbridge. Its Victorian hotel had a ballroom with a bouncing sprung floor, built in 1898 for Edwardian tea dances. By the mid-1950s the place was decaying, and decay turned out to be exactly what the young wanted.

From 1956, jazz sessions began under a local trumpeter, and a Kingston junk-shop owner named Arthur Chisnall took the club on and turned it into something like a social experiment with a beat. Through the early 1960s the roll-call of who played the island reads like the founding charter of British rock: the Rolling Stones (a Wednesday residency), the Yardbirds, the Who, John Mayall's Bluesbreakers with Eric Clapton, the Tridents with Jeff Beck, Rod Stewart, and later Pink Floyd, David Bowie and Black Sabbath. One regular remembered the floor bouncing the whole crowd up and down in time; the guitarist Don Craine of the Downliners Sect called it an automatic sex machine — you played the music and the floor did the rest.

In 1967 the police forced the hotel to close over a repair bill of £200,000 that the owner could not meet. It reopened briefly in 1969 as Colonel Barefoot's Rock Garden, but by then the building had been colonised by a hippy commune — for a time one of the largest in Britain — and the residents were burning the floorboards for warmth. In 1971, with a demolition order pending, the hotel burned down in what every source calls a mysterious fire.

Today the island is a private enclave of artists' studios and boatyards, opened to the public a couple of weekends a year. The ballroom is gone; the sprung floor that helped teach Britain to dance is ash. Worth remembering, though, when Redlands is invoked as the moment the young collided with the law, that the Stones learned their trade on a bouncing floor over the Thames — a short row upstream from Hampton Court, where a very different court had once done its own scandalising.

### WEB VERSION — redlands-west-wittering
On 12 February 1967, eighteen police officers walked into Keith Richards's thatched house near West Wittering and changed British cultural history without meaning to. They were after drugs. They found a group coming down from an LSD trip to a Bob Dylan record, and very little else: four amphetamine tablets that Mick Jagger took responsibility for (bought legally in Italy by Marianne Faithfull), some hashish, and heroin belonging to the art dealer Robert Fraser. The tip-off is widely believed to have come via the *News of the World*, which Jagger was then suing.

The raid produced the most famous sexual rumour in British history, and it is entirely false. Faithfull, then twenty, had just had a bath and was wrapped in a fur rug. That is all. The tale of a Mars bar used in a sex act appeared in no charge, no court evidence and no newspaper of the day; it spread by word of mouth, possibly from police gossip, and Faithfull spent decades calling it a dirty old man's fantasy. It made the men legends and the woman a punchline.

At Chichester in June 1967, Jagger got three months, Richards a year. Then *The Times* editor William Rees-Mogg ran his famous leader, "Who Breaks a Butterfly on a Wheel?", arguing Jagger had been punished for being famous. On appeal the sentences collapsed. But Faithfull's life did not recover for years: she lost custody of her son, and lived homeless and addicted in Soho before her extraordinary comeback with *Broken English* (1979). The men walked away myths; she was destroyed by a lie.

From 1956 it became one of the crucibles of British rock and R&B. Under promoter Arthur Chisnall, the Rolling Stones held a Wednesday residency; the Yardbirds, the Who, Rod Stewart, John Mayall's Bluesbreakers with Eric Clapton and, later, Pink Floyd and David Bowie all played the hall. The floor bounced the dancers in time with the music — "an automatic sex machine", one musician called it.

It reopened briefly in 1969 as Colonel Barefoot's Rock Garden before a large hippy commune took over the crumbling building, burning the floorboards for fuel. In 1971, with demolition looming, it burned down in a still-unexplained fire. The island is now a private colony of artists' studios and boatyards, open to visitors only a couple of weekends each year.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The soaps they took instead of the cocaine.** Among the odder details of the Redlands search: officers walked off with Keith Richards's collection of hotel soaps, apparently suspecting them, while — by his own later account — missing substances they simply did not recognise. The drug squad of 1967 was learning on the job, and the gap between the moral panic and the officers' actual expertise is one of the quiet comedies of the whole affair.

**The Acid King who was never charged.** Among the Redlands guests was a mysterious American, David Schneiderman, the "Acid King", who had supplied the LSD — and who vanished afterwards, his bag of chemicals conspicuously unsearched. Suspicion that he was an informer, working for the press or the police or both, has never been proven and never quite died. He died in 2004, taking whatever he knew with him.

**Rod Stewart, discovered on a station platform.** Not everyone who made their name around Eel Pie arrived through the front door. Rod Stewart was famously spotted busking on the platform at Twickenham station by the blues singer Long John Baldry, and went on to sing with Baldry's Hoochie Coochie Men on the island. The Thameside R&B scene was small enough that a chance meeting at a railway station could launch a career.

## 52 — Eros Isn't Eros
*Shaftesbury Memorial Fountain, Piccadilly Circus, London · 1886–present*

### BOOK VERSION

The winged archer over Piccadilly Circus is not Eros. He is Eros's brother, and the difference is the entire point. London's most famous erotic monument was never meant to be erotic at all. The sculptor Alfred Gilbert intended it as Anteros — the god of selfless, mature, requited love, as against what Gilbert dismissively called the frivolous tyrant Cupid. It stands atop the Shaftesbury Memorial Fountain, unveiled in 1893 to commemorate Anthony Ashley-Cooper, 7th Earl of Shaftesbury, the factory reformer and campaigner against child labour — a man of such conspicuous Christian earnestness that a nude love-god would have been the very last thing anyone would choose to honour him. That, at any rate, was the theory.

Gilbert described his intention in properly Victorian prose: blindfolded Love, he wrote, sending forth his missile of kindness indiscriminately yet with purpose, ever soaring onwards. The figure was modelled on his studio assistant, an Anglo-Italian boy named Angelo Colarossi, who was fifteen when he posed for it in 1891. And it was, notably, the first statue in the world cast in aluminium — then a rare and novel metal, light enough to let the figure balance on one tiptoe at a jaunty angle that bronze could never have held. Beneath it Gilbert lavished a bronze fountain crawling with fish and crustaceans, a whole marine bestiary, into which the water was supposed to fall in a graceful dome.

It did not fall in a graceful dome. The basin was too narrow, the water splashed passers-by, and the surrounding area turned to mud. Gilbert had also chained drinking cups to the fountain; they were stolen almost at once. And the figure meant to embody chaste philanthropic love was immediately denounced as far too sensual for a memorial to the saintly Earl, and quite unsuitable for the theatre district, which respectable opinion regarded as a haven for exactly the sort of behaviour Anteros was supposed to rise above. Officialdom tried to fix this by renaming it the Angel of Christian Charity — roughly what you would get if you asked a committee to un-invent a joke.

Nobody listened. The public looked at a naked winged archer over Piccadilly and decided, sensibly enough, that it was Eros, god of desire, and Eros it has been ever since. There is a persistent story that Gilbert aimed the bow deliberately down Shaftesbury Avenue as a pun — the shaft buried, *Shaftes-bury* — but the arrow has been repointed so many times during the statue's various exiles that the pun, if it was ever intended, is now largely an accident of traffic engineering. The likelier truth is that the pun grew up afterwards to explain a statue that had already been misnamed.

The fountain ruined the man who made it. Gilbert's commission was for £3,000; the memorial is said to have cost him around £7,000, much of it on that gorgeous, doomed bronze base and on the high price of copper. He called the whole thing a great financial débâcle. He fell into debt, resigned from the Royal Academy, and left England for Bruges in disgrace, not to be rehabilitated for a quarter of a century. He refused even to attend the unveiling. At his lowest he is said to have suggested melting the whole thing down and using the metal to build shelters for the homeless — which would at least have honoured Shaftesbury properly.

The statue itself has led a well-travelled life. It was taken down in the mid-1920s while the tube station was built beneath, and stood for years in the Victoria Embankment Gardens; it went to Egham, in Surrey, for safety during the Second World War while its base was boarded up; it came home in 1947 in heavy rain, before thousands of spectators, re-sited to the corner where it now stands, arrow pointing somewhere new.

And here is the final irony, the one Gilbert never lived to see. A monument raised to selfless, non-sexual, Christian love, in a spot chosen for a man who fought to protect children, became over the twentieth century one of the great pick-up points of London. As "the Dilly", Piccadilly Circus was the acknowledged capital of male prostitution in the West End, a trade documented from Oscar Wilde's day down to the young men who leaned on the very railings around the fountain. Mervyn Harris's 1973 study *The Dilly Boys* followed six of them over a year; the poet Jeremy Reed later traced the whole submerged history in a book simply titled *The Dilly*. Both make the same point: this was a real world, mostly of the poor and the young, and mostly ignored until it was policed.

So London's most famous statue of love is the wrong god, on the wrong site, ruining the right sculptor — and it became, of all things, a byword for precisely the kind of love its maker was at pains to disown. Every couple who has ever said "meet me under Eros" has been keeping an assignation under the god of chastity. He would, one suspects, have seen the funny side. Gilbert never did.

Start with the correction, because it is the whole story: the winged archer over Piccadilly Circus is not Eros. Sculptor Alfred Gilbert made it as Anteros — Eros's brother, the god of selfless, mature, requited love — to crown the 1893 memorial to the 7th Earl of Shaftesbury, the Victorian reformer who fought child labour. A nude love-god was the last thing anyone would have chosen for so pious a man, which is exactly why Gilbert picked the *chaste* brother. It was the first statue in the world cast in aluminium, light enough to balance on one tiptoe.

Almost nothing went to plan. The fountain's basin was too narrow and splashed passers-by into mud; the drinking cups were stolen at once; the "sensual" nude was condemned as unfit for the sober Earl. Officials tried renaming it the Angel of Christian Charity. The public ignored them all and called it Eros — the god of desire — and the name stuck forever.

The statue itself has been shifted repeatedly — to Embankment Gardens for tube works, to Egham in the war — before returning to its present corner in 1947.

The final irony would have appalled Gilbert: his monument to non-sexual love became one of London's most famous pick-up spots, and Piccadilly — "the Dilly" — the recognised centre of male prostitution in the West End, documented from Wilde's time onward. A statue of chastity became a byword for exactly the thing it was built to disown.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The flower girls of the steps.** For decades the fountain's steps were the pitch of Piccadilly's flower sellers, so much a fixture that when the memorial was removed in 1925 for tube works, *The Times* ran a leader on where the ten displaced flower girls would go. Six found new stands around the Circus; the Duke of Westminster provided a site near Park Lane for the rest. The monument to charity had become, without trying, a small welfare case of its own.

**The snow globe that deflated.** In the winter of 2013–14, in a bid to protect the statue from festive vandals (and to sell yet more advertising), the authorities sealed Eros inside a giant inflated PVC snow globe with fans blowing fake snow. High winds promptly damaged and deflated it, and it was never repaired. Gilbert's fragile aluminium god, having survived two world wars and several house-moves, was nearly finished off by a novelty bubble.

**The Evening Standard's borrowed god.** For generations the figure everyone wrongly calls Eros served as the masthead symbol of London's *Evening Standard*. A statue that is the wrong god, on a fountain that never worked, commemorating a man it doesn't resemble, thus became the daily emblem of the city itself — which is, if you think about it, extremely London.

## 53 — The Mothers' Clinic, 1921
*Marie Stopes's Mothers' Clinic, 61 Marlborough Road, Holloway, London · 1921–1925*

### BOOK VERSION

Here is the object at the centre of the story: a small, high-domed rubber cap, about the size of an eggcup, with a tab for easy removal and, printed clearly across the top of the dome, the words "Pro-Race". It is a cervical cap, a barrier contraceptive, and for tens of thousands of British women in the 1920s it was the first reliable control they had ever been offered over their own fertility. The name — Pro-Race — tells you, before we have gone a step further, that this is not going to be a simple story of a heroine.

The woman who designed it and gave it away at cost was Marie Stopes, and she was, on the evidence, both a liberator of women and a eugenicist. There is no honest way to write her that keeps only one of those facts.

Stopes came to the subject the hard way. A distinguished palaeobotanist — the first woman on the science faculty at Manchester — she married, in 1911, a man whose impotence, she said, left the marriage unconsummated, and she claimed to have worked out what was wrong only after private study in the British Museum reading room. (Historians, notably the Wellcome's Lesley Hall, think the tale of total innocence sits at some angle to the truth; Stopes was a great curator of her own myth.) The marriage was annulled in 1916. Out of it came *Married Love* in 1918, a book which argued, to a scandalised nation, that women felt sexual desire, that they were entitled to satisfaction, and that a good marriage required a husband who understood as much. It sold out its first printing in a fortnight and ran through edition after edition; couples kept it like scripture. Later the same year came *Wise Parenthood*, which did the practical work *Married Love* had only gestured at, and told readers how to prevent conception.

On 17 March 1921, Stopes and her second husband, the aircraft manufacturer Humphrey Verdon Roe, opened the Mothers' Clinic for Constructive Birth Control at 61 Marlborough Road, in working-class Holloway, north London. It was the first birth-control clinic in Britain. It was free. It was open to married women only. It was staffed by midwives — the first was Nurse Hebbes — with a visiting doctor, and it was deliberately run by women, in an ordinary house, so that the women who came would not have to be examined by men.

What actually happened there is worth spelling out, because the record is unusually good. A woman would be seen, examined, and fitted with the small rubber cervical cap — Stopes's own high-domed design, a modified French pattern, sold at cost. She was shown how to insert it, moistened with soapy water or a quinine-based ointment, and advised in some cases to use a soluble quinine pessary as well. Stopes disapproved of the condom, of withdrawal, and of the "safe period", which she thought both unreliable and joyless. In that first year an average of three women a day came through the door. In 1925 the clinic moved to Whitfield Street in central London, and that year Stopes published its own report under the title *The First Five Thousand*, claiming that fewer than one per cent of her patients had fallen pregnant while using her methods. By 1929 some ten thousand women had been given contraceptive advice.

The opposition was ferocious, and loudest from the Catholic Church. In 1923 Stopes sued a Catholic doctor, Halliday Sutherland, for libel after he attacked her clinic in print, accusing her in effect of experimenting on the poor. The case ran up through the courts; a jury reached a famously muddled verdict; Stopes was awarded a hundred pounds; and on final appeal to the House of Lords, in 1924, she lost. The Catholic community had raised a substantial fighting fund for Sutherland. The publicity, characteristically, did her cause more good than the verdict did harm.

Now the part that must not be softened, because it was not incidental. Stopes was a committed eugenicist, a life fellow of the Eugenics Society, and her birth-control work was framed, in her own words, around "racial" improvement. The Pro-Race brand was no accident of marketing; her clinic's parent body was called the Society for Constructive Birth Control and Racial Progress. She wanted contraception concentrated in poor districts precisely because she believed the poor were breeding the nation into degeneration, and she advocated the compulsory sterilisation of those she judged unfit for parenthood. She applied the creed to her own family with a coldness that still shocks: when her only surviving son, Harry, married Mary Wallis, who was short-sighted — a defect Stopes regarded as heritable — she refused to attend the wedding and cut him almost entirely out of her will. And in August 1939, weeks before the war, she sent a volume of her love poems to Adolf Hitler with a fawning covering note. She admired the Nazi enthusiasm for "fitness", though she disapproved of some of the regime's aims.

Both of these things are true at once, and the clinic is where they meet. The same small rubber cap that gave a Holloway mother, worn out by a child a year, the power to say "no more" was branded with a slogan of racial purity. You cannot have the liberation without the ideology attached; they came into the world in the same house, on the same day, from the same woman.

It is right to hold both. Think back through this book — the Welsh bundling couples with no sure way to know, Anne Hathaway three months gone at her wedding, the string of children Burns fathered and could not always keep — and you see what the absence of reliable contraception actually cost women, century after century. Stopes ended that, or began to. She gave hundreds of thousands of women a measure of control over their own bodies that no poet, king or Act of Parliament in the preceding chapters had ever managed. And she held views that are indefensible. The plaque on Marlborough Road records the first; it does not mention the second; and the honest reader has to carry them both up the street together.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The graph of desire.** Before she was a clinician, Stopes was a scientist, and she brought a scientist's habits to the bedroom. Among her papers is a hand-drawn chart plotting what she believed was the natural rhythm of female sexual desire across the monthly cycle — an attempt to prove, with a curve on graph paper, that women's wants were regular, real and worth a husband's attention. It is eccentric, and it is also, for 1918, quietly radical.

**The cap that went round the dinner table.** Stopes had no patience for polite squeamishness. At a 1934 fundraising supper in Belfast she reportedly produced a Dutch cap from her handbag and passed it round the table while she explained how it worked, to the horror of the assembled guests. In a city where few doctors dared support birth control at all, it was either monstrous bad manners or a deliberate act of teaching. With Stopes, it was usually both.

**A name the charity finally shed.** The organisation Stopes founded outlived her by generations, carrying her name around the world as Marie Stopes International. In 2020, confronting the eugenics at the root of her work, it renamed itself MSI Reproductive Choices — keeping the mission and dropping the woman. Few reputations have been so completely split down the middle by history.

## 54 — The Gateways
*The Gateways Club, 239 King's Road, Chelsea, London · c.1931–1985*

### BOOK VERSION

The door was painted green — not a striking green, not a green anyone would remark on. That was the point. For fifty years you had to know it was there, at 239 King's Road on the corner of Bramerton Street in Chelsea, and know what was behind it. Down a steep flight of steps into a windowless basement was the Gateways, described by English Heritage as London's best-known and longest-lived lesbian social venue and often called one of the longest-running lesbian clubs in the world. An English Heritage blue plaque was unveiled beside the door on 2 July 2025.

It opened around 1931 as a bohemian drinking club that welcomed the people other places threw out: artists, black musicians, "theatrical types", and, increasingly, women who loved women. A man called Ted Ware took it over in 1943 — legend says he won it in a poker game, or possibly a boxing bet — and it was his wife, the Italian actress Gina Cerrato, who from the later 1950s ran the place with an iron hand, joined by an American ex-airforce woman known only as Smithy, whose exact relationship to Gina was never discussed and widely assumed. In 1967, the year male homosexuality was partly decriminalised, the Gateways went the other way and barred men altogether. It became women-only, and stayed that way to the end.

The physical world of it was tiny and specific. Through the green door in Bramerton Street, down the steps, past Gina at the cloakroom, into a smoky cellar room only about thirty-five feet long: a bar at one end, usually worked by Smithy; a jukebox with, as one visitor put it, a distinct preference for songs about girls; a fruit machine by a central pillar; and a dance floor barely big enough to move on, which was rather the appeal. The artist Maggi Hambling remembered it as all sweat and sway — so many people dancing in so small a space that the crush *was* the excitement.

It ran on rules. No admission after ten. Troublemakers barred on the spot, which mattered, because the nearest alternative was in Brighton. No kissing in the bar — that was for the toilets. And, most tellingly, the place ran on the butch-and-femme codes of its era. Newcomers were, in effect, asked which they were and expected to dress and behave accordingly: the butches in tailored suits and shirts and short hair, the femmes in dresses and heels. The oral historians who later gathered the members' own testimony — above all Jill Gardiner, whose *From the Closet to the Screen* collected some eighty voices — recorded the friction when a younger generation arrived who did not want to choose. One of them, quoted as Brighton Sue, remembered the old diehards with their Brylcreem eyeing the newcomers and demanding: what are you — butch or femme?

In 1968 the club got the fame it never wanted. The director Robert Aldrich filmed scenes for *The Killing of Sister George* there and, wanting authenticity, used the real members as extras, paying them ten pounds a day. Gina herself appears, as the receptionist. For many women in Britain it was the first glimpse they had ever had of a room full of people like them — but the film's portrayal of lesbian life was hardly flattering, Gina regretted taking part, and the exposure brought a mixture of curiosity and hostility to the green door. The Gay Liberation Front later picketed the place for being insufficiently political; Gina, who had no time for politics in her bar and asked members to leave their debates at the top of the stairs, called the police on them.

Which brings us to the law, and to a myth that needs killing on sight. It is often said that sex between women was legal in Britain only because Queen Victoria refused to believe it existed and struck it out of the 1885 Act. This is nonsense, twice over. There was never a clause about women for her to strike out; and the monarch cannot veto part of a bill in any case — royal assent is all or nothing, and the sovereign does not even see the final text. The historian Caroline Derry, whose *Lesbianism and the Criminal Law* is the authority here, has shown that lawmakers knew perfectly well that lesbians existed and chose silence deliberately, partly for fear of putting ideas into respectable women's heads. The story about Victoria seems to have grown up only after a 1977 lesbian protest at a statue of her in New Zealand.

The practical effect of that legal silence is the whole reason a place like the Gateways existed as it did. Because sex between women was never a crime, there were no molly-house raids, no dock, no Oscar Wilde of the lesbians — but there was also no recognition, and the policing was done another way: through licensing, through landlords, through the constant low pressure of a society that preferred you not to exist. A green door in Chelsea was the answer to a law that would not say your name.

The Gateways lost its late licence in 1985, after complaints about the noise, in a Chelsea grown far too expensive for it, and closed in September that year. On the final private night the members prised the nameplate off the door and took it away as a souvenir. The plaque went up forty years later, on 2 July 2025. It is the direct descendant, this basement, of Anne Lister's coded diaries and the Ladies of Llangollen's respectable cottage and the Caravan Club's brief, defiant life — the same long story of women who loved women, told this time behind a door painted the exact green of not being noticed.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**Mick Jagger, turned away at the door.** According to Gina's daughter, Mick Jagger would stop and chat with Gina as he passed on the King's Road, and repeatedly begged to be let inside the famous club. She refused every time. He offered, at one point, to wear a dress. "Darling, I can't," she told him — "it's women-only." One of the most famous men in the world could not buy his way through the green door.

**The book that hid the club in plain sight.** In 1966, the writer Maureen Duffy described "the famous Gateways Club" — the dull green door, the cellar steps, the girls gathering for the weekend — in her chapter on Lesbian London in a popular guide to the city, and fictionalised it as the "House of Shades" in her novel *The Microcosm*. Until then the club had been passed by word of mouth alone. Print made it findable, and slightly less safe.

**A haven that welcomed more than one minority.** The Gateways was never only a lesbian club. In Ted Ware's day it was a meeting place for the Chelsea Arts Club and a rare venue where black Londoners were welcome — the jazz pianist Chester Harriott played there — alongside gay men and anyone else the respectable world excluded. As one member put it, everyone mixed, because everyone was in a minority of one kind or another.

## 55 — Marble Arch, 1970
*First Ann Summers shop, Edgware Road / Marble Arch, London · 1970–present*

### BOOK VERSION

In 1981 a twenty-one-year-old with next to no business training sat in a friend's front room in Thamesmead at what was meant to be a Tupperware party. Someone had asked her to bring along a few things from the family firm, which happened to sell sex toys. Out they came at the end of the evening, and the room — full of perfectly ordinary women — dissolved into giggling, buying and frank conversation. Jacqueline Gold watched it happen and understood something no one running a sex shop in Britain had ever grasped: that the customer for this stuff was a woman, and that she would buy it happily among friends, and never, ever in a shop with a blacked-out window.

To see how large that idea was, you have to go back eleven years and half a city away. On 9 September 1970, the first Ann Summers shop opened on the Edgware Road, near Marble Arch, in London. It was the creation of Michael Caborn-Waterfield, a raffish figure known as "Dandy Kim" — a former black-market nylons trader who had had a long affair with Diana Dors, served time in a French jail, and, it is said, written a sex manual under a pseudonym. He named the shop after his young secretary and former girlfriend Annice Summers (born Annice Goodwin), persuading her to take the surname in return for a directorship, because he thought his own notoriety might frighten the customers. The *Daily Mirror* photographed her outside it and reached for the phrase of the day: a "sex supermarket".

The stock, by the accounts that survive — chiefly the 2016 obituaries, which are better read as trade lore than as audited fact — ran to sex aids, magazines, erotic books, massage lotions and inflatable dolls: the contents of the Soho under-the-counter trade, dragged blinking into a high-street window. It was, unmistakably, a shop for men. Annice fell out with Caborn-Waterfield within months and left, later complaining that she had never agreed to the more explicit goods being sold under her name; the business foundered and went into voluntary liquidation. In 1971 or 1972 — sources differ — the brothers Ralph and David Gold bought the wreck, the name and its debts for a figure usually given as around £10,000. David Gold's daughter Jacqueline joined the firm in 1981, hated the atmosphere of what was still, essentially, the sex trade as everyone pictured it, and then found herself in that suburban front room.

The party plan she built out of it is one of the genuinely revolutionary ideas in modern British retail, and its genius lay entirely in *who it was for*. An Ann Summers party was held in a woman's own living room, hosted by a friend, women only, no men admitted: an evening of drink, games, giggling and the discreet passing-round of lingerie and sex toys, with an order taken at the end. It sold the exact same products as the furtive shop, but it moved them out of the place women found intimidating and into the one place they felt entirely safe. It also, not incidentally, sidestepped the law: because there was no shop and no public display, it slipped past the rules that governed sex retail.

She had to fight for it. She has described taking the idea to Ann Summers's all-male board and being, in her own account, nearly laughed out of the room — one director reportedly threw down his pen and announced that women were not even interested in sex, which told her, she said, rather more about his marriage than about her plan. The banks were wary; she later said she was sent a bullet in the post. She got it launched anyway, and by the end of the first year had recruited more than five hundred party organisers. The model grew at around twenty per cent a year for two decades. Gold became chief executive in the late 1980s, took Ann Summers onto the high street proper, and turned a failing men's sex shop into a chain of well over a hundred stores, run — as the company liked to say — by women, for women. By the 2000s thousands of organisers were hosting thousands of parties a week.

The wider law was catching up all the while, and not in Ann Summers's favour. The Local Government (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1982 introduced, for the first time, a system of local-authority licensing for "sex establishments" — a response, in part, to public disquiet about the spread of Soho-style shops. Whether an Ann Summers counted as a "sex shop" under the Act turned on whether it sold sex articles "to a significant degree", and the company spent years arguing, shop by shop and council by council, that a high-street store full mostly of lingerie and nightwear did not. It is a very British kind of victory: the shop that had once been too disreputable for the high street now insisting, in licensing hearings, that it was really quite respectable.

And that is the note on which to close a chapter called *Monuments to Pleasure*. The great erotic revolution in modern Britain did not happen in Soho, behind the blacked-out windows and the beaded curtains, run by men for men. It happened in suburban living rooms in places like Thamesmead and Sheffield, over a glass of wine, run by women for women — and it was built by a twenty-one-year-old the board had laughed at. A statue that turned out to be the wrong god; a clinic that gave women their bodies back; a basement club behind a green door; and a shop that finally worked out its customer had been a woman all along. Not a law, not a poet, not a king. A handful of buildings and objects and businesses, quietly changing what Britain was allowed to want.
