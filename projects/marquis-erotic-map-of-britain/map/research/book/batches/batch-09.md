## 41 — I Know My Own Heart: Anne Lister
*Shibden Hall, Halifax, West Yorkshire · and Holy Trinity Church, Goodramgate, York · 1791–1840*

### BOOK VERSION
There is a scrap of paper, and on it, in Anne Lister's small crowded hand, the words "In God is my —", the last word written in a code of Greek letters, algebra and zodiac symbols. That word was "hope". In the 1890s a Halifax antiquarian named John Lister, the last of his line to live at Shibden Hall, and his friend Arthur Burrell, a Bradford schoolmaster, used those four letters to crack the whole cipher. When they had read enough of what it concealed, Burrell advised John Lister to burn the diaries. He could not do it. Instead he hid them behind the panelling at Shibden, where they stayed until after his death in 1933. That single act of cowardice, or love, or both, is why we have the most detailed record of a lesbian life anywhere in the English language.

Anne Lister kept a diary from her teens until the year she died, and by the end it ran to some five million words across twenty-six volumes and nearly eight thousand pages. Roughly a sixth of it is in the code she called her "crypt hand". The plain-hand passages record a busy Tory landowner: coal pits and canal shares, rents and roof repairs, mountains climbed and languages learned. The coded passages record the rest of her — her venereal complaints, her opinions of her neighbours, and above all her sexual life, set down with an anatomical frankness that startled the first modern readers so thoroughly that some assumed the diaries were a hoax until the code was checked against her plain hand.

She did not have the word "lesbian" in the sense we use it. She had better words, her own. On 29 January 1821 she wrote, in plain hand, one of the flattest and bravest self-descriptions in the language: "I love & only love the fairer sex & thus beloved by them in turn, my heart revolts from any other love than theirs." In code she was more particular. A "kiss" was not a kiss but an orgasm — hers or a lover's. "Grubbling" was fingering; she was, the diaries make clear, almost always the one who gave rather than received. "Queer" was her word for the vulva. When she pleasured herself she drew a small cross in the margin and wrote that she had "incurred a cross". These are not euphemisms a modern editor has imposed; they are Lister's, recovered by the historian Helena Whitbread, whose first volume, *I Know My Own Heart*, appeared in 1988 and whose second, *No Priest but Love*, followed in 1993, and who called the diaries what they are — a life lived, and recorded, without apology.

The affairs are a whole social world. There was Eliza Raine, the York schoolgirl with whom she first shared the code, the two of them writing to each other as husband and wife. There was Isabella "Tib" Norcliffe, and there was Mariana Belcombe, the great love of her early adulthood, who married a Mr Lawton for security and from whom Lister contracted a venereal infection that she then had to treat, recording the boils and the mercury with the same steady attention she gave the weather. There was Maria Barlow in Paris. And finally, from 1832, there was Ann Walker, a shy and wealthy neighbour whose estate marched conveniently alongside her own, and whom she set out to win with a frankness that reads, at moments, like a business plan and, at others, like devotion.

The town knew what she was, more or less. She dressed always in black, walked like a man, was nicknamed "Gentleman Jack" — not kindly — and did not much care. She ran her estate, opened her own coal pits in the teeth of a male rival, travelled to Paris and the Pyrenees, made the first recorded ascent of the Vignemale in 1838, and died of a fever on 22 September 1840 at Kutaisi, in what is now Georgia, thousands of miles from Shibden, with Ann Walker beside her. Walker had her embalmed body carried the whole way home.

Shibden Hall still stands in its wooded valley outside Halifax, a rambling timber-framed house that Lister loved and improved and drew mortgages against. It is a museum now, run by Calderdale, and since the BBC drama *Gentleman Jack* it receives more visitors in a day than it once did in weeks. The diaries themselves are at the West Yorkshire Archive Service, and in 2011 UNESCO added them to its UK Memory of the World Register, the citation singling out not the coal pits but the "comprehensive and painfully honest account of lesbian life". Every page has been digitised; an army of volunteer transcribers is still working through the plain hand, which turns out to be harder to read than the code.

Twenty-two miles away, in York, is the second place. Holy Trinity, Goodramgate, is a small medieval church reached down an alley off a shopping street, easy to walk past, full of box pews and old glass. On Easter Sunday, 30 March 1834, Anne Lister and Ann Walker knelt here and took the sacrament together. They had already exchanged rings; Lister's diary records that she prayed their union might be happy. Neither the Church of England nor the law had any category for what they were doing, and they knew it. They treated it as a marriage anyway. It is now widely called the first lesbian marriage in Britain, which is a modern honour laid on a private act, but the private act was real and Lister recorded it plainly.

In July 2018 York Civic Trust put up a plaque outside the church — the country's first permanent rainbow plaque. It described Lister as a "Gender-nonconforming entrepreneur" and did not use the word lesbian. The omission caused a national row. A petition started by the campaigner Julie Furlong gathered more than 2,500 signatures, arguing, reasonably, that Lister was indeed gender-nonconforming all her life and was also, unmistakably and in her own words, a woman who loved only women. In February 2019 the Trust replaced it. The new plaque calls her, correctly, "Lesbian and Diarist", and Helena Whitbread gave a talk to mark the unveiling. The Trust also quietly fixed the rainbow border, which had first gone up upside down.

It is worth holding the shape of the whole thing in mind. A woman writes down her desire in a code so that it will survive; a man decodes it, is appalled, and hides it rather than burns it; historians a century later read past the coal accounts to the crosses in the margin; and a plaque committee in our own decade has to be argued into using the right word. The record survived, in other words, only because Lister encrypted it and then someone lost his nerve at the crucial moment. She had wondered, in that same 1834 spring, whether she and Walker could ever live openly. Twelve years earlier she had asked exactly that question of another couple, two elderly Irishwomen in a Gothic cottage in North Wales, and driven the length of the country to find out.

Behind the code lay the most detailed record of a lesbian life in the English language: her lovers, her techniques and her own vocabulary for them — a "kiss" meant an orgasm, "grubbling" meant fingering, a cross in the margin marked masturbation. ### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**The two-million-word enigma.** For decades the diaries sat half-read. Arthur Burrell handed the code key to Halifax's librarian in the 1930s; researchers in the 1950s were made to submit extracts for approval, effectively censoring the sex. Only a 1984 *Guardian* feature and Helena Whitbread's arrival in the archive broke the silence — proof that suppression did not end with John Lister's panelling.

**The venereal cross.** Not every coded passage is about pleasure. Lister used the same crypt hand to record contracting a venereal infection from Mariana Lawton, who had it from her husband, and to track her own treatment — the boils, the mercury. The frankness that makes the diaries precious about desire makes them equally unflinching about its costs.

**Embalmed home from Georgia.** When Lister died of fever at Kutaisi in 1840, fourteen months into a vast tour of Russia and the Caucasus, Ann Walker had her body embalmed and shipped the entire way back to Halifax for burial. Walker's own later years were unhappy; she was removed from Shibden and declared of unsound mind, and only recently has her story been told in her own right.

## 42 — Delicious Retirement: The Ladies of Llangollen
*Plas Newydd and St Collen's Church, Llangollen, Denbighshire · 1778–1831*

### BOOK VERSION
The woman she had come the length of the country to interrogate was, in the end, not much help. When Anne Lister called at Plas Newydd in July 1822 she was on a private mission: she wanted to know whether two women really could build a life together, and whether the most famous such pair in Britain were lovers. She met only Sarah Ponsonby, then in her sixties, and tried to establish the point obliquely, asking whether the Ladies were "classical" — code, in Lister's world, for Sapphic. Ponsonby gave nothing away. But Lister came away certain enough, and confided it to her diary and to her own lover Mariana: "I cannot help thinking that surely it was not platonic. Heaven forgive me, but I look within myself & doubt." One queer woman had assessed another couple and reached a verdict. It is the sharpest single piece of evidence we have, and it is still only a guess.

Rewind forty-four years, to a spring night in 1778 in County Kilkenny. Lady Eleanor Butler, daughter of a grand and impoverished Catholic family and by then approaching forty, and Sarah Ponsonby, an orphaned Protestant of twenty-three fending off the unwanted attentions of her guardian, climbed out into the dark and ran. Ponsonby, the story goes, was in men's clothes and carrying a pistol. They were caught and dragged back. Butler was threatened with a French convent; Ponsonby hid her, at one point, in a bedroom while a loyal servant named Mary Carryll smuggled in food. The families raged, then wearied, then relented, and settled small allowances on the pair to be rid of the scandal. In May 1778 the two women left Ireland for good, with Carryll, and after wandering North Wales for some weeks they leased a cottage above the little town of Llangollen. They renamed it Plas Newydd, and there they stayed for half a century.

What they made of it was extraordinary and slightly mad. They turned a plain stone cottage into a black-and-white Gothic fantasia, encrusting it with carved oak salvaged from churches, fitting pointed arches and stained glass, setting a medieval font in the garden. They read in French, Italian and Spanish, kept a library, laid out walks, and dressed alike in dark riding habits with their hair powdered and cropped short, so that visitors never quite knew what to call the effect. They referred to their life as "delicious retirement" — and then proceeded to be anything but retiring, receiving a stream of the age's most famous people. Wordsworth came, and wrote a not very good sonnet. Byron, Shelley, Walter Scott, the Duke of Wellington, Josiah Wedgwood, Madame de Genlis, the young De Quincey — all made the pilgrimage up the hill. Queen Charlotte took an interest and helped secure them a royal pension in 1788.

The question of what they were to each other was raised in their own lifetime, and they hated it. In July 1790 an article headed "Extraordinary Female Affection" appeared in the *St James's Chronicle* and was reprinted in the *General Evening Post*. It praised the cottage and its occupants but then drew a portrait that any reader could decode: Miss Butler "tall and masculine", always in a riding habit, hanging up her hat "with the air of a sportsman"; Miss Ponsonby "polite and effeminate, fair and beautiful". This was the standard eighteenth-century sketch of a mannish woman and her feminine companion, and the Ladies were furious enough to consider legal action. In private, the diarist Hester Thrale — who counted them as friends — called them, in her own journal, "damned Sapphists", and reckoned no woman would spend a night at Plas Newydd without a man for propriety. Byron, imagining an ideal love with a Cambridge chorister, listed the Ladies among history's great couples he hoped to outshine.

And yet. The honest answer to the obvious question is that we do not know, and cannot. No diary of theirs records a "kiss" in Lister's sense; no letter confesses. They shared a bed, called each other "my Beloved" and "the darling of my heart", named a dog Sappho, and lived as a married couple in every visible respect for fifty years. They also presented themselves, fiercely, as models of chaste and elevated "romantic friendship" — a category their era permitted to women, and one that functioned, whether they intended it or not, as a defence. To insist they were lovers is to project; to insist they were chaste is to project the other way, and to do the era's own respectability work for it. What is certain is the devotion, the half-century, and the shared grave.

Eleanor Butler died in 1829, in her ninetieth year and by then nearly blind. Sarah Ponsonby followed in December 1831. They are buried together at St Collen's Church in Llangollen under a large three-sided monument, each face given to one of the three women who had run away together and stayed together. For the third face belongs to Mary Carryll, the servant who had hidden Eleanor in a bedroom in 1778, who kept house for them without wages for over thirty years, who saved and bought land and left it to them, and who died in 1809, twenty years before either of the women she served. They had promised she would lie with them, and she does. Whatever the Ladies of Llangollen were to each other, they were, all three, a household — and it is the maid, arriving first, who has waited longest under the stone.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**The house that ate churches.** The Ladies were magpies of the Gothic. Much of Plas Newydd's astonishing carved-oak interior was salvaged from demolished churches and old furniture, and in the garden they set a medieval font from the ruined Valle Crucis Abbey and a fragment of Chester's High Cross. They were curating a mood — melancholy, medieval, deliberately out of time — a century before anyone called it heritage.

**Wordsworth's bad sonnet.** In 1824 Wordsworth visited and wrote a sonnet praising Plas Newydd as a "low-roofed Cot" in a "Vale of Friendship". The Ladies were reportedly less than thrilled that he had called their beloved house a cot. Byron was ruder in private and Hester Thrale ruder still; being famous for wanting to be left alone brought a great deal of company and comment.

**The pension and the poverty.** For all their grand visitors, the Ladies were chronically short of money, their families' allowances barely covering the constant improvements. Queen Charlotte's intercession secured Eleanor Butler a royal pension in 1788, and various admirers left them legacies. They lived, in effect, on their celebrity — the price of a private life made public.

## 43 — The Marrying Room: Mother Clap's
*Field Lane, Holborn, London · c.1724–1726*

### BOOK VERSION
Go looking for Mother Clap's molly house and you will find a viaduct. Field Lane, the narrow Holborn street where it stood between an archway and the Bunch o' Grapes tavern, was swallowed whole in the 1860s by the building of Holborn Viaduct, and there is nothing to see — no plaque, no fragment, no doorway. This is worth saying plainly at the outset, because the erasure is part of the story. The men who drank and danced and paired off in Margaret Clap's rooms have no monument. What they have instead is a set of trial records, gathered by the people who wanted them dead.

A molly house was a tavern, coffee house or private room where men who desired men could meet — to drink, to talk, to dance, to take female names and personae, and to have sex. "Molly" was the word gay men of the period used among themselves. There had been such places in London since about 1700, and the standard modern study, Rictor Norton's *Mother Clap's Molly House* of 1992, argues persuasively that most were not brothels but something warmer: social clubs, meeting places, somewhere to be among your own. Margaret Clap's, in Field Lane, was the most celebrated of them in the mid-1720s. It was owned in her husband's name but was hers to run, and on a Sunday night it might hold forty or fifty men. There was a fire, there was drink, and, by the account of one hostile witness, there was a bed in every room.

We know what went on there in humiliating detail because a constable named Samuel Stevens infiltrated the place in the autumn of 1725, pretending to be the "husband" of a molly turned informer, and later swore to what he had seen. His deposition, read out at the Old Bailey at Gabriel Lawrence's trial in April 1726 and preserved in the printed trial reports, is the fullest description we have of the inside of such a house — and it is, of course, a prosecution document, written to hang people. Read it that way and it becomes something the constable never intended: a picture of a party. "I found between 40 and 50 Men making Love to one another," Stevens deposed. "Sometimes they would sit on one another's Laps, kissing in a lewd Manner... Then they would get up, Dance and make Curtsies, and mimick the voices of Women. O, Fie, Sir! — Pray, Sir. — Dear Sir. Lord, how can you serve me so!... Then they'd hug, and play, and toy, and go out by Couples into another Room on the same Floor, to be marry'd, as they call'd it." That other room, the mollies called the marrying room. A doorkeeper named Eccleston stood guard so that the couples inside were not disturbed.

The mollies had their own rituals and their own theatre, though the record here needs careful handling. The most famous is the mock "lying-in", in which a man mimed the whole performance of childbirth and was "delivered" of a wooden doll, which was then mock-christened. That scene is vivid and real, but it comes from a satire — Ned Ward's account of a "Mollies Club" published in 1709, well before Clap's existed, describing an unnamed City tavern where a "Sodomitical" brother, his belly cushioned up and dressed in a woman's nightgown, groaned to be delivered of "a joynted Babie". Ward wrote to titillate and moralise, not to bear witness; but he did not invent the ritual whole, for versions of it recur across the century, and men were caught performing it as late as the 1810s. The mock marriage is better attested — Stevens's "to be marry'd, as they call'd it" is trial evidence — and at a separate molly house, George Whittle's Royal Oak off Pall Mall, a room was reportedly called "the Chapel". So: the marrying room at Clap's is court record; the wooden baby is Ned Ward's satire; and it is worth keeping them apart, because the whole difficulty of this history is that almost everything we know was written down either to mock these men or to kill them.

In February 1726 the raids came. They were driven by the Societies for the Reformation of Manners, self-appointed moral vigilantes who paid informers and had made it their business to rid London of sodomites, prostitutes and Sabbath-breakers. Clap's had been watched for two years; the informing was set off, it seems, by a molly named Mark Partridge, outed by a lover and then pressured into betraying his friends. On a Sunday night the constables blocked the exits and took some forty men to Newgate. None had been caught in the act of sodomy — a few had their breeches unbuttoned — and most were eventually released for want of evidence. But the machine, once started, needed victims. On 9 May 1726 three men were hanged together at Tyburn on the triangular gallows, each from his own beam: Gabriel Lawrence, a forty-three-year-old milkman and widowed father; William Griffin, a furniture upholsterer of the same age who had lodged at Clap's; and Thomas Wright, who kept his own molly house. They were hanged, essentially, on the word of a single turncoat, Thomas Newton, who had agreed to testify in exchange for his own skin. The crowd was large; the mollies shared the day with a famous husband-murderer, and a spectators' scaffold collapsed, killing and maiming onlookers who had come to watch lovers die.

Margaret Clap herself was tried in July 1726 for keeping a disorderly house. She protested, reasonably enough, "I am a Woman, and therefore it cannot be thought that I would ever be concern'd in such Practices." It did her no good. She was sentenced to stand in the pillory at Smithfield, to pay a fine and to serve two years' imprisonment. The pillory was not a mild punishment; a crowd could maim or kill a person pinned in it, and it is often said she died of her injuries, though the record simply loses her after the sentence. That is where Mother Clap vanishes — not into legend, but into the ordinary silence that closes over people the state has finished using.

The joke of the whole episode, if there is one, is entirely at the expense of the Societies for the Reformation of Manners, who spent two years and several lives to close one coffee house and succeeded only in creating, in the trial papers, the richest surviving record of the very subculture they meant to erase. They wrote it all down so that they could destroy it, and by writing it down they preserved it. Two hundred years later, and a mile to the west, the Metropolitan Police would do exactly the same thing to a basement club in Endell Street — and once again the surveillance file would outlast the persecution and become the memorial.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**Princess Seraphina.** The best-documented individual molly is John Cooper, known to everyone as Princess Seraphina — a servant and "messenger" for the mollies who wore, one witness said, "a white Gown, and a scarlet Cloak". In 1732 she did something astonishing: she went to the Old Bailey not as defendant but as prosecutor, charging a man with robbing her of her clothes at knifepoint. Witnesses cheerfully called her "the Princess" and used "she". He was acquitted; she was never charged with anything.

**The wooden baby.** The mock "lying-in" — a man miming childbirth and being "delivered" of a jointed wooden doll, then mock-christened — is the most theatrical molly ritual. But its fullest description is Ned Ward's satire of 1709, written to mock, not to witness, and set in an unnamed tavern years before Clap's. Handle with care: it is real folklore, recorded by a hostile hand.

**Not caught in the act.** For all the hangings, almost none of the men raided at Clap's had been caught having sex. A few had unbuttoned breeches; the rest were convicted, when convicted at all, on the paid testimony of informers like Thomas Newton and Mark Partridge, mollies who turned on their friends to save themselves. The evidence that survives is the evidence that was manufactured to kill.

## 44 — The Love That Dare Not: Wilde's Arc
*Portora Royal School, Enniskillen, County Fermanagh · Magdalen College, Oxford · 34 Tite Street, Chelsea · and the Cadogan Hotel, Sloane Street, London · 1864–1900*

### BOOK VERSION
On a classroom windowsill at Portora Royal School, in Enniskillen, County Fermanagh, a boy once scratched his initials — O.W. In 1895, when that boy had become the most famous playwright in London and then, within a season, the most notorious convict in England, a headmaster took the trouble to have the two letters scraped away. That small act of erasure — the effort of it, in a large school scrimshawed with the graffiti of generations of bored boys — tells you almost everything about how the age chose to remember Oscar Wilde: by unremembering him. His name was painted out of the school's honours board too. It went back up, decades later, and now, the story goes, shines brighter than the names around it because the gilt is newer. Whether the exact detail is legend or fact scarcely matters; the shape of it is true. They tried to remove him and could not.

Wilde was at Portora from 1864 to 1871, a bookish boy nicknamed "Grey Crow" who was not, by his own later account, happy there and preferred to remember only a year of it. From Enniskillen he went to Trinity College Dublin and then, in October 1874, to Magdalen College, Oxford, on a demyship — a scholarship peculiar to the college. Oxford made him. He took a double first in classics, won the Newdigate Prize in 1878 for his poem *Ravenna*, and fell under the two contrary influences that would pull at him for the rest of his life: John Ruskin, who believed beauty carried a moral duty, and Walter Pater, whose *Studies in the History of the Renaissance* urged instead a life lived for intense sensation, burning always with a hard, gemlike flame. Wilde took the flame. He grew his hair, decorated his rooms, cultivated the pose of the aesthete, and left Oxford already half-famous for being himself.

In 1884 he married Constance Lloyd, and the following January the couple moved into a narrow house at 16 Tite Street, Chelsea — now number 34 — in the artists' quarter by the river. E. W. Godwin decorated the interior, much of it startling white, and here Wilde spent his ten years of fame. Both his sons were born in the house; here he wrote almost everything that matters — *The Happy Prince*, *The Picture of Dorian Gray*, the great comedies. And here the double life took hold. In 1886 he began an affair with Robert Ross, then seventeen and probably the first of his male lovers, later the most loyal of his friends and his literary executor. In 1891 he met Lord Alfred Douglas — "Bosie" — a beautiful, spoilt, twenty-one-year-old aristocrat, and the affair that would destroy him began.

Here the honest telling has to slow down. Douglas introduced Wilde to a world of young working-class men — some procured through Alfred Taylor, who kept a house that functioned as an introducing-place — whom Wilde took to hotel rooms and dinners and showered with gifts and money. Several were teenagers of seventeen or eighteen; several were "renters", who traded sex for cash; the class gulf between them and Wilde was enormous, and money changed hands. None of this fits the martyr's outline neatly, and it should not be sanded down. But two things must be held alongside it. First, almost everything we "know" of these encounters comes from a criminal prosecution that set out to destroy Wilde and paid and pressured its witnesses accordingly, so the record is skewed by the same hostility that shaped the molly trials a century and a half before. Second, the men were consenting adults or near-adults under the law of their day, and what was on trial was not exploitation but desire between men as such. To sanitise Wilde is dishonest; to join his prosecutors is worse.

The catastrophe was of Wilde's own making, at least in its timing. Bosie's father, the Marquess of Queensberry — a violent, half-mad aristocrat who had given the world the rules of boxing — left a card at Wilde's club accusing him of "posing somdomite", the misspelling now more famous than anything Queensberry did on purpose. Against all advice, Wilde sued him for criminal libel. It was a disaster. Queensberry's defence had assembled a file of witnesses; under cross-examination by Edward Carson the case collapsed, and on the morning of 5 April 1895 Wilde withdrew it. A warrant for his own arrest followed within hours.

He spent that day not fleeing but waiting, in the Cadogan Hotel on Sloane Street, in a room now generally given as 118, drinking hock and seltzer with Ross and Reggie Turner while friends begged him to catch the boat train to France. He would not go. At about twenty past six in the evening two detectives came up the stairs and took him away. The exact date is worth pinning, because sources differ: the arrest is variously dated 5 and 6 April, and the confusion is real — the libel case collapsed on the morning of the 5th and the arrest happened that same evening, though many accounts, including the hotel's own, give the 6th. John Betjeman later made the scene into one of his best-loved poems, all Nottingham lace and the plainclothes men at the door; it is in copyright, so take it as recommended rather than quoted, but its picture of Wilde too drunk and too proud to run is close to the truth.

The trials that followed were held under Section 11 of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885 — the Labouchere Amendment, which criminalised "gross indecency" between men without troubling to define it, and which lawyers rightly nicknamed the blackmailer's charter. The first jury could not agree; the Crown tried again. On 25 May 1895 Wilde was convicted and sentenced to two years' hard labour, the maximum, the judge complaining it was too little. He served it in Pentonville, Wandsworth and finally Reading Gaol, where the regime broke his health and where, given paper at last, he wrote the long, wounded letter to Douglas that Ross would publish, cut down, as *De Profundis*. Released in 1897, bankrupt and disgraced, he went into exile in France under an assumed name, wrote *The Ballad of Reading Gaol*, and died in a cheap Paris hotel on 30 November 1900, aged forty-six, of meningitis following an ear infection. Robbie Ross was at the bedside. Bosie was chief mourner at the funeral.

Tite Street carries a plaque. The Cadogan sells a room and a cocktail on the strength of the arrest. Portora put the name back on the board. And in 2017, under the provisions of the Policing and Crime Act — the "Alan Turing law" — Wilde was among tens of thousands of men pardoned posthumously for convictions that would not be crimes today. It was the right thing, and it was also, for Wilde, a piece of paper issued to a man a hundred and seventeen years dead, for a crime that was never a crime, by the successors of the state that had jailed him. It changed nothing and it corrected nothing that could still be felt. The scraped windowsill at Portora had already been proved wrong the moment the name went back up.

Wilde excluded from the reading room." In 2025 the British Library reissued a reader's card in his name, its expiry date set to 30 November 1900 — the day he died. His grandson Merlin Holland collected it.

## 45 — All-Night Gaiety: The Caravan Club
*81 Endell Street, Covent Garden, London · July–August 1934*

### BOOK VERSION
The door is still there. Walk east from Cambridge Circus into Endell Street and, on a building that now houses ordinary offices and flats, you can find number 81 — a dark doorway with a row of bells and a small grille, the sort of entrance you would pass a hundred times without a glance. Down the stairs behind it, for six weeks in the summer of 1934, was the most extraordinary queer social space in interwar London, and the reason we can describe what it looked like at all is that the Metropolitan Police wrote it all down before they destroyed it.

The Caravan Club opened on 14 July 1934 in the basement of 81 Endell Street, and advertised itself with cheerful nerve as "London's Greatest Bohemian Rendezvous, said to be the most unconventional spot in town", promising "all-night gaiety" and "dancing to Charlie", the club's musician. "Bohemian rendezvous" was a code any knowing reader could break: it meant a place where men could dance with men and women with women. It was run by Jack Neave — a former escapologist and strongman known as "Iron Foot Jack" for the metal brace that lengthened his short leg, and who styled himself King of the Bohemians — and financed by a young man named William Reynolds, called Billy, who had a string of convictions and a recent inheritance to spend. They rented the basement, furnished it fast and cheap with divans and cushions and draped fabric in a vaguely oriental style, and threw open the doors.

The response was immediate. In six weeks the Caravan enrolled 445 members and drew, by the police count, 2,004 visitors. Membership cost a shilling, or one and six on the door. And from the start it was watched. Neighbours and Holborn Borough Council complained; one anonymous handwritten note, now in the file at The National Archives, denounced the place as an "absolute sink of iniquity... frequented by sexual perverts, lesbians and sodomites", and begged the authorities to act. They already were. Plainclothes officers had been observing the club from an unused part of the Shaftesbury Theatre and an old school building nearby, keeping hour-by-hour logs of who came and went and straining to overhear anything that would let them call the place a "disorderly house" in law. It is from these surveillance reports, and the ones written after the raid, that we know the Caravan from the inside.

They are remarkable documents, and like Samuel Stevens's deposition two centuries before, they record with hostile precision the very life they meant to end. The officer in charge of the raid, Divisional Detective Inspector Clarence Campion, described a small dance floor so crowded his men could barely take their positions unnoticed, men dancing together, couples pressed close, make-up and plucked eyebrows and effeminate dress noted down as evidence. When he stopped the music he announced that the police were there and that everyone present was aiding and abetting a house kept "in a manner likely to corrupt morals". The file preserves individual voices too. Torn up under a divan, with a powder puff, the police found a love letter from a member who signed himself Cyril — Cyril Coeur de Leon — which included the extraordinary, matter-of-fact line, "I have only been queer since I came to London." And when the raid began, Cyril, according to Campion's own report, approached the inspector and asked, coolly, whether he might have one of the officer's "nice boys" to take home. The prosecution kept his letter to damn him; instead it kept him.

The raid came in the early hours of 25 August 1934. A total of 103 people were arrested and taken to Bow Street. Crowds gathered outside the magistrates' court to watch the accused arrive. The members faced charges of aiding and abetting; the proprietors, Neave and Reynolds, faced the graver charge of keeping a disorderly house. The trial ended in late October 1934. Both men were convicted and sentenced to hard labour — Reynolds to twelve months, Neave to twenty. The *Daily Express* reporter noted that as Neave was taken down to the cells, the heavy clamp of his iron boot could be heard on the stairs.

And there the Caravan should have vanished, as Mother Clap's vanished, as Field Lane vanished under its viaduct. Instead its persecution became its preservation. The police files — catalogued at The National Archives as MEPO 3/758 and DPP 2/224, and including the surveillance logs, the seized membership cards, Cyril's retyped love letter, a sketch plan of the basement and photographs of the empty, draped interior taken during the raid — turn out to be the fullest surviving record of an interwar queer social space anywhere in Britain. Historic England has made the file a centrepiece of its Pride of Place and LGBTQ heritage work; the National Trust and The National Archives recreated the Caravan, cocktails and accordion and all, in 2017, to mark fifty years since the partial decriminalisation of homosexuality. We can throw that party again only because a detective inspector once tried to end it and filed his report.

It is exactly the pattern of the molly houses, two centuries and a mile apart. In 1726 the constables of the Societies for the Reformation of Manners watched Mother Clap's, gathered their evidence, and hanged three men — and left behind, without meaning to, the richest picture we have of the eighteenth-century molly subculture. In 1934 the Metropolitan Police watched the Caravan, gathered their evidence, and jailed two men — and left behind the richest picture we have of the interwar one. The persecutors kept the only albums. Everything the lovers themselves might have written was torn up under a divan, or hidden behind panelling, or encrypted, or never set down at all. What the state wrote down to destroy them is, too often, all that is left to remember them by — and it is, against every intention of the men who compiled it, enough.
