## 21 — The Rude Man of Cerne
*Cerne Abbas Giant · Cerne Abbas, Dorset, England · c. AD 700–1100 to present*

### BOOK VERSION

There is a photograph the English Post Office would not, for many decades, allow through the mail. It was not smuggled contraband or a saucy French postcard. It was a picture postcard of a Dorset hillside. The offending item was the Cerne Abbas Giant, and the trouble was the thing you cannot help noticing about him: a chalk-cut naked man, 55 metres from heel to head, brandishing a knobbled club in his right hand and, below a set of neatly drawn ribs, an erection some 11 metres — around 36 feet — long, nearly the length of his own head. He has been called, with some justice, Britain's most famous phallus.

Start with the anatomy, because the anatomy turns out to be the whole argument in miniature. The Giant we photograph today is not quite the Giant our great-great-grandparents photographed. Somewhere in the figure's long history of being neglected, grassed over and re-cut, his navel and his penis were joined into a single feature, and the length of the latter increased. Leslie Grinsell's 1980 paper in the journal *Antiquity* drew attention to this, comparing the earliest illustration of 1764 with the modern outline, and concluded that the navel had "become added to the penis, increasing the length of the latter by some 5 or 6 feet". A navel that appears clearly on that eighteenth-century drawing, and still on a picture postcard from the very end of the nineteenth century, had vanished by the time the twentieth was under way, absorbed upwards during a re-cut around 1908. Whether that was clumsiness or a private joke by the workmen we shall never know. But it means the single rudest thing about the rudest figure in England is, in its present dimensions, younger than the motor car.

For most of the last three hundred years the argument about the Giant was a two-horse race. Either he was ancient — Iron Age, Romano-British, a god of the Durotriges, a chalk Hercules — or he was a joke, a seventeenth-century lampoon of Oliver Cromwell cut by a bored landowner, the club a dig at Puritan rule and the phallus a comment on Puritan hypocrisy. In 1996 the two theories were put on trial, literally, in a public and televised debate in Cerne Abbas village hall, published as *The Cerne Giant: An Antiquity on Trial*. The jury could not decide, because there was nothing to decide with. That is the crux of the whole thing, and it deserves to be said plainly: there is no record of the Cerne Giant before 1694. None. No medieval chronicler, no monk of the abbey that sat at the foot of his hill from 987, no Tudor surveyor, no antiquarian, no map-maker mentions a colossal naked man on the hillside above the village. The first documentary trace of him is an entry in the Cerne Abbas churchwardens' accounts recording three shillings spent on the re-cutting of the figure. A giant with a 36-foot erection is not an easy thing to miss, and the silence of everyone who ought to have seen him is not a gap in the evidence. It is the evidence.

Then, in 2020, the National Trust — which has owned the Giant since the Pitt-Rivers family gave him over in July 1920 — decided to settle the matter for the centenary. Working with the geoarchaeologist Mike Allen and Professor Phillip Toms of the University of Gloucestershire, Trust archaeologists led by Martin Papworth cut four small trenches into the elbows and feet and took samples for Optically Stimulated Luminescence dating, a technique that reveals when grains of sand last saw daylight. The samples then sat in a locked laboratory through the first Covid lockdown before anyone could read them. When the results came, in 2021, they surprised everyone. The two earliest samples ranged between AD 650 and 1110, with a tenth-century median date — placing the Giant's making startlingly close to the founding of Cerne Abbey in 987. Not Iron Age. Not Cromwell. Medieval. "Everyone was wrong," said Allen, cheerfully, "and that makes these results even more exciting." His environmental work turned up a supporting witness in the soil: microscopic snail shells of species that only arrived in Britain in the medieval period, probably as stowaways in imported hay.

If the date was a surprise, it also opened a door. In 2024, in the medieval studies journal *Speculum*, Thomas Morcom of the University of Oslo and Helen Gittos of the University of Oxford made the case that the Giant was cut in the ninth or early tenth century as an image of Hercules, and that he stood where he stands because his hillside was a muster station — an unmistakable landmark where West Saxon armies could gather at a time when Dorset was being raided by Viking fleets. The club and the outstretched arm fit Hercules and his lion-skin, they argue; a resistivity survey back in 1980 had already suggested the ghost of a cloak or skin over the left arm. And here the story folds neatly back on itself, because by the eleventh century the monks in the abbey below appear to have quietly re-branded their embarrassingly nude neighbour as an image of their own hermit-saint, Eadwold — nakedness, after all, being a recognised sign of holy poverty. From Hercules to hermit, and eventually to fertility god: the Giant has been re-imagined by every century that has looked at him. He is less an ancient monument than a mirror.

Which brings us, at last, to the sex. The Cerne Giant is famous today as a fertility figure — the place childless couples come to conceive. What is remarkable, given the fame of the belief, is how young it is. The fertility folklore is first recorded in the Victorian era, not before. The specific practices are these: a woman who wished to conceive would spend a night alone on the figure, most efficaciously within the outline of the phallus itself; and couples hoping for a child would have intercourse on him. Just above his head, on the crest of the hill, sits a small Iron Age earthwork called the Trendle, or the Frying Pan, and in this ring the village raised a maypole, around which — the former sexton of Cerne told a folklorist in 1901 — young people danced on May morning. Dancing round a maypole above a giant phallus does rather suggest itself as fertility magic, and the antiquarians of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, steeped in Frazer's *The Golden Bough*, duly wrote it up as the survival of an immemorial pagan rite. But the maypole memory reaches back no further than living recollection, and the earliest firm record of any of it is Victorian. The belief is real and moving; it is simply not old.

Does it work? A local report noted that women in North Dorset average close to double the national birthrate, which proves nothing except that Dorset is good at statistics as well as at chalk. The most quoted testimonial belongs to the sixth Marquess of Bath, who visited in the 1980s having struggled to conceive; he later told the *New Yorker* that he "explained the problem and sat on him", and that a daughter arrived some ten months afterwards. One sits with that image for a moment, and moves on.

The Giant is now a scheduled monument, protected by the highest grade of archaeological law, and the Trust asks people to stay off him — every footfall speeds his erosion, and the seventeen tonnes of fresh chalk hammered in by hand every decade or so cannot keep pace with enthusiasm. He has, over the years, been fitted with a giant condom by a family-planning charity, given the word "Theresa" spelled out along his member, worn a Borat mankini, and been recreated on a Sussex school playing field by the girls of Roedean. We came looking for a prehistoric god of fertility and found, instead, a medieval war-marker with a Victorian sex life and a modern comedy career. Yet the detail that stays with you is quieter: through every theory, re-cut and publicity stunt, he has kept the same expression he has worn since a Saxon turf-cutter first gave him a face.

Peru has the Nazca Lines; Dorset has a 55-metre naked man with a club and an erection you can see from the next hillside. The Cerne Abbas Giant is Britain's largest and rudest chalk hill figure, and for three centuries nobody could agree how old he was. Was he an Iron Age god? A Romano-British Hercules? A seventeenth-century skit on Oliver Cromwell? In 2020, for the centenary of National Trust ownership, archaeologists finally found out. Professor Phillip Toms of the University of Gloucestershire used Optically Stimulated Luminescence to date the deepest chalk, and the answer, announced in 2021, floored everyone: the two earliest samples ranged AD 650–1110, with a tenth-century median date — close to the founding of Cerne Abbey in 987. Not ancient, not a joke. Medieval. Microscopic snail shells in the soil, of species that reached Britain only in the Middle Ages, backed the date up.

The crucial fact is the silence. There is no written record of the Giant before 1694, when the Cerne Abbas churchwardens noted three shillings spent re-cutting him. A giant with a 36-foot erection is hard to overlook, yet no monk, surveyor or map-maker mentions him for centuries. In 2024, Oxford's Helen Gittos and Oslo's Thomas Morcom argued in *Speculum* that he was cut as Hercules to mark a muster point for West Saxon armies facing the Vikings, and later re-branded by the local monks as their hermit-saint Eadwold.

His fertility fame is younger still. The belief that a woman could conceive by sleeping on him — best of all within the phallus — or that couples should make love on the figure is first recorded only in the Victorian era. So is the maypole that once stood in the Trendle earthwork above his head. Even his most famous feature has shifted: around 1908 his separate navel was absorbed into the penis during a re-cut, lengthening it by five or six feet. The rudest thing about him is younger than the car. He's a scheduled monument now; admire him from the lay-by, and please don't climb on him.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The Marquess who sat on him.** The best-known fertility testimonial belongs to the sixth Marquess of Bath, who visited in the 1980s after struggling to conceive. He later told the *New Yorker* he "explained the problem and sat on him", and a daughter followed about ten months later. It proves nothing, of course — but it is exactly the kind of anecdote that keeps a Victorian folk-belief alive and well into the twenty-first century.

**The trial of 1996.** Before science settled it, Cerne Abbas held a mock trial. In March 1996, in the village hall, three theories of the Giant's origin — prehistoric, Civil-War-era lampoon, and "the biography matters more than the date" — were argued before a jury and televised, then published as *The Cerne Giant: An Antiquity on Trial*. The jury couldn't reach a verdict. There simply wasn't enough evidence, which was rather the point.

**The stunts.** As a scheduled monument the Giant has the highest legal protection, which has not stopped him being co-opted for publicity. He has been given a giant mesh pair of jeans, a Borat mankini, a red nose, a football shirt, and, in 2017, the name "Theresa" pegged out along his phallus in painted hardboard. The National Trust, which foots the clean-up bill, is not amused, and points out that pegging boards into a scheduled monument is an offence.

## 22 — Chalk and Desire: the Long Man and the White Horse
*Long Man of Wilmington, East Sussex; Uffington White Horse, Oxfordshire, England · c. 1380–550 BC and c. 16th–17th century AD*

### BOOK VERSION

Two figures are cut into the chalk of southern England, and between them they contain the entire argument of this chapter. One is genuinely, provably ancient and has almost no erotic folklore attached to it at all. The other is early modern, a relative newcomer, and has attracted fertility legends, neo-pagan dawn rituals and a persistent rumour about a missing penis. Guess which one the public thinks is the older.

Begin on the steep north face of Windover Hill, above the village of Wilmington in East Sussex, where the Long Man stands 235 feet — around 72 metres — tall, a lean outline of a man holding a long staff in each raised hand. He is the tallest representation of the human form in Europe, and for a long time everyone assumed he was prehistoric: an Iron Age god, perhaps, or Neolithic, keeping company with the flint mines and long barrows nearby. The trouble is the paperwork. The earliest known depiction of the Long Man is a drawing made by the surveyor John Rowley in 1710 — a drawing not rediscovered until 1993 — and it shows something rather different from the crisp figure of today: a shadowy indentation in the grass, with facial features, and a head shaped like a helmet. There is no earlier record of him at all.

In 2003 the matter was settled, or as near settled as chalk ever gets. Professor Martin Bell of the University of Reading excavated the slope for Aubrey Manning's Open University series *Landscape Mysteries* and found that the hillside had been stable for a very long time until a sudden episode of instability — a wash of chalk rubble down the slope — in the sixteenth or seventeenth century, exactly what you would expect from the fresh cutting of a giant figure. A species of land snail in the relevant soil layer had not reached England until the sixteenth century. The Long Man, in other words, is early modern. Ronald Hutton, the Bristol historian who is the indispensable guide to all of this, observed that we could at least "celebrate the fact that we have our first, apparently unequivocally, Early Modern hill figure". He may be a Tudor or Stuart creation, possibly a Reformation religious image, possibly a piece of political satire in the manner now proposed for Cerne. Nobody knows. What he is not is ancient.

Two things follow, both instructive. The first is that the Long Man you see is barely a lifetime old in its physical fabric. Until the nineteenth century he was visible only in certain lights, or under a dusting of snow, which is why the locals sometimes called him the Green Man. In 1874 the Reverend W. de St Croix had his outline marked out in yellow bricks, and since 1969 the figure has been rendered in white-painted breeze blocks and lime mortar. You are looking at a concrete diagram of a Tudor shadow.

The second is the penis. There is a stubborn, much-loved belief that the Long Man was once ithyphallic — proudly endowed like his Dorset cousin — and that prudish Victorians unmanned him. It is a lovely story and there is no evidence for it whatsoever. The Sussex Archaeological Society, which has cared for the figure since the Duke of Devonshire gave it to them in 1925, states flatly that there is no archaeological or historical evidence that the Victorians robbed him of anything. The 1710 drawing shows no such feature. He probably never had one. That has not stopped modern hands: pranksters gave him a twenty-foot appendage at the 2010 summer solstice, and a television programme once added breasts and pigtails, to the fury of neo-Druids who regard the "ancient" figure as sacred.

And here is the delicious part. The Long Man has a thriving pagan ritual life, and it is younger than the Ford Cortina. The Long Man Morris Men dance at dawn at the foot of the figure every May morning — a genuinely lovely thing to witness, the sun coming up over the Weald, the bells and the hankies, the old man on the hill above. The side was formed in Eastbourne in 1978. The tradition they perpetuate is, at most, a little older than the average British marriage. This is no criticism of the dancers, who are keeping something warm and good alive. It is only that the "ancient rite" is a modern kindness, and the joke is entirely on the antiquarian who assumed otherwise. The Long Man stands, as it happens, within sight of the Downs above Charleston, where Bloomsbury painted and loved a few miles off; the same chalk landscape has served as a backdrop for both pagan revival and modern bohemia, and both were making it up as they went along, beautifully.

Now travel north-west to the Berkshire Downs — administratively Oxfordshire now — and everything inverts. On the flank of White Horse Hill above Uffington runs a horse, 110 metres of sleek, abstract, almost modern-looking line: a streaming back, disjointed legs, a beaked bird-like head. And this one is the real thing. In the 1990s Julie Rees-Jones and Mike Tite, at Oxford's Research Laboratory for Archaeology, used Optically Stimulated Luminescence on the soil in the lowest layers of the figure's trenches — among the first such dates ever obtained from a British archaeological site — and dated its creation to between about 1380 and 550 BC, the late Bronze Age or early Iron Age. Roughly three thousand years old. The oldest chalk hill figure in Britain by a distance, and one of only a handful anywhere that is genuinely prehistoric.

The Horse survived not by luck but by love. Left alone for a decade, it would vanish under the grass; it has lasted a hundred generations because people kept coming back to clean it. The scouring, held roughly every seven years, was the occasion for a great fair on the hill — cheese rolling down the dramatic dry valley called the Manger, wrestling, backsword play (in which you won by drawing an inch of blood from your opponent's scalp), a greased pole, and a very great deal of beer. Thomas Hughes, the Rugby-educated author of *Tom Brown's Schooldays*, attended the scouring of September 1857 and turned it into a book, *The Scouring of the White Horse* (1859), our best eyewitness account of the festive machinery that kept a Bronze Age drawing alive. Below the Horse sits the flat-topped mound of Dragon Hill, where — the story goes — St George killed the dragon, and where nothing grows on the bare patch because dragon's blood poisoned the ground.

Here is the point of putting the two together. The Uffington Horse is three thousand years old and has almost no erotic or fertility folklore worth the name — a sun-horse, a tribal emblem, a goddess perhaps, but not a bedroom aid. The Long Man is perhaps four hundred years old and has collected fertility rumours, a phantom penis and a dawn fertility dance. Age and sauce, it turns out, have nothing to do with each other. The customs gather where the customs gather, and the antiquarian who reads longevity into rudeness, or rudeness into longevity, has simply mistaken one for the other.

This one is the real thing. Cut into White Horse Hill above the village of Uffington, the White Horse is 110 metres of sleek, abstract line — a streaming back, spindly legs, a strange beaked head — and it is the oldest chalk hill figure in Britain by a wide margin. In the 1990s Julie Rees-Jones and Mike Tite, at Oxford's Research Laboratory for Archaeology, dated the soil in the lowest layers of its trenches by Optically Stimulated Luminescence — among the first such dates from any British site — and got a range of roughly 1380 to 550 BC: the late Bronze Age or early Iron Age. About three thousand years old. Where the Cerne Giant and the Long Man turned out to be young pretenders, the Horse is exactly as ancient as it looks, and then some.

It has survived because people kept scouring it. Left untouched for even a decade, the grass would swallow it. Roughly every seven years the local community re-cut and re-chalked the figure, and the work became the excuse for a riotous fair on the hill: cheese rolling down the steep dry valley called the Manger, wrestling, backsword play, a greased pole, and prodigious drinking. Thomas Hughes — of *Tom Brown's Schooldays* — went to the scouring of 1857 and wrote it up as *The Scouring of the White Horse* (1859), the best account we have of how a Bronze Age drawing was kept alive by festivity. The last great scouring-fair was that one; the custom guttered as Victorian England urbanised, and care passed eventually to the National Trust.

Below the Horse sits Dragon Hill, a natural knoll with an artificially flattened top, where legend says St George slew the dragon — the bare chalk patch is supposedly where its poisoned blood spilled. And here is the quiet lesson of the place: the Horse is genuinely, provably prehistoric, and yet it carries almost no fertility or erotic folklore at all. Age and sauce, it turns out, are unrelated. Best seen from the vale to the north, on a clear day, from a couple of miles off.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The name the locals used.** For most of his history the Long Man was barely there — a difference in the shade of the grass, or an outline picked out after a light fall of snow. Because he showed greenish against the hill, Sussex people often called him not the Long Man but the Green Man. It is a small detail that quietly demolishes the ancient-monument romance: for centuries he was less a carving than a trick of the light.

**Backsword and the bloody inch.** The Uffington scouring fair had a brutal centrepiece: backsword play, in which two men wrapped one arm and fought with wooden cudgels until one drew at least an inch of blood from the other's scalp. Thomas Hughes recorded it, along with cheese-rolling down the Manger and a race to catch a greased pig. This was the "fun" that kept a three-thousand-year-old monument scoured and visible. Preservation, in the Vale of the White Horse, was a contact sport.

**The horse that grazes at night.** Folklore holds that the Uffington Horse descends the hill at night to graze in the Manger, the dry valley below, and that on the day England is in peril it will rise and dance on Dragon Hill. Unlike the fertility tales pinned to younger figures, this is the kind of story that gathers naturally around something genuinely, unfathomably old — a way of explaining a shape nobody living remembered making.

## 23 — The Woman on the Wall at Kilpeck
*Kilpeck sheela-na-gig, Church of St Mary and St David · Kilpeck, Herefordshire, England · c. 1140*

### BOOK VERSION

About nine miles south-west of Hereford, in a village of a couple of hundred souls, stands what many people who know about these things consider the most perfect small Norman church in England. The Church of St Mary and St David at Kilpeck was built around 1140 of the local red sandstone, and it has survived, astonishingly, more or less as its builders left it — nave, chancel, apse, and a riot of carving by the group of twelfth-century masons known to art historians as the Herefordshire School. Around the outside, under the eaves, runs a corbel table: a row of some eighty-five little carved brackets, each one worth stopping for. There are musicians and wrestlers, a dog and a rabbit, a pig, dragons eating their own tails, and assorted grotesques. And, on the south-west, there is a naked woman holding her vulva open with both hands.

She is a sheela-na-gig, and there is no delicate way to describe her, so let us not try. She squats, she stares out with a large round head, and she pulls apart an exaggerated, gaping vulva — plainly, deliberately, unmistakably. The Kilpeck figure is the best-known and most-photographed sheela in Britain, partly because she is so well preserved and partly because, unlike most, she is not a stone imported from some older building but an original, integral part of the 1140s church. Somebody who built one of England's loveliest churches decided, as part of the design, to put an explicit carving of female genitalia on the outside wall, and nobody has ever been entirely sure why.

What is a sheela-na-gig? The name is Irish, of uncertain meaning and first written down in the 1840s, applied to a class of carved figures — naked women displaying an outsized vulva — found across the British Isles and, crucially, across France and Spain too. Counting them is a fool's errand, because scholars disagree about what qualifies, but the researchers Joanne McMahon and Jack Roberts tallied 124 in Ireland and 45 in Britain, with more turning up as people look. Ireland has by far the greatest concentration, which is one reason the interpretations differ so violently.

And the interpretations are the whole battlefield. There are, roughly, four. The first, and by far the most popular, is that she is a survival of a pre-Christian fertility cult — a pagan mother-goddess, the Cailleach, smuggled onto the church wall by masons who still half-believed in her, or tolerated by a Church willing to let the old goddess sit quietly among the saints. This was the reading championed in the 1930s by Margaret Murray, the same Egyptologist who did so much to convince the twentieth century that the witch trials had persecuted a surviving pagan religion. The second reading is the opposite: she is a Christian warning against the sin of lust, *luxuria*, her ugliness and exposure designed to repel rather than entice, a sermon in stone for a congregation that could not read. The third is that she is apotropaic — a charm against evil, genitalia exposed to frighten off the devil, in the same family as the gargoyle and the grotesque. And the fourth, the one that has quietly won most of the academic argument, is that she is not a survival of anything: she is a Romanesque import, part of a new fashion in church decoration that travelled up from the pilgrimage churches of France and Spain in the eleventh and twelfth centuries and arrived in Britain and Ireland with the Normans.

This last case is worth stating because it is the least romantic and the best supported. Jørgen Andersen, in *The Witch on the Wall* (1977), first traced the figure to the Continent; Anthony Weir and James Jerman, in *Images of Lust* (1986), followed the motif along the pilgrimage routes and argued the warning-against-lust reading. The clinching detail belongs to Eamonn Kelly of the National Museum of Ireland, who pointed out that almost every sheela still in its original place in Ireland stands in an area of Anglo-Norman conquest, and hardly any in the areas that stayed under native Irish control. If the sheela were a survival of native Irish paganism, you would expect the reverse. As Ronald Hutton puts it, the pagan-survival reading — the popular one — is precisely the one the evidence least supports. The woman at Kilpeck sits on a Christian church, built by a Norman lord, carved in a French-influenced style, around 1140. She is medieval to her bones.

None of which makes her less extraordinary, and none of which has dented her modern life. She has been claimed by the women's movement as an image of female power and sacred sexuality, adopted by neo-pagans as a goddess of the crone-cycle, and set to music: the English musician PJ Harvey called a fierce 1992 song after her, which introduced the word "sheela-na-gig" to a great many people who had never seen a corbel table. The lyrics need not detain us; the point is only that a twelfth-century Herefordshire carving found a second career on the indie charts, which is not a sentence one gets to write often.

There is a darker footnote, and it concerns her neighbours. Kilpeck kept its carvings when so many churches lost theirs, but not quite all of them. The folklorist Roy Palmer, in *Herefordshire Folklore* (2002), recorded the recollection of an old man who claimed to have been present, in the 1920s, at the destruction of a number of "obscene" figures at the church, on the orders of an offended lady of the parish — "I never could suffer that un," he remembered, "so I got her a pole and pothered un off." Palmer's careful guess is that the destroyed corbels were male exhibitionist figures, possibly aroused ones, which the Victorian and Edwardian sensibility found intolerable in a way it apparently did not find the sheela — perhaps because the men were unambiguous, and nobody was quite sure what the woman was. It is a grimly funny thought: she may have survived precisely because the person with the pole did not understand what she was looking at. The artist George Lewis, who drew the church in 1842, described her not as a sheela at all but as "a fool" opening his heart to the devil. Sometimes the best protection is to be misread.

The church is Grade I listed, still in use, and open to anyone. Go and look. She has been holding herself open on that wall for nearly nine hundred years, outlasting the men who offended the lady with the pole, outlasting the antiquarians who called her a goddess and the ones who called her a warning, and she will very likely outlast this book.

### WEB VERSION — kilpeck-sheela-na-gig

The Church of St Mary and St David at Kilpeck, built around 1140, is often called the most perfect small Norman church in England — and around its outside runs a corbel table of some eighty-five carved brackets by the twelfth-century Herefordshire School of masons. Among the musicians, wrestlers, dogs and dragons sits a naked woman holding open an exaggerated vulva with both hands. She is a sheela-na-gig, the best-preserved and most famous in Britain, and — unlike most, which are older stones reset into later walls — she is an original part of the 1140s design. Somebody built one of England's loveliest churches and chose to include explicit female genitalia on the wall.

Why? Four theories compete. The popular one is that she's a survival of a pagan fertility goddess, smuggled onto the church — a reading pushed in the 1930s by Margaret Murray. The others: a Christian warning against the sin of lust; an apotropaic charm to frighten off evil; or, best supported of all, a Romanesque import, part of a decorative fashion that travelled from the pilgrimage churches of France and Spain with the Normans. Jørgen Andersen (1977) and then Weir and Jerman (1986) built that case; Eamonn Kelly of the National Museum of Ireland clinched it by noting that Ireland's in-situ sheelas cluster in areas of Anglo-Norman conquest, not native Irish ones. As Ronald Hutton observes, the pagan reading is the popular one and the least supported. She sits on a Christian church, built by a Norman lord, carved in a French-influenced style, around 1140.

Her neighbours were less lucky. The folklorist Roy Palmer recorded an old man's memory of "obscene" corbels — probably male figures — being knocked off in the 1920s by an offended parishioner with a pole. The sheela may have survived because nobody was sure what she was: in 1842 George Lewis catalogued her as "a fool" opening his heart to the devil. She's had a modern afterlife too, lending her name to a 1992 PJ Harvey song. The church is Grade I listed and open to all.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The man with the pole.** Kilpeck kept most of its carvings, but not all. In *Herefordshire Folklore* (2002), Roy Palmer recorded an old man recalling the destruction of several "obscene" corbels in the 1920s, ordered by an offended local woman: "I never could suffer that un, so I got her a pole and pothered un off." Palmer suspected the lost figures were aroused male exhibitionists — unambiguous in a way the puzzling sheela was not.

**The whitewashed frescoes.** The artist George Lewis visited Kilpeck in 1818 and saw wall paintings and possible statuary inside the church. When he returned to make his careful survey, published in 1842, they had been whitewashed over — to his open disgust. It is a reminder that "perfectly preserved" Kilpeck lost things too; what survives on the outside walls partly survives because stone outlasts the reforming impulse better than paint.

**Counting the uncountable.** Nobody agrees how many sheela-na-gigs exist, because nobody quite agrees what one is. McMahon and Roberts counted 124 in Ireland and 45 in Britain; other scholars add or subtract worn and doubtful figures. Ireland has the lion's share — which, since almost all its in-situ examples sit in zones of Anglo-Norman conquest, is itself an argument that the motif arrived with the Normans rather than surviving from a Celtic past.

## 24 — Stones That Grant Children
*Mên-an-Tol, Cornwall; Rudston Monolith, East Yorkshire; the Giant's Ring, County Down; Castlerigg, Cumbria · Neolithic and Bronze Age to present*

### BOOK VERSION

The wish to bargain with a stone for a child is very old and very human, and you find it wherever there are large old stones and people who want babies. What you do not always find — indeed, what you almost never find intact — is the actual ancient rite. Again and again the specific practice turns out to be younger than advertised, or attached to a monument that has been shuffled about since the folklore first fastened on it. Four stones make the case.

Begin in the far west of Cornwall, on the moor between Madron and Morvah, at the Mên-an-Tol — the name means, straightforwardly, "the holed stone". It is a strange and lovely little monument: a round granite stone with a clean circular hole bored through its middle, flanked by two uprights, so that from the right angle the three stones read as the numerals "101". It is also known as the Crick Stone, and its folklore is medical before it is sexual. For generations, children suffering from rickets or scrofula were passed naked through the hole — three times, or nine, and against the sun — to be cured. That much is well documented. The fertility version — that a woman who passes through the hole seven times backwards at the full moon will conceive — is later and more thinly evidenced, the sort of claim that accretes onto a suggestive shape.

And the shape may not even be the original one. In 1993 the archaeologist Ann Preston-Jones published a reassessment of the site in *Cornish Archaeology*, following survey work by the Cornwall Archaeological Unit, and concluded that the stones have been rearranged — probably in the early nineteenth century — and that the monument may originally have been part of a stone circle, with the holed stone standing elsewhere within it. The westernmost stone was moved into line with the others some time after 1815. So the monument we crawl through and photograph is, in its present arrangement, a couple of centuries old at most. The hole is genuinely ancient; the tidy little "101" through which the folklore supposedly flowed is a Georgian rearrangement. The wish is old. The stage set is recent.

Travel to the other end of England, to the village of Rudston in the East Riding of Yorkshire, and find the second stone standing — improbably — in a Christian churchyard. The Rudston Monolith is the tallest standing stone in Britain: about 7.6 metres, some 25 feet, of gritstone, weighing perhaps forty tonnes, dragged or carried by ice from at least ten miles away and raised in the late Neolithic or early Bronze Age. The eighteenth-century antiquarian Sir William Strickland is said to have dug around its base and concluded that there was as much stone again below ground as above. The church of All Saints was planted right beside it — almost certainly a deliberate Christian claim on a site already sacred, the same instinct that built churches on so many old stones — and the very name of the village records the takeover: Rudston, from the Old English *rood-stane*, the cross-stone, a pagan monolith rebranded with a crucifix on top. The fertility and pagan-worship claims made for it are the usual antiquarian speculation; what is solid is the stone itself, its Neolithic date, and the eloquent fact of a church built to stand next to it and, in a sense, to tame it.

Cross the Irish Sea to County Down, a few miles south of Belfast, for the third: the Giant's Ring at Ballynahatty, a colossal Neolithic henge some 180 metres across, its bank still standing four metres high, with a small dolmen — a passage tomb — at its centre. It was built around 2700 BC, before the pyramids, and it is genuinely a place of the dead: excavation nearby by Barrie Hartwell of Queen's University Belfast in the early 1990s revealed timber enclosures that seem to have been part of the funerary ritual, where bodies may have been left to decay before burial. The antiquarian imagination, naturally, reached for druids and sun-worship and fertility. But the Giant's Ring offers a wonderful corrective to all that solemnity, because we know exactly what the enclosure was used for in the eighteenth century, and it was not worship. It was horse-racing. The great bank made a natural grandstand, and the horses ran six circuits to the heat, which the locals reckoned made two miles. A five-thousand-year-old temple of the ancestors, repurposed as a racecourse, with the mourners' amphitheatre turned over to punters. There is, tellingly, almost no genuine old folklore attached to the Ring at all — no fertility rite, no druidic legend that can be traced back — which is itself instructive. Sometimes the stones stay silent, and honest.

Which brings us to the fourth stone, and the best debunk of the lot: Castlerigg, in Cumbria, on its hilltop platform east of Keswick with the Lakeland fells ringed around it like a stone audience. This one is the real thing — a genuine Neolithic circle of some thirty-eight stones, built around 3000 BC, one of the earliest in Britain. And it is here that we can catch the romantic imagination red-handed. English Heritage, which cares for the site, records that thirteen of the stones were re-erected or shifted between about 1794 and 1848, that these adjustments conveniently created an alignment towards the midwinter sunset, and that a stand of larch trees — a fake "druidical grove" — was planted at the centre to improve the atmosphere. This was not vandalism; it was set-dressing, done in the great age of Lakeland tourism to make the circle feel more primevally mystical than it already did. And we have a witness. When Samuel Taylor Coleridge climbed up to the circle in 1799, in the company of Wordsworth, he noted in his characteristic way that "the Keswickians have been playing Tricks with the stones." The Druid associations, the fertility mysticism, the solstice alignment — Romantic and Victorian invention, laid over a genuinely ancient monument by people who found the truth insufficiently picturesque. Coleridge, who knew the Lake District intimately — he would settle at Greta Hall in Keswick, in the same landscape that gave us the Maid of Buttermere and the appalling Wicked Jimmy Lowther — saw the trick being played and wrote it down.

Bind the four together and the pattern is plain. At Mên-an-Tol the stones were shuffled; at Rudston the Church built alongside to claim the ground; at the Giant's Ring the temple became a racetrack; at Castlerigg the Georgians moved the stones and planted a grove and invented the druids. The human wish to pass a sick child through a hole, or to ask a great stone for a baby, is old and real and worth honouring. Almost every specific "ancient rite" attached to these particular stones is not. The impulse is genuine. The tradition is usually about as old as your grandmother, and sometimes a good deal younger.

### WEB VERSION — men-an-tol

On the moor between Madron and Morvah in far-west Cornwall stands the Mên-an-Tol — "the holed stone" — a round granite slab with a clean circular hole through it, flanked by two uprights so that from one angle the three read as "101". Its oldest folklore is medical: for generations children with rickets or scrofula were passed naked through the hole, three or nine times and against the sun, to be cured. That earned it its other name, the Crick Stone. The fertility legend — that a woman passing backwards through the hole seven times at the full moon will conceive — is later and much more thinly evidenced, the kind of story that gathers around a suggestive shape.

And the shape itself may be a rearrangement. In 1993 the archaeologist Ann Preston-Jones, writing in *Cornish Archaeology* after survey work by the Cornwall Archaeological Unit, concluded that the stones have been moved — probably in the early nineteenth century — and that the monument may once have formed part of a stone circle, with the holed stone standing elsewhere within it. The westernmost stone was brought into line only after 1815. So the neat little "101" we photograph is, in its present form, barely two centuries old. The hole is genuinely prehistoric; the arrangement is Georgian.

That gap — between an ancient object and a recent ritual — is the whole point. The wish to bargain with a stone for a healthy child is old and real and found wherever there are big old stones. The specific rite, and even the specific set-up of the stones, usually turns out to be far younger than the folklore claims. Mên-an-Tol is Bronze Age in origin but early-modern in staging, a monument reassembled by the same Romantic era that loved to imagine druids everywhere. Visitors still pose with their heads through the hole. It sits on a moorland footpath off the Madron–Morvah road; wear boots, and don't expect to conceive by moonlight.

### WEB VERSION — rudston-monolith

In the churchyard of All Saints at Rudston, in the East Riding of Yorkshire, stands the tallest standing stone in Britain: a slab of gritstone about 7.6 metres — 25 feet — high, weighing perhaps forty tonnes, and quite possibly extending as far below ground as above. It was raised in the late Neolithic or early Bronze Age, and the nearest source for its stone lies at least ten miles away, so it was either dragged there by human effort or carried by glacial ice. Either way it has stood on this spot for some four thousand years — far longer than any church.

And that is the striking thing: the church was built beside it, not the other way round. The Norman church of All Saints was almost certainly planted here on purpose, to claim a site already held sacred — the same instinct that set churches on top of so many old pagan places. The village name records the conquest: Rudston comes from the Old English *rood-stane*, meaning "cross-stone", implying a venerated standing stone that was topped or marked with a cross and absorbed into Christian use. The eighteenth-century antiquarian Sir William Strickland is said to have excavated the base and found as much stone below ground as above.

The fertility and sun-worship stories told about the monolith are the familiar antiquarian speculation, and little supports them beyond the suggestive fact of a giant stone in a holy place. What is solid is the stone itself and the drama of the takeover: a prehistoric monument so imposing that the medieval Church chose not to topple it but to build alongside and rename the whole parish after it. Three cursus monuments — long prehistoric earthwork avenues — appear to converge on the site, suggesting it was a major focus of ceremony thousands of years before the first Christians arrived. The stone was capped with lead in 1773 to slow its weathering, and is capped still. The churchyard is open; the stone dwarfs the church, as it was always going to.

### WEB VERSION — giants-ring-ballynahatty

A few miles south of Belfast, at Ballynahatty in County Down, lies one of the great Neolithic monuments of Ireland: the Giant's Ring, a henge some 180 metres across, ringed by an earthwork bank still standing around four metres high, with a small dolmen — a passage tomb — at its centre. It was built around 2700 BC, before the Egyptian pyramids, and it was a place of the dead. In the early 1990s, Barrie Hartwell of Queen's University Belfast excavated timber enclosures just outside the henge and interpreted them as part of the funerary ritual — places where bodies may have been left to decay before entombment.

The antiquarian imagination did with the Ring what it did everywhere: it reached for druids, sun-worship and fertility rites. But the Giant's Ring offers a bracing correction, because we know precisely what the great enclosure was used for in the eighteenth century, and it was not solemn. It was horse-racing. The huge circular bank made a ready-made grandstand, and contemporaries recorded that the horses ran six circuits to the heat, reckoned at two miles. A five-thousand-year-old temple of the ancestors, in other words, spent the Georgian era as a racecourse, with holidaymakers from Belfast lining the bank where mourners had once stood.

What the Ring notably lacks is much genuine old folklore at all — no traceable fertility rite, no deep druidic legend, just the documented racing and the honest silence of a monument whose original meaning is lost. That silence is worth respecting. The archaeology tells us this was a ceremonial and funerary landscape of real importance — the "Ballynahatty woman", a Neolithic skeleton found nearby, has even yielded ancient DNA — but it does not tell us the enclosure was a fertility temple, and no reliable tradition says so either. It is a State Care historic monument now, freely open, grazed by sheep and walked by dogs, its bank as good a viewpoint as it was a grandstand.

### WEB VERSION — castlerigg-stone-circle

On a hilltop platform east of Keswick, ringed by the Lakeland fells, Castlerigg is perhaps the most beautifully sited stone circle in Britain — and the best place in this book to catch the Romantic imagination red-handed. The circle itself is genuine: some thirty-eight stones, raised around 3000 BC, one of the earliest stone circles in Britain. But much of what makes it feel primevally mystical was arranged by people who found the real thing insufficiently atmospheric.

English Heritage, which looks after the site, records that thirteen of the stones were re-erected or shifted between about 1794 and 1848; that these adjustments conveniently produced an alignment towards the midwinter sunset; and that a stand of larch trees — a fake "druidical grove" — was planted at the centre to improve the mood. This was the great age of Lakeland tourism, when poets and painters were selling the Lakes as an untamed, ancient landscape, and the stones were quietly edited to match the brochure. We even have an eyewitness: when Samuel Taylor Coleridge visited in 1799 with Wordsworth, he noted that "the Keswickians have been playing Tricks with the stones."

So the druids, the fertility mysticism, the solstice worship — all of it is Romantic and Victorian invention, laid over a genuinely Neolithic monument. The stones are real and ancient; the "ancient rites" attached to them are barely two centuries old, and some were literally staged. This is the chapter's argument in its purest form: the monument deserves awe, and gets it; the mythology deserves scepticism, and rarely gets that. Coleridge, who later settled nearby at Greta Hall in Keswick, saw the tricks being played and wrote them down — the same Lakeland that gave us the Maid of Buttermere and Wicked Jimmy Lowther. Castlerigg was taken into state care in 1883, among the first monuments in Britain so protected. It stands on National Trust land, is managed by English Heritage, and is free to visit in daylight — a five-thousand-year-old monument with better opening hours than most museums.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The brass pins of Mên-an-Tol.** Beyond curing and conceiving, the holed stone was used for divination. Two brass pins were laid crosswise on top of the stone, and a question put; the pins were said to move of their own accord in answer. It is a reminder that these monuments were all-purpose folk technology — a stone could heal your child, foretell your future and, later, grant you a baby, depending on what the century needed from it.

**Coleridge's tricks.** The line is worth having in full context. In his notebook in 1799 Coleridge, fresh from the circle with Wordsworth, recorded that the Keswick locals had "been playing Tricks with the stones." He meant the re-erections and the planted grove. It is one of the earliest documented complaints, by a first-rate witness, that a British "ancient" site was being tidied up to suit Romantic taste — the debunk, delivered in real time, by a poet who loved the place.

**The counting curse of Castlerigg.** Like many circles, Castlerigg carries the folk belief that its stones cannot be counted twice to the same total — try it, and you will get a different number each time. The prosaic truth is that observers genuinely disagree about which lumps count as stones, so totals really do vary between thirty-eight and forty-odd. A rare case of a "magical" property that turns out to be plainly, boringly true.

## 25 — Rites Still Danced
*Abbots Bromley Horn Dance, Staffordshire; Padstow 'Obby 'Oss, Cornwall; Beltane Fire Festival, Edinburgh · AD 1065 antlers to a 1988 revival*

### BOOK VERSION

Some of the customs in this chapter are dead, recorded and gone. Three of them are emphatically alive, danced every year by people who will happily buy you a drink afterwards, and they make the best possible ending, because they show the whole tangle at its most enjoyable: genuinely old things wrapped in genuinely recent meanings, and nobody the poorer for it.

Start in Staffordshire, at Abbots Bromley, on the Monday after the first Sunday after the fourth of September — Wakes Monday — when the Horn Dance is performed. Twelve people process ten or twelve miles around the parish all day: six dancers carrying sets of reindeer antlers mounted on carved wooden heads, three painted white and three a dark blue-brown, plus a Fool, a hobby horse, a Maid Marian (traditionally a man in a woman's dress), a boy with a bow and arrow, a triangle player and a musician. They fetch the horns from St Nicholas's church first thing, dance them round the parish, and return them at dusk. And the extraordinary thing — the thing that makes this the strangest fact in the whole chapter — is the antlers. In 1976 a splinter from one of them was radiocarbon-dated, and it came back at around AD 1065, give or take eighty years. Reindeer had been extinct in Britain for centuries by then. Which means the antlers were brought here, most likely from Scandinavia, nearly a thousand years ago, and have been kept and danced in this one village ever since. That part is real, and astonishing.

The dance itself, though, is a subtler case. The earliest written record of it is Robert Plot's *The Natural History of Staffordshire* of 1686, and Plot — writing beautifully — calls it a "Hobby-horse dance", performed "within memory" at Christmas and New Year, in which "6 others, carrying on their shoulders as many Rain deers heads" danced the hays and country dances while a man worked a hobby-horse with a snapping bow. An earlier mention of a hobby horse at Abbots Bromley in 1532 makes no mention of horns at all. So the custom is genuinely old — comfortably medieval in its bones, with millennium-old antlers — but the antlers may have joined a pre-existing hobby-horse dance at some point, and the confident modern reading of the whole thing as a pagan fertility rite is exactly that: a modern reading. There is, as the folklorists honestly admit, no evidence for it. Even the "medieval" costumes are Victorian; the current designs replaced earlier ones, and the splendid outfits worn today were made around the turn of the millennium. What you are watching is authentically strange and genuinely continuous. It is just not a fertility rite, whatever the guidebooks tell you.

Now to Cornwall, and the loudest, wildest, most physical of the three: the Padstow 'Obby 'Oss on May Day. It begins at midnight on the thirtieth of April, when the town gathers outside the Golden Lion to sing the Night Song, and by morning Padstow is dressed in greenery and flowers around its maypole. Then the 'Osses come out — there are two, the Old (or Red) 'Oss and the Blue Ribbon (or "Peace") 'Oss, the latter introduced in the nineteenth century — each a man inside a black hooped frame six feet across, hung with a skirt of black tarpaulin and topped with a snapping, masked head. Each 'Oss is goaded through the streets by a capering Teaser with a padded club, followed by the Mayers in white with red or blue sashes, the accordions and the drums, and the whole town singing. And every so often the 'Oss lunges and catches a young woman under its black frame. The documented belief is that a woman caught under the 'Oss will be married or pregnant within the year; in the older, rougher version, there was tar or blacking under there, and she came out marked. It is the frankest surviving fertility gesture in the English calendar, and it is thrilling to watch.

Here is where Ronald Hutton earns his place as the presiding spirit of this chapter. The 'Obby 'Oss feels immemorial, and Padstonians will tell you it is thousands of years old. But the first written record of it is 1803 — an account by the antiquarian Richard Polwhele of a man dressed in a horse's skin, led through the streets, ducking his head in every dirty pool and throwing the water over the crowd. There is no proof it is older than the eighteenth century. The reading of it as an ancient pagan fertility rite was not handed down by the townspeople at all; it was fixed onto the custom by outside folklorists in the early twentieth century — first Francis Etherington in 1907, then, decisively, Thurstan Peter in 1913, who, marinated in Frazer's *The Golden Bough*, argued that the 'Oss "might have once been" a pre-Christian fertility ritual. That reading then percolated back into Padstow itself, so thoroughly that when Hutton visited in 1985 he found the locals confidently describing their own custom to him in Frazerian terms the Frazerians had supplied. It is a perfect little loop: the experts told the town its tradition was ancient and pagan, and the town believed them, and now tells the experts. None of which, and this matters, makes the 'Obby 'Oss any less real, any less moving, or any less Padstow's own. Hutton himself calls it one of the most dramatic folk customs of modern Britain and "a tremendous reaffirmation of communal pride and solidarity". The custom is authentic. The pagan pedigree is imported. Both things are true, and the town dances anyway.

Which leaves the last fire, and the honest one. Every year on the evening of the thirtieth of April, thousands of people climb Calton Hill in the middle of Edinburgh for the Beltane Fire Festival: a torchlit procession led by the May Queen and the Green Man, drummers, acrobats, fire-sculptures, semi-nude and body-painted performers in red and white, and a bonfire, all telling the story of the death of winter and the rebirth of summer. It looks, and feels, like something dragged up from the Iron Age Celtic dark. It is not. It was started in 1988 — within living memory, more recent than the compact disc — by a small group including Angus Farquhar of the industrial music collective Test Dept, with academic support from folklorists at the University of Edinburgh's School of Scottish Studies. The first one drew a few hundred; from that core of a dozen performers it had grown, by 1999, to several hundred performers and over ten thousand spectators, and today it draws crowds of many thousands (around 6,800 attended in 2025), run by the Beltane Fire Society, a registered charity of elected volunteers. They chose Calton Hill partly to reclaim a space that then had a reputation for drugs and anonymous sex, and turn it back over to the community.

And here is why Beltane is the right note to end on. The organisers are completely honest about what it is. They do not claim an unbroken line back to the druids. They say plainly that it is a modern celebration, a deliberate reinvention, drawing on old material to make something new and meaningful now. It is an invented tradition that is truthful about being invented — and it is, by any measure, a real one, freighted with genuine feeling, welcomed into thousands of lives. Which turns the chapter's whole argument on its head at the last minute, and gently. The Victorian antiquarian took a young custom and lied that it was ancient. The Edinburgh Beltane took a young custom and told the truth — that it is ours, and now, and made on purpose — and it works just as well. Perhaps better. The old gods, it turns out, do not mind being freshly invented, so long as the dancing is good and nobody pretends otherwise.

### WEB VERSION — abbots-bromley-horn-dance

Every year on Wakes Monday — the Monday after the first Sunday after 4 September — the village of Abbots Bromley in Staffordshire performs the Horn Dance. A team of twelve processes ten or twelve miles around the parish all day: six dancers carrying sets of reindeer antlers on carved wooden heads (three painted white, three blue-brown), plus a Fool, a hobby horse, a Maid Marian played by a man, a boy with a bow, a triangle player and a musician. They collect the horns from St Nicholas's church at dawn and return them at dusk.

The astonishing part is the antlers. In 1976 a fragment of one was radiocarbon-dated to around AD 1065, give or take eighty years — by which time reindeer had long been extinct in Britain. So the horns were imported, most likely from Scandinavia, nearly a thousand years ago, and have been kept and danced in this one village ever since. That much is genuinely extraordinary and genuinely old.

The dance itself is a subtler story. Its first written record is Robert Plot's *The Natural History of Staffordshire* (1686), which describes a "Hobby-horse dance" with six men carrying reindeer heads. An earlier note of a hobby horse here, in 1532, mentions no horns at all — so the antlers may have joined an existing dance later. The popular reading of the whole thing as a pagan fertility rite is a modern overlay; folklorists candidly admit there's no evidence for it. Even the "medieval" costumes are Victorian inventions, remade around the year 2000.

What you are watching, then, is authentically strange and genuinely continuous — a village that has danced thousand-year-old antlers through its streets for centuries — without being the pagan fertility ceremony the guidebooks promise. The antlers are legally the property of the parish council and hang in St Nicholas's church for the other 364 days of the year. Come early: the dance starts at the church not long after dawn.

### WEB VERSION — padstow-obby-oss

Padstow's 'Obby 'Oss is the loudest, wildest May Day custom in England. It begins at midnight on 30 April, when the Cornish town gathers outside the Golden Lion to sing the Night Song; by morning the streets are dressed in greenery around the maypole. Then the two 'Osses appear — the Old (Red) 'Oss and the Blue Ribbon 'Oss — each a man inside a six-foot black hooped frame hung with tarpaulin, topped with a snapping masked head, and goaded through the town by a club-wielding Teaser. Accordions, drums and the whole town singing follow behind. Every so often the 'Oss lunges and traps a young woman under its black skirt: the belief is that she'll be married or pregnant within the year, and in the older version there was tar or blacking under there to mark her.

It feels thousands of years old. But — as Ronald Hutton has shown — the first written record is only 1803, an account by Richard Polwhele of a man in a horse's skin dipping his head in puddles and dousing the crowd. There's no proof it predates the eighteenth century. And the famous "ancient pagan fertility rite" reading wasn't handed down by Padstonians at all: it was pinned on by outside folklorists — Francis Etherington in 1907, then Thurstan Peter in 1913, steeped in Frazer's *The Golden Bough*. That interpretation then seeped back into the town, so completely that when Hutton visited in 1985 the locals described their own custom to him in exactly those imported terms.

None of which makes it any less real or less Padstow's own. Hutton calls it one of the most dramatic folk customs of modern Britain and a tremendous reaffirmation of communal pride. The custom is authentic; the pagan pedigree is borrowed; the town dances regardless, and it is unforgettable. Expect thousands of people packed into narrow streets — arrive early, wear red or blue, and sing.

### WEB VERSION — beltane-calton-hill

Every 30 April, thousands of people climb Calton Hill in the centre of Edinburgh for the Beltane Fire Festival: a torchlit procession led by the May Queen and the Green Man, with drummers, acrobats, fire sculptures, body-painted performers in red and white, and a great bonfire, all staging the death of winter and the birth of summer. It looks like something hauled up from the Iron Age. It isn't — and that is exactly its charm.

The festival was started in 1988, within living memory, by a small group including Angus Farquhar of the industrial band Test Dept, with academic support from folklorists at the University of Edinburgh's School of Scottish Studies. It grew from a core of a dozen performers and a few hundred spectators to several hundred performers and over ten thousand spectators by 1999; around 6,800 attended in 2025. It is staged by the Beltane Fire Society, a charity run by elected volunteers. They chose Calton Hill deliberately, in part to reclaim a place then known for drugs and anonymous sex and give it back to the community.

What makes Beltane the perfect end to any tour of Britain's "ancient" rites is its honesty. The organisers make no claim to an unbroken line back to the druids. They say openly that it is a modern celebration — a deliberate reinvention that draws on old Gaelic material to make something meaningful now. It is an invented tradition that admits it is invented, and it has become, by any measure, a real one: genuinely moving, genuinely communal, woven into thousands of lives in under forty years. Where the Victorian antiquarian took a young custom and lied that it was old, Edinburgh took a young custom and told the truth about it — and it works just as well, perhaps better. Tickets are required and sell out; dress warm, and prepare to stay up past midnight.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**Plot's "within memory".** When Robert Plot described the Horn Dance in 1686, he wrote that it had been performed "within memory" — a phrase that has puzzled everyone since, because it implies the custom had lapsed and been revived at least once even then. The likeliest explanation is the Puritan Commonwealth after the Civil War, which banned dancing and music. Even Britain's most "unbroken" folk custom has, it seems, been broken and mended before.

**The horns that never leave.** The Abbots Bromley antlers are so precious that they never leave the parish except for the dance itself. When the dancers are invited to perform elsewhere — as they often are — they carry a replica set of red-deer antlers instead. The thousand-year-old reindeer horns spend the rest of the year hanging in St Nicholas's church, legally the property of the parish council since 1981.

**Aunt Ursula Birdhood's ewe.** The Padstow May Song is full of gloriously obscure figures — chief among them "Aunt Ursula Birdhood" and her old ewe, embedded in verses nobody can now fully explain. The song also carries a genuinely mournful note, listing the young men "some in England, some in France" who should be dancing and are not — a First World War wound folded quietly into a custom the folklorists insisted was about Bronze Age fertility.
