# BATCH 14 — Connective Matter (complete: Topics 66–70)

## 66 — Introduction

### BOOK VERSION

Almost everything we know about what the British got up to in private, we know because somebody was trying to stop them.

Consider how the evidence reaches us. We can describe the inside of a Georgian molly house in Field Lane, Holborn — the fire, the drink, the men dancing and mimicking women's voices, the back room they called the marrying room — because a constable named Samuel Stevens infiltrated the place in 1725 and swore to what he had seen, and because the Old Bailey printed his deposition before three of the men he described were hanged at Tyburn. We can reconstruct a night at the Caravan Club in Covent Garden, a bohemian basement that stayed open for six weeks in the summer of 1934, because the Metropolitan Police watched it from an empty theatre across the road, logged who came and went, seized the membership cards, and photographed the draped interior on the night they closed it down. The fullest surviving record of interwar queer London is a prosecution file.

There is one great exception, and it is instructive. Anne Lister of Shibden Hall wrote roughly five million words of diary, and encrypted about a sixth of them in a code of her own devising, and in that code she recorded her lovers, her techniques and her own frank vocabulary for both. She is the rare case of a person in these pages who documented her desires herself, on purpose, in the reasonable expectation that nobody would ever break the cipher. When someone finally did, in the 1890s, his first instinct was to advise burning the lot.

That is the shape of the problem, and it is also the shape of this book. The erotic history of Britain survives in two forms: what people hid well enough to keep, and what the authorities wrote down while trying to destroy it. Everything else has gone — and it went, most often, at the hands of somebody who loved the dead and thought they were being kind.

What that surviving record shows, once you have enough of it laid out on a map, is a single continuing argument. Britain has spent something over a thousand years building rules about who may desire whom, where, at what price, and in what light. It has spent exactly the same thousand years getting round them. The rules are worth studying because they tell you what each age was frightened of. The evasions are worth studying because they tell you what people actually wanted, which is rarely the same thing and is almost always more interesting.

The oldest evasion is geographical, and it is beautifully simple: go somewhere the rule does not reach. The licensed brothels of medieval Bankside sat on the south bank of the Thames because the City's writ stopped at the water, and because the manor on the far side answered to the Bishop of Winchester, who was content to license, regulate and tax what London forbade. The women who worked there were nicknamed Winchester Geese after their landlord. Six centuries later, couples thwarted by Lord Hardwicke's Marriage Act of 1754 discovered the same principle and drove north for a village on the far side of a small stream, where a different law ran. Vauxhall's pleasure gardens were across the river; Soho and Covent Garden sat outside the old City; Shepherd Market flourished in the richest square mile in Britain precisely because nobody in Mayfair wished to make a scene. Britain's erotic geography is, to a striking degree, a map of jurisdictions and the gaps between them.

The second force is class, and it runs through this book like a seam of coal. For the better part of four decades the Georgian state tolerated an annual directory of Covent Garden prostitutes — names, addresses, prices and specialities — selling thousands of copies at half a crown. When the Proclamation Society finally prosecuted its publishers in 1794, James Roach's defence was that nobody had ever been prosecuted for publishing it before. He was fined £100 and sent to Newgate for a year, and the List ended. In the following century, historians argue, prosecution intensified as pornography became cheap enough to reach working-class and youthful readers — an interpretation consistent with Lord Campbell's stated concern for the morals of youth, though not a causal claim he made himself. A century later, prosecuting Penguin Books over Lady Chatterley's Lover, counsel for the Crown asked a jury whether this was a book they would wish his wife or his servants to read — and lost the case in a single sentence, because he had said the quiet part aloud. When a male brothel was uncovered in Cleveland Street in 1889, the working-class telegraph boys were named, dismissed from the Post Office and pressed to emigrate; the aristocratic clients were allowed to reach France. British law has been remarkably relaxed about the pleasures of people it trusted, and remarkably vigilant about everyone else.

Third, and inseparable from class, is commerce. Somebody was always selling. A shilling bought you into Vauxhall, and what the shilling really bought was the right to walk the same gravel as a duchess, in a landscape engineered so that a great deal might happen and nothing need be admitted. The same instinct built the Georgian bagnio behind a respectable frontage, put a card reading "Model" beside a Mayfair doorbell, and, in 1981, moved the whole trade into the front rooms of suburban Britain when a twenty-one-year-old realised that the customer for sex toys had been a woman all along and would buy them cheerfully among friends. Desire in Britain has almost never been free, and following the money is usually the fastest route to the truth.

Fourth is censorship, which is where the comedy lives. The Lord Chamberlain licensed the British stage for more than two centuries, and Soho built an industry around the absurd distinctions his office enforced. Chapter Ten follows the motionless nudes, private-club loopholes and licensing committees in full. Censorship in this country has rarely been effective and frequently ridiculous, but it should not be laughed at too easily: the machinery that regulated a tableau belonged to the same culture of control that jailed Oscar Wilde for two years' hard labour.

Fifth is policing, and here the book's map leaves London behind. Enforcement was often not a matter of statute at all but of neighbours. Robert Burns was hauled before his kirk session and set on the cutty stool to be rebuked by name in front of the congregation, and the parish minute books of Scotland record thousands who were treated the same way with no verse to console them. Before there were vice squads there were elders, ministers, chapel societies and servants who talked.

Sixth is technology, which arrives repeatedly and always does the same thing: it makes desire cheaper to reproduce and harder to police. Print gave the eighteenth century its bestselling directory of prostitutes. Photography and the stereoscope stocked the back rooms of Holywell Street. The party plan, of all things, opened the sex trade to half the population.

And underneath all six, unchanging, is the seventh: private appetite. It is the one variable in this book that does not move. What alters is the vocabulary, the address, the price and the penalty.

A word about method, because it bears on the pleasure of the thing. This book flags what it cannot prove in the prose rather than hiding uncertainty in a footnote. Where a place has been restored, a legend is late, or a lurid biography rests on one hostile or salacious source, the distinction is made at the point where it matters. That discipline is not a caveat. It is the method.

Finally, and most importantly: the people. A great many of those who appear here were treated abominably by their own century, and the comedy in these pages is aimed at the powerful, the pious and the absurd, never at them. The women buried in unconsecrated ground at Crossbones had no choice about much. Neither did the boys of Cleveland Street. Wilde died broken in a Paris hotel. Desire is treated in this book as ordinary, universal and interesting, which is what it is, and the moral panics are treated as the historical curiosities they generally turn out to be.

We begin where the stakes were highest. In most of Britain, for most of its history, who you took to bed was a private matter that might, if you were unlucky, become a parish matter. In exactly one bedroom in England it was a matter of treason, succession and the constitution — where a king's boredom could dissolve a marriage, a church and the standing of a nation in Europe, and where a queen's alleged adultery was tried as a capital crime against the state.

So we start at Hampton Court.

#### Chapter 1 — Crowns and Bedchambers

In most of England, for most of its history, the consequences of an ill-judged love affair were a fine, a spell of public shame, and a village that remembered. In roughly a dozen bedrooms in this chapter, the consequences were a broken church, a contested succession, a beheading, or the loss of a throne.

That is the only real difference between royal desire and everybody else's, and it is a difference of scale rather than of kind. Henry VIII wanted a woman who would not sleep with him, waited the better part of seven years, and dismantled England's relationship with Rome to get her — then decided he wanted somebody else and had her tried for treason on evidence that would not now convict a shoplifter. Mary Stuart married for love, or something adjacent to it, and had her secretary butchered in front of her by her husband's friends while she was six months pregnant. George IV married a Catholic widow in a Mayfair drawing room in 1785, in a ceremony he knew perfectly well to be void twice over under two separate Acts of Parliament, and then had his friend Charles James Fox deny it flatly in the House of Commons.

The mechanism that made these things constitutional rather than merely sad was inheritance. A crown descends through bodies, so the law took a close interest in whose body had been where and with whom. This produced a legal apparatus of quite spectacular intrusiveness — the Royal Marriages Act of 1772, the Act of Settlement, the whole machinery of annulment, legitimacy and precontract — and it produced the peculiar situation in which a nation's foreign policy could be reorganised around one man's boredom.

It also produced the evasions, which is why this chapter opens the book. Faced with rules of this weight, royal lovers did precisely what the fishwife and the curate did, only with better lawyers and more expensive houses. They married in secret. They kept establishments at a discreet distance from the palace, and when Nell Gwyn objected to holding her Pall Mall house on a mere lease, Charles II granted the freehold on 1 December 1676 to two trustees, who conveyed it to her own trustees the following April and settled it on her for life. As late as 1960 it was still the only property on the south side of Pall Mall not held of the Crown: a piece of conveyancing that is really a love letter. They built a house on the Isle of Wight where nobody could see in. They retreated to a lodge in Windsor Great Park to make the least private decision in modern British history. And when the affair was over, the family burned the letters. Beatrice copied her mother's journals into a hundred and eleven notebooks and burned most of the originals as she went, finishing in 1931 over the objections of George V and Queen Mary. In 1905 Edward VII destroyed more than three hundred letters Victoria had written to her Balmoral factor Alexander Profeit about John Brown, after they were recovered from a blackmailer. A great deal of what this chapter would like to tell you went up a chimney in the interests of the institution.

What survives is therefore skewed, and skewed in a particular direction: towards what the state needed to write down, and away from what the parties would have preferred to keep. Seventeen love letters in Henry's own hand survive in the Vatican Library. How they reached Rome is unknown; the most recent theory, advanced by Peter Lake, Michael Questier, Kendra Packham and Estelle Paranque in 2023, argues that they were still in England in 1601 and reached Rome through an English Jesuit. It remains a hypothesis. Holyrood has the murder because a murder requires depositions. Osborne has the marriage because Victoria could not stop writing about it and only her daughter's editorial hand stands between us and the whole of it.

We start at the top of the social order for the same reason a physicist starts with the frictionless case. Here the forces are enormous and unmistakable — law, money, succession, Rome, Parliament — and you can watch them act on private appetite without anything getting in the way. Everything that follows in this book is the same collision, at lower voltage, among people who did not have a palace to conduct it in.

#### Chapter 2 — The Scandal Sheets

Getting caught is not a constant. It is a technology, and in the eighteenth century it improved dramatically.

What changes between this chapter and the last is not that people started behaving worse. It is that a commercial press arrived, discovered that other people's marriages sold papers, and set about industrialising the business of exposure. Print runs rose; caricature became a national art form; the satirical print shops of St James's put engravings in their windows within days of a rumour reaching the clubs. By Amelia Rauser's count, at least eighty-nine different political prints survive from April 1784 alone, the month the Duchess of Devonshire canvassed for Charles James Fox in the Westminster election; many turned her into a duchess trading kisses for votes. The story was manufactured, distributed and believed at a speed that would have been impossible thirty years earlier.

The result is a strange asymmetry, and it is the engine of this chapter. The behaviour on display here is not obviously more scandalous than what came before. A ménage à trois ran openly at Chatsworth for twenty-five years — the Duke, the Duchess, and her closest friend Bess Foster, all under one roof, with children by both women in the nursery. Nelson, the nation's hero, lived at Merton Place with another man's wife and their daughter, and the entire country agreed, more or less politely, not to notice. What varies is not the conduct but the exposure: who gets written about, in what tone, and who is protected.

Protection, as ever, tracked money and sex in combination. Nelson could keep Emma Hamilton in Surrey because he had won the Nile; Emma, once he was dead and his codicil ignored, died in Calais with nothing. Georgiana was sent abroad to give birth in France and the child was raised elsewhere; the Duke's illegitimate children by Bess were simply absorbed into the household. Mary Robinson, the Maid of Buttermere, was famous for a fortnight, and the man who married her bigamously was hanged for forgery, which tells you what the eighteenth century actually valued.

And there is a second, quieter theme running underneath the noise, which is the sheer scale of what did not get written about at all. For every ducal ménage that generated a hundred prints there were thousands of arrangements — servants, tenants, the woman who kept house for a widower for thirty years — that generated nothing whatever. This chapter's Edinburgh brothel-keeper was a public figure in her own city, prosecuted repeatedly, entirely tolerated in practice, and yet the exact prices she charged do not survive in any open source. Fame is a preservative; obscurity is not.

So the scandal sheets should be read the way one reads any hostile witness: carefully, and with attention to what the witness wanted. They are frequently the only record we have. They are also, almost without exception, produced by men with something to sell, aimed at women with something to lose. Georgiana's kisses, whether or not she ever gave one, were a political attack dressed as gossip. That the attack still shapes how she is remembered, two and a half centuries on, is a fair measure of how well this technology worked.

#### Chapter 3 — Rakes, Monks and Pleasure Gardens

Somewhere around the Restoration, the English worked out that pleasure could be organised — that it could be built, staffed, advertised, timetabled and sold by the ticket. This chapter is about what they built, and about the persistent gap between the brochure and the premises.

The gap is the point, and the Hellfire Club is the purest demonstration of it. Sir Francis Dashwood's friends met at Medmenham Abbey and later in the chalk caves at West Wycombe, dressed as monks, drank a great deal of claret, and called the women they brought with them nuns. That much is solid. Almost everything else — the black masses, the Satanism, the orgiastic rites in the inner chamber — descends from a hostile novel of the 1760s and from the political vendetta of a former member. The caves themselves were dug as famine relief, providing paid work for local labourers. The most notorious sex club in English history was, in its origins, a public works scheme with a fancy-dress theme, and it has been dining out on the rumour ever since.

Vauxhall was the honest version, and considerably cleverer. For a shilling the visitor got twelve acres of designed possibility: supper boxes hung with paintings, an orchestra, triumphal arches, a thousand oil lamps lit simultaneously at a whistle — and, deliberately retained among all that illumination, a set of unlit walks. Jonathan Tyers installed the lights to make the gardens respectable and kept the shadows because respectability alone did not pay. That double instinct, selling the light and the dark in the same enclosure at the same price, is the commercial logic of the entire chapter.

What such places really sold was not sex but proximity and deniability — the chance to be somewhere other people were, in conditions that permitted a great deal to happen and nothing to be admitted. The shilling was the control mechanism as much as the price. Half a crown at Ranelagh bought a better class of company; a guinea at a masquerade bought anonymity along with it.

The private houses in this chapter ran on the same principle at a smaller scale. Byron kept a skull cup at Newstead and dressed his friends as monks, in what looks like deliberate homage to Dashwood, which is theatre borrowed from theatre. The Delavals of Northumberland spent a fortune on practical jokes and amateur dramatics. Bloomsbury, arriving a century and a half later, dispensed with the costumes and kept the essential arrangement: a house, a set of friends, and a private understanding that the ordinary rules were suspended within its walls.

The through-line from Medmenham to Charleston is not licentiousness. It is the discovery that if you assemble the right group of people in the right building and agree on the rules in advance, you can construct a small temporary jurisdiction in which different things are permitted. That is what a pleasure garden is, what a club is, and what a country house full of painters is. This chapter is about people who understood that the surest way round a rule is to build somewhere it does not apply.

#### Chapter 4 — The Old Gods

The chalk giant above Cerne Abbas, in Dorset, is fifty-five metres tall, carries a club, and has an erection of some thirty-five feet. He is also, on the best current evidence, medieval — dated by optically stimulated luminescence to a range of AD 700 to 1100, with the deepest sample from his elbow pointing to a date around 905, which is neither the Iron Age fertility god of the guidebooks nor the seventeenth-century joke about Cromwell that replaced him. Most tellingly, no surviving document securely mentions him before 1694, when the churchwardens recorded three shillings for repairing him. A colossal naked man on a hillside is not easy to overlook. The silence of everyone who should have seen him is the loudest evidence we have.

This chapter is about that kind of silence, and about what got poured into it later.

Britain's ancient fertility rites are, to a degree that becomes comic once you start counting, a Victorian invention laid over genuinely old things. Castlerigg is a real Neolithic circle, but its grove and apparent alignment were altered during the Romantic era. The Long Man of Wilmington, that immemorial pagan colossus, first appears in a drawing of 1710 and was dated by excavation to the sixteenth or seventeenth century. The Padstow 'Obby 'Oss is first recorded in 1803, and the pagan reading was fixed on it by Frazerian folklorists around 1913 and then absorbed by the town itself, so that visiting scholars are now told their own theory back. The Green Man was named and defined in an article published in 1939.

The temptation is to treat this as debunking, and to feel superior about it. That would be a mistake and, worse, dull. The customs are genuinely old and genuinely marvellous. Abbots Bromley dances with reindeer antlers radiocarbon-dated to around 1065, in a country where reindeer had been extinct for centuries — a set of horns carried from Scandinavia and kept in one Staffordshire village for the better part of a millennium. Nobody invented that. What the antiquarians invented was the meaning.

And the human impulse underneath is neither invented nor recent. People genuinely did pass sick children through the holed stone at Mên-an-Tol. Women genuinely did take their hopes of conception to a chalk figure, a well, a monolith in a Yorkshire churchyard. That such practices are often documented no earlier than the nineteenth century tells us about the history of record-keeping, not about the history of longing.

So the argument of this chapter is not that the old gods are fake. It is that Britain has repeatedly required its landscape to be more anciently and more explicitly sexual than the evidence supports, and has been willing to move stones, plant trees and rewrite customs to make it so. The joke lands on the antiquarian with his Golden Bough and his theory, never on the villagers dancing at dawn. They knew what they were doing. It was the scholars who needed it to mean something.

#### Chapter 5 — Love in the Library

Three sisters lived in a parsonage on the edge of a Yorkshire moor with their father, their brother and a graveyard at the door. Between them they had almost no romantic experience whatsoever. Within roughly two years they produced Jane Eyre, Wuthering Heights and The Tenant of Wildfell Hall, and reviewers refused to believe women had written them.

That is the argument of this chapter, stated in one household. English literature's most powerful writing about desire was produced, again and again, by people with restricted, thwarted, censored or entirely absent sexual lives — and the constraint was not an unfortunate background condition. It was the engine.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Where the thing itself is unavailable, the imagination does the work, and the prohibition supplies the pressure. Austen's restraint is not a Victorian evasion of sex — she predates the Victorians — but a technique, and the charge lives precisely in the negative space where the kiss does not happen.

There is a second, harder theme running through these houses, and it concerns who controlled the record afterwards. Cassandra Austen burned the bulk of her sister's letters. Wordsworth's family concealed his French daughter for over a century, until an American scholar reconstructed Annette Vallon from Dorothy's correspondence and published in 1916. Coleridge cut Sara Hutchinson out of his own greatest poem, replacing her name with "Lady" and publishing the remnant on Wordsworth's wedding day. A passage of Dorothy's Grasmere journal was scored out by an unknown hand. Hardy is said to have read his dead wife's account of their marriage and destroyed it, then wrote her the tenderest elegies in the language.

So the literary chapter is also a chapter about editing — about sisters, widowers, executors and descendants deciding what posterity would be permitted to know. What we inherit is a cleaned version of some extremely complicated households, and the cleaning was usually done by people who loved the dead and thought they were being kind.

The houses themselves survive, and they are smaller than the work suggests. Austen wrote at a twelve-sided walnut table barely larger than a dinner plate, in a room with no privacy, with a creaking door left un-oiled as an early warning system. Keats had half a villa and a plum tree. Dylan Thomas had a shed on a cliff. It is worth standing in these rooms and registering the disproportion: the enormous interior weather, and the very small space it was made in.

#### Chapter 6 — Hidden in Plain Sight

The queer past reaches us in exactly two forms. There is what people encrypted themselves, and there is what the state wrote down while trying to destroy them. Almost everything else was burned.

Anne Lister is the first kind, and she is very nearly unique. An estimated five million words of diary, roughly a sixth in a code of Greek letters and algebra she devised herself, recording her lovers, her techniques and her own vocabulary — a "kiss" for an orgasm, a cross in the margin for masturbation. In plain hand she wrote that she loved and only loved the fairer sex. When her descendant cracked the cipher in the 1890s, a friend advised him to burn the diaries; he hid them behind the panelling instead, which is the single luckiest act of cowardice in this book.

Mother Clap's molly house is the second kind. We can describe the inside of that Holborn establishment — forty or fifty men drinking, dancing, taking female names, going in couples into the room they called the marrying room — because a constable infiltrated it in 1725 and swore to what he saw, and because his deposition was printed at the Old Bailey before three of the men he described were hanged at Tyburn on 9 May 1726. The Caravan Club at 81 Endell Street survives in the same way: police surveillance logs, seized membership cards, a torn-up love letter recovered from under a divan, all filed at the National Archives after the raid of August 1934. The evidence exists because somebody was compiling a prosecution.

Between those two poles lies everything ambiguous, and this chapter is careful there. The Ladies of Llangollen lived together for fifty years, dressed alike, called each other beloved, and were furious when a newspaper implied what everyone suspected. Whether they were lovers is unknowable, and the "romantic friendship" framing they insisted on was both a genuine category of their age and, conveniently, a defence. Anne Lister herself went to Plas Newydd in 1822 specifically to find out, and came away doubting it was platonic — one queer woman's assessment of another couple, which is the best evidence anyone has and is still only a guess.

The law behaves differently on either side of the ledger, and the asymmetry is instructive. Sex between men was capital until 1861 and remained criminal until partial decriminalisation in England and Wales in 1967, and for over a decade longer in Scotland and Northern Ireland; sex between women was never criminalised at all — not because Queen Victoria refused to believe in it, which is a myth with no basis, but because male legislators chose deliberate silence, partly for fear of giving respectable women ideas. The practical effect was that lesbian spaces were policed by licensing and social pressure rather than by the dock, which is why the Gateways survived behind a green door on the King's Road for half a century while molly houses were raided.

The families did the rest of the damage. The sixth Marquess of Anglesey destroyed his cousin's papers, which is why the Dancing Marquess can only be inferred. Forster wrote Maurice in 1913 and refused to publish it for the rest of his life, because it ended happily.

#### Chapter 7 — Doomed and Determined

This is the shortest chapter in the book, deliberately. Most of Britain's evasions left a trail of raids, ruined lives and vanished rooms. Gretna Green produced something rarer: a place where the evasion became more durable than the rule.

Its importance lies in scale rather than complexity. A difference between English and Scottish marriage law turned the first village beyond the border into a national shorthand for escape. The couples came first; innkeepers, farmers and anvil priests built the industry around them. Long after the legal loophole closed, people kept coming because the journey itself had acquired meaning.

There is therefore no need to make a procession of one stop. The chapter crosses the border once and stays there long enough to see how a legal accident became a trade, a legend and finally a tradition.

#### Chapter 8 — The Century of Scandal

The twentieth century's great sex scandals were never really about sex. They were about class, power, and who gets believed — and the reliable test is to ask who ended up ruined.

The pattern is not subtle. The men involved were protected by the institutions they belonged to or elevated by the myth-making that followed; the women carried the shame for everyone. What made these episodes possible was a press that had acquired cameras, telephoto lenses and a proprietor's appetite for the bedroom, arriving at exactly the moment when deference to the governing class was beginning to fail. The combination was combustible.

There is a third element, easily missed. Both scandals hinged on a lie told to an institution — Profumo to Parliament, the Redlands prosecution to itself about what it had actually found — and in both cases the institution's need to be seen to act mattered more than the facts. That is why the pattern recurs. It was never the sex that brought governments down. It was the discovery that the people in charge had been lying about it, and the ancient British conviction that somebody less important should pay.

#### Chapter 9 — Monuments to Pleasure

A statue, a clinic, a basement and a shop. Between them, these four addresses did more to change what Britain was allowed to want than any king, poet or Act of Parliament in the preceding chapters — which is a large claim, and this chapter sets out to earn it.

Start with the joke, because it sets the tone. The winged figure above Piccadilly Circus is not Eros. Alfred Gilbert made it as Anteros, god of selfless and requited love, to crown a memorial to the Earl of Shaftesbury, the campaigner against child labour. Officialdom, panicking at a nude, tried to rename it the Angel of Christian Charity. The public looked at a naked archer over the West End and called it Eros anyway — and the monument to chaste philanthropy became, over the following century, one of the best-known pick-up spots in London. The fountain bankrupted its sculptor, who fled to Bruges. Britain's most famous erotic landmark is the wrong god, on the wrong subject, ruining the right man.

The pattern is worth naming. Change did not arrive by proclamation. It arrived at four ordinary addresses where a monument, a clinic, a club and a shop changed what could be acknowledged, controlled, shared and sold. Each worked because people used it until the law, the market or public memory was obliged to catch up.

#### Chapter 10 — London After Dark

A word before we go in, because the register changes here and you should know why.

This chapter follows London's commercial sex trade and erotic nightlife across eight centuries, and it is written for adults. Where the record documents what people did, what they wore, what they paid and what apparatus they used, this chapter says so plainly, in ordinary English, without asterisks and without the coy Victorian circumlocutions that made so much of this material unreadable in the first place. The reticence practised elsewhere in this book would here amount to a kind of dishonesty: you cannot write about the Bankside stews or a Georgian flogging house and then decline to say what happened in them. There is also, unavoidably, material about the exploitation of the poor and the young — the Cleveland Street prosecutions of 1889 turn entirely on it — and where that is the subject, the comedy stops. If you would rather not read explicit historical description, this is the chapter to skip. The book resumes its ordinary manners on the other side.

Now the argument, which is the sharpest in the book.

London's sex trade was never simply suppressed by the authorities. It was also licensed, taxed and regulated by them, usually by the same institutions publicly condemning it. The brothels of medieval Bankside operated under ordinances issued in the name of the Bishop of Winchester, who drew rents from the stews for centuries, until Henry VIII suppressed the licensed houses in 1546; the women were nicknamed Winchester Geese after their landlord. An annual printed directory of Covent Garden prostitutes, giving names, addresses, prices and specialities, sold thousands of copies a year for the better part of four decades in the eighteenth century, advertised in newspapers and sold openly at booksellers' stalls. It was not prosecuted until 1794 — by which point its publisher could tell the court, accurately, that nobody had thought it worth prosecuting before.

What changed in the nineteenth century, historians argue, was not morality so much as access. As explicit material became cheaper and reached beyond the collectors whom the authorities trusted, regulation hardened. The closer the trade came to working-class and youthful readers, the more urgently the state discovered an interest in suppressing it. That is a historical interpretation rather than the stated object of any one statute, but it reveals the argument beneath this chapter: obscenity law in Britain has always been, at bottom, a law about class and price.

## 70 — Coda

### BOOK VERSION

Desire does not disappear. It changes address.

The map has been saying this all along. Put a wall around the City and the stews, theatres and pleasure gardens gather beyond it. Tighten English marriage law and carriages rattle north to the first Scottish village. Clear the women from the pavement and the trade moves upstairs. Raid a basement club and the party survives in the police file. Demolish Holywell Street and the books travel west. A rule changes the route far more reliably than it ends the journey.

Yet movement is only half the story. The other half is what gets left behind. Authority preserved the rooms it entered with a warrant, the letters it seized and the names it needed for an indictment. Lovers made a different archive: a diary in cipher, a ring engraved on the inside, a letter repaired after it had been torn, a carved spoon, an unopened envelope taken into a grave. The state recorded the offence. People recorded the meaning.

That is why the places in this book do not all belong to the same kind of erotic history. A royal bedchamber mattered because succession passed through it. A Welsh well held curses, marriages and hopes for children. A Yorkshire parsonage contained almost no lived romance and produced some of the fiercest desire in English fiction. A green door in Chelsea mattered because the women behind it could be together without explanation. A patch of woodland matters because it is still doing the work for which earlier generations had so few safe rooms.

The map is therefore not a catalogue of sex acts. It is a map of pressure points, the places where private appetite met law, class, money, belief and memory. Sometimes the collision produced a church, a trial or an abdication. Sometimes it produced a joke. Often it produced a silence, and the silence was not empty. Somebody had burned the letters, changed the name, removed the carving or decided that the servant did not belong in the obituary.

Discretion runs through the book, but it was never simply a national virtue. It could be shelter or prison. It allowed two women to build a household in Llangollen without supplying posterity with a confession. It also allowed families to destroy evidence and call the destruction kindness. It kept a club alive behind an unmarked door and kept a partner out of a death notice. The same instinct protected people from the world and protected the world from having to acknowledge them.

The myths deserve their place too. The Long Man did not lose a penis to the Victorians. The Green Man was named in 1939. The Mars bar was never at Redlands. Theresa Berkley's biography may be resting on a collector's appetite for a good story. Removing these additions does not make the history smaller. It restores the people and places that the embroidery had hidden. A fact need not be ancient to be moving, and a story need not be true to tell us something about the century that wanted it.

Change is real, although it arrives late and cannot compensate the dead. Wilde received a pardon that altered nothing he had endured. Anne Lister's code became a world document long after she had been carried home from Georgia. The Gateways acquired a plaque forty years after the green door closed. Laws were repealed, names restored and archives opened. Progress is genuine, uneven and never retroactive.

The present tense continues the pattern. A nightclub closes and reopens under another operator. A royal censor gives way to a council licensing committee. An app offers privacy in a pocket, while men still choose the wooded ground their predecessors used before the telephone. Technology changes the doorbell. It does not abolish the knock.

Britain never lacked an erotic history. It placed it across the river, over the border, behind the panelling, beneath the pavement and inside the ring. The map remains unfinished because desire has never been good at staying where it is put.

### WEB VERSION — marquis-de-mayfair-boutique

Mayfair is named after a fair that was shut down for indecency. The May Fair ran for a fortnight each spring on the ground north of Piccadilly, and by the early eighteenth century it had acquired such a reputation for disorder and lewdness that it was presented as a public nuisance and eventually suppressed altogether. The architect Edward Shepherd bought the site, laid out his alleys and his market building, and the district reinvented itself as the most expensive square mile in Britain — while quietly keeping the habit of discretion it had learned when it was a fairground.

That habit is the real Mayfair tradition, and it is older than any single shop. Within a short walk are premises that have been trading since 1676, 1698 and 1730, selling hats, wine and perfume respectively, and what they have actually sold across three centuries is something less tangible: privacy, quality, and the sense that a transaction here requires no explanation. Shepherd Market, at the district's heart, sustained a discreet sex trade well into living memory on precisely the same principle. Mayfair has always understood that the most valuable thing a shop can offer is the absence of a raised eyebrow.

The Marquis de Mayfair is a present-day endpoint of the long story this map traces, rather than a documented continuation of it. No ledger links it to the Georgian trade in beautiful private objects, and none is claimed. What links them is an approach: that pleasure is best served by things that are well made, properly presented, and nobody else's business.

It is worth saying plainly that this is an argument about national character rather than a chain of ownership. The British erotic tradition, as the hundred-odd locations on this map suggest, has never been one of public abandon. It has been one of concealment and craft — the inscription hidden inside the ring, the coded diary, the discreet doorway — and a Mayfair boutique dealing in fine objects for private use sits recognisably within it.
