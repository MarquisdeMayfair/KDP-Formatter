## 36 — The Cleaned-Up Households of the Lakes
*Dove Cottage, Grasmere · Greta Hall, Keswick · Calais and Blois · Cumbria & France · 1791–1810*

### BOOK VERSION
On the morning of 4 October 1802, in a farmhouse at Gallow Hill in Yorkshire, Dorothy Wordsworth put her brother's wedding ring on her own finger and wore it through the night. In the morning William came to her room and she gave it back to him; he slipped it onto her finger again, blessed her, and went off to church to marry Mary Hutchinson. Dorothy could not go. She lay on the bed until the two men came running up to say it was done, and then, she wrote, she could stand it no longer and threw herself down. The passage is one of the most quietly astonishing things in English literature, and it survives because Dorothy wrote it in the Grasmere Journal in her own hand — "I gave him the wedding ring — with how deep a blessing! I took it from my forefinger where I had worn it the whole of the night before — he slipped it again onto my finger and blessed me fervently."

What we do with that passage is another matter entirely, and here we should tread carefully. Some readers, including serious scholars, have found an unmistakable erotic charge in it and in the whole texture of William and Dorothy's life together at Dove Cottage — the shared bed on cold nights, the recorded lying-down together in a hollow as if in the grave, the intensity of a devotion that no husband and no wife ever displaced. Others regard it as an extraordinarily intense but essentially chaste sibling attachment, expressed in the heightened emotional idiom that the age permitted between family members, and warn against reading twenty-first-century categories back into 1802. Both readings are defensible. Neither can be proved. What is certain is only what Dorothy wrote — and, tellingly, what she did not leave: a passage in the journal was later scored out by an unknown hand, in ink of roughly the same age, so that we will never know what those lines said. Somebody, at some point, decided the record should be quieter.

Dove Cottage itself is a small, dark, low-ceilinged former inn at Town End, Grasmere, where William and Dorothy settled in December 1799 and stayed until 1808. It is where Dorothy kept the journal, where William wrote a great deal of his best work, and where the household expanded and contracted with an astonishing cast: Mary Hutchinson, who married William and moved in; her sister Sara Hutchinson, who came and went; and, endlessly, Samuel Taylor Coleridge, who lived twelve miles off and treated the walk as nothing. When the Wordsworths finally left, the tenancy passed to a young admirer named Thomas De Quincey, who would later frighten the neighbourhood with his opium and his memoirs. It is the same small Lakeland world, and Coleridge is the man who links its beds together.

Coleridge had taken Greta Hall, a handsome house on a rise above Keswick, in 1800, partly to be near Wordsworth. He was married to Sara Fricker, unhappily, and had been more or less from the start. And in the autumn of 1799 he had met the other Sara — Sara Hutchinson, Mary's sister — and fallen for her with a completeness that never left him and never, so far as anyone can establish, got anywhere at all. He coded her in his notebooks as "Asra", partly to distinguish her from his wife, partly to hide her; scholars think the anagram nods at "Astra", the stars. He wrote of his soul fluttering in a spider-web net of steel around the idea of Sara. It is one of the most thoroughly documented hopeless passions in literature, and it is documented because Coleridge could not stop writing it down.

The key document is a poem. On the night of 4 April 1802 — the same terrible spring in which all this was coming to a head — Coleridge sat down and wrote a verse letter of some 340 lines addressed directly to "Sara", pouring out his misery, his dead marriage, his lost creative power, and his love for a woman he could not have. Over the following months he cut it almost in half, took "Sara" out and replaced her with the neutral "Lady", removed the references to Wordsworth, and published the result as "Dejection: An Ode". The date he chose to publish it was 4 October 1802 — Wordsworth's wedding day, and the seventh anniversary of his own. The full verse letter to Sara Hutchinson did not appear in print until 1937. Coleridge had, in other words, performed on his own greatest late poem exactly the operation the Wordsworth family would later perform on William's life: he took out the woman and tidied the record.

The friendship that held all these people together did not survive. In 1810 a mutual acquaintance, Basil Montagu — a man the Wordsworths had known for years, whose motherless small son they had once brought up — repeated to Coleridge some crushing remarks William had made about his drinking and his hopelessness, to the effect that he was a nuisance and a hopeless drunkard. Coleridge, by now deep in opium, was devastated. The two men who had made English Romanticism between them were essentially estranged for years, and never fully recovered the old intimacy. Wordsworth's descendants would later insist he had been "massively supportive" and that the real fault was Montagu's tale-bearing. As so often in this story, the family wanted the record improved.

Which brings us to the thing the family most wanted improved. In 1791, aged twenty-one, William Wordsworth went to France, fell in with the Revolution, and fell in love with a young Frenchwoman named Annette Vallon, four years his senior, from a royalist Catholic family in Orléans. On 15 December 1792 the register office at Orléans recorded the baptism of Anne-Caroline, daughter of Marie Anne Vallon and William Wordsworth. He had already gone to Paris, and soon afterwards returned to England; war between Britain and France then slammed the door for the best part of a decade. When the brief Peace of Amiens reopened it, William and Dorothy travelled to Calais in the summer of 1802 to meet Annette and the daughter William had never known — a settling of accounts before his marriage to Mary that autumn. It produced the tender sonnet about a walk with a child by the sea, "It is a beauteous evening, calm and free", written to a daughter he would rarely see again.

And then the whole business simply vanished. Wordsworth concealed it; the family after him concealed it; the most morally elevated poet in Victorian England, the one whose verses were printed in improving editions for children, had a French daughter that almost nobody knew about. It stayed hidden until the American scholar George McLean Harper reconstructed it — from Dorothy's own letters, found among the British Museum manuscripts, in which she referred quite openly to "Madame Vallon" and to Caroline as her niece — and published it in his 1916 biography, following it in 1921 with a slim book bluntly titled *Wordsworth's French Daughter*. The affair had never been a secret to the Wordsworths. It had simply been kept from everyone else.

That is the argument of this whole small Lakeland world. The most respectable poets in England ran, between them, a household of quite extraordinary emotional complication — a sister who wore the wedding ring, a poet in love with his sister-in-law, a concealed child across the Channel — and then spent the best part of a century, with the diligent help of their families, tidying it into something a vicar could recommend.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The dream before the wedding**
The night before William married, Coleridge dreamed of Dorothy — but transformed, unrecognisable, a heavy woman with thick limbs. "If I did not know you to be Dorothy," the dream-Coleridge said, "I never should suppose it." It is a small, eerie thing, recorded in the circle's papers, and it sits oddly close to the wedding-ring passage: two of the men who loved Dorothy's wild, ardent quality seem to have sensed, that autumn, that something was ending.

**The eyes everyone remembered**
Nobody who met the young Dorothy Wordsworth forgot her eyes. The radical John Thelwall called her "the maid of ardent eye"; William wrote of the "shooting lights" of her "wild eyes"; De Quincey called her the wildest, most ardent person he had ever known. By the end of a long life clouded by illness she had become a recluse — a transformation that biographers, reaching for the drama, have sometimes overstated. The wildness was real, and so was the tenderness.

**Sara's Seat**
On the fells above Grasmere the household built a stone seat and named it for Sara Hutchinson. It is the kind of ordinary domestic act — a place to rest on a walk — that the journals are full of. But the name carries a freight the walkers themselves might not have owned: the woman Coleridge could not have, memorialised in stone by the family she could not quite join, on a hillside they all crossed together, again and again.

## 37 — The Novelist of the Barely-Touched Hand
*Jane Austen's House, Chawton · The Cobb, Lyme Regis · Hampshire & Dorset · 1795–1817*

### BOOK VERSION
On the morning of 3 December 1802 Jane Austen did the single most dramatic thing she is known to have done, and she did it by leaving. The evening before, at Manydown Park near Steventon, she had accepted a proposal of marriage from Harris Bigg-Wither, the younger brother of two of her dearest friends. He was twenty-one; she was a few days short of twenty-seven. He was heir to a large Hampshire estate, tall, awkward, prone to a stammer, and — this being the whole point of the age — a solution. Marrying him would have made Jane mistress of a manor, secured her ageing parents, and relieved her brothers of two unmarried sisters. She said yes. And then she lay awake, and in the morning she found Harris, took it back, and demanded to be driven the eleven miles to her brother's rectory at once, in tears, refusing to say why. She and Cassandra fled the same day.

That is the central documented erotic fact of Jane Austen's life: not a consummation but a refusal, a woman of nearly twenty-seven turning down the only secure offer she is known to have received because she could not marry without love. It is Elizabeth Bennet's principle, tested on its author in the dark, at real cost. Everything else is fragments. There was a flirtation with a charming young Irishman named Tom Lefroy in the winter of 1795–96, documented in her own giddy letters to Cassandra — she confessed to behaving in the most "profligate and shocking" way in the matter of dancing and sitting down together — and then he was gone, packed off by prudent relatives, and that was that. There was a mysterious gentleman met at the seaside, of whom Cassandra spoke only in old age to her nieces: someone charming, seemingly serious, who meant to see them again and then abruptly died. Cassandra believed, if Jane ever truly loved, it was him. She would say no more, and there is no more to say, because the person who could have told us made sure of it.

For this is the other central fact, and it runs right through the book: after Jane's death in 1817, Cassandra Austen went through her sister's letters and burned the great bulk of them. What survives is a fraction, and Cassandra kept back or destroyed almost everything touching the years of deepest feeling — the seaside suitor, the aftermath of the Bigg-Wither night. She was not being careless; she was protecting Jane, in the way sisters did. But it means the erotic life of the greatest English novelist of manners reaches us as a series of gaps that another woman deliberately made. The map of Jane Austen's heart is mostly blank, and it is blank on purpose.

The house where most of the work happened is at Chawton, in Hampshire — a red-brick former inn (once, briefly and disreputably, an alehouse called the New Inn) that her brother Edward, adopted into the wealthy Knight family, gave rent-free to his mother and sisters in 1809. Jane lived here for the last eight years of her life, and here she revised *Sense and Sensibility* and *Pride and Prejudice* and wrote *Mansfield Park*, *Emma* and *Persuasion*. She wrote at a tiny walnut table, twelve-sided, scarcely larger than a dinner plate, set by a window in the general sitting room, because she had no study of her own and did most of her work amid the interruptions of a busy household. The often-repeated detail is that she asked that a creaking door be left un-oiled, so that its noise would warn her to cover her manuscript before anyone came in. It is a wonderful image of a private art conducted in a house with no privacy — the novelist of concealment, concealing.

And concealment is exactly how the novels generate their extraordinary erotic voltage, because there is almost nothing on the surface. Austen does not describe a kiss. She barely describes a touch. What she gives you instead is the hand — a hand pressed, a hand withdrawn, a glove, an arm offered on a walk, the precise social temperature of who may touch whom and when. When Captain Wentworth silently lifts a troublesome child off Anne Elliot's back in *Persuasion*, or removes her, wordlessly, into a carriage, the reader's nerves do more work than any bedroom scene could ask of them. The charge is entirely in the restraint. It is the negative space that hums.

Which is why Lyme Regis, on the Dorset coast, matters. Austen visited the little resort in 1803 and 1804 — she wrote to Cassandra about the bathing-machines and the walks — and she loved the place enough to give it the single most physical scene she ever wrote. In *Persuasion*, a party walks out along the Cobb, the great curving stone harbour wall, and the giddy, headstrong Louisa Musgrove insists on being "jumped down" the steep steps by Captain Wentworth. She likes the sensation; on their walks he has always had to jump her from the stiles. He obliges once, reluctantly. She runs back up to do it again, he is not ready, she leaps too soon — "she was too precipitate by half a second, she fell on the pavement on the Lower Cobb, and was taken up lifeless!" It is a fall in every sense, the closest Austen comes to letting a woman's physical craving for a man drive the plot off a wall. A critic writing in *Partisan Review* (Vol. 62, No. 1, 1995) read the whole episode as a scarcely-veiled sexual consummation, thrilling and dangerous and punished — pointing to Austen's own wry line elsewhere in the novel that Wentworth had been "making love, by breaking his mistress's head", and to the pattern that runs through the book connecting sex and childbirth with injury. The steep, rail-less steps jutting from the wall are known as Granny's Teeth, and generations of visitors have tried them, though the steps we now point to may not be the ones Austen imagined — she was more likely thinking of a flight further along. When Tennyson walked nine miles to Lyme in 1867 he brushed aside all offers of refreshment: "Don't talk to me of the Duke of Monmouth," he said; "show me the exact spot where Louisa Musgrove fell."

The Cobb had a second literary life. John Fowles lived in Lyme, and it is on that same wall, in the sea-fret, that *The French Lieutenant's Woman* (1969) opens with its motionless, staring figure — a book, and a 1981 film, in which desire is once again a matter of a woman standing where she should not, looking out to sea. Two of the most charged scenes in English fiction happen on one Dorset breakwater, and in both the eroticism is a matter of position, distance and the held breath. Austen would have understood exactly.

The novels do their erotic work by pure restraint — the hand, the glove, the barely-touched moment — without a kiss on the page. The house is a museum.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The wine, and the shaking hand**
Austen's surviving letters are funnier and franker than the pious Victorian image allows. In one to Cassandra she owns up cheerfully: "I believe I drank too much wine last night… I know not how else to account for the shaking of my hand today." It is a tiny thing, but it is the actual voice — dry, self-aware, entirely unshockable — that Cassandra's bonfire mostly took from us.

**Separate rooms**
In another surviving letter, Austen notes an acquaintance who has just had a baby and drily recommends to the couple "the simple regimen of separate rooms." It is as close as the record comes to Austen speaking plainly about the physical facts of marriage and childbearing — a subject her novels end just before reaching, and one she watched play out, pregnancy after pregnancy, among her sisters-in-law.

**The niece who asked for silence**
When Jane's nephew came to write the first family memoir, her niece Caroline urged him to leave the Bigg-Wither episode out entirely, lest it embarrass the family. It very nearly worked: the story of the accepted-and-retracted proposal survives largely because later relatives could not quite keep it down. The Austen instinct to tidy the record did not die with Cassandra.

## 38 — Gothic Desire: The Dead Woman's Nightdress and the Coffin-Ship
*Menabilly, near Fowey, Cornwall · Whitby Abbey, North Yorkshire · 1890–1969*

### BOOK VERSION
The house that became Manderley was one Daphne du Maurier never owned. Menabilly, the Rashleigh family's long, grey, shuttered mansion buried in woods on the Gribbin peninsula near Fowey, she first reached as a trespasser — walking miles through rhododendrons to find it apparently abandoned, windows barred, "like the sleeping beauty of the fairy tale", she wrote, "until someone should come to wake her." She was obsessed. She wrote *Rebecca* in 1938 while still only able to prowl its grounds by permission, rechristening the place Manderley and opening the novel with a line every novelist since has envied: "Last night I dreamt I went to Manderley again." Then, extraordinarily, in 1943 — with the house roofless, lightless, waterless, fungus on the ceilings, in the middle of a war — she talked Dr Rashleigh into leasing it to her, and she lived and wrote there for twenty-six years, until the family reclaimed it in 1969. She never got to buy it. Leaving, she said, was like tearing up a mandrake, which bleeds and shrieks.

*Rebecca* is usually filed as romance or gothic thriller, but its real subject is sexual jealousy and the erotic power a dead woman goes on exerting over a living one. The narrator — famously never named, so that the first, dead wife Rebecca dominates even the space where the heroine's name should be — arrives at Manderley as the second Mrs de Winter and finds the house, her husband and her own imagination wholly possessed by her predecessor. The custodian of that possession is the housekeeper, Mrs Danvers, who loved Rebecca with an intensity the novel never quite names and never needs to. In the celebrated scene in Rebecca's preserved bedroom, Danvers takes the shy young narrator through the dead woman's things — draws the nightdress from its case, has her feel the fabric, presses the underclothes on her, makes her handle the hairbrush and the furs. It is a seduction conducted through the body of a corpse, unmistakably erotic and faintly unbearable, and du Maurier writes it without a single word that a 1938 lending library could have objected to. The charge is all in the touching of things.

Du Maurier's own sexuality was complicated, and she described it herself, in her own private framing, in terms of a boy shut in a box. As a young woman she had adopted an alter ego she called "Eric Avon", and she spoke of a masculine self — the boy in the box — kept locked away inside the wife, mother and public persona. Her biographer Margaret Forster, who gained access to du Maurier's candid letters to Ellen Doubleday only at the editing stage of her 1993 life, concluded that the strain of feeling herself "two distinct people" both tortured du Maurier and fuelled her: without the boy in the box, Forster wrote, there would have been nothing. The two great loves that letters and biography point to were both women — Ellen Doubleday, the wife of her American publisher, for whom the feeling was an unconsummated obsession that she poured into the play *September Tide*; and the actress Gertrude Lawrence, who played the lead in that very play, and with whom Forster judged du Maurier probably did have an affair. Du Maurier disliked the plain words for all this — she preferred, drily, to call them "Venetian tendencies" — and handling her honestly means using her own frame, not a modern label she never chose.

Three hundred miles north-east, on a different coast, a different kind of desire came ashore. In late July 1890 Bram Stoker — exhausted from managing the actor Henry Irving's theatrical empire — took a holiday at Mrs Veazey's guesthouse, 6 Royal Crescent, in Whitby, on the Yorkshire coast. He walked the 199 steps up to the ruined abbey, sat in the churchyard of St Mary's, copied names off the tombstones (one, Swales, went straight into the book), and talked to the coastguards about a shipwreck they all still remembered: in October 1885 a Russian schooner named the *Dmitry*, out of Narva with a cargo of silver sand, had been driven ashore below the East Cliff in a gale. On 8 August 1890 he walked into the little lending library on the quayside and asked for an obscure 1820 book by a former British consul, William Wilkinson — *An Account of the Principalities of Wallachia and Moldavia* — and found, in a footnote, a fifteenth-century warlord and a single sentence: "Dracula in the Wallachian language means Devil." He wrote the word down, went back through his notes, and crossed out the name of his villain, "Count Wampyr", everywhere it appeared. Whitby gave him the ship — the *Dmitry* became the *Demeter*, out of Varna, an anagram of Narva — the steps, the churchyard, the storm, and the name. That much is genuinely documented, mostly from Stoker's own working notes, now at the Rosenbach Museum in Philadelphia; the wider claim that Whitby somehow "inspired *Dracula*" whole is overstated, since the vampire and the Transylvanian setting predated the visit.

But the Whitby chapters of *Dracula* (1897) are where the novel's real business — sex — becomes impossible to miss, and it should be described as what it is, because Stoker built it deliberately. It is at Whitby that the *Demeter* strikes the harbour with a dead man lashed to the wheel and a great dog leaps ashore. It is at Whitby that the vampire begins to visit Lucy Westenra — a warm, flirtatious girl who has just wondered aloud, scandalously, why a woman may not marry three men at once — meeting her at night in the clifftop churchyard as she sleepwalks. What follows is one long coded seduction. Lucy, drained and transformed, becomes "voluptuous", her sweetness turned to "voluptuous wantonness"; her fiancé and his friends must drive a stake through her to restore her to decent, dead purity. Earlier, in Transylvania, Jonathan Harker has met the three vampire women, and Stoker lets him say the quiet part almost aloud — a "deliberate voluptuousness which was both thrilling and repulsive", and "a wicked, burning desire that they would kiss me with those red lips." Modern critics read the bites frankly, as penetration and worse. The point for our purposes is simpler: in 1897, when respectable fiction could not describe married desire, a novel about a Transylvanian count could be more explicit about wanting, and about the terror of a woman who wants back, than almost anything else on the shelf. The monster was permission. He still is; every 26 October the town fills with people in black, and the anxiety Stoker smuggled in has become a very good weekend.

**The house that would not be bought**
Du Maurier tried for years to buy Menabilly outright and never could; the Rashleighs would only ever lease. The single most cinematic image Whitby gave Stoker is real in outline. Stoker simply welded a local ghost story onto a real shipwreck and produced the most famous landing in gothic fiction.

Long before Stoker, Whitby Abbey was a place of women and story: it was the abbey of St Hild, where in the seventh century the herdsman Cædmon is said to have been given, in a dream, the gift of English song. Stoker knew the abbey also featured in Scott's *Marmion*, with its tale of a nun walled up alive. He arrived at a ruin already thick with legends of buried and haunted women, and added one more.

## 39 — Dylan and Caitlin: The Drink Story
*5 Cwmdonkin Drive, Uplands, Swansea · The Boathouse, Laugharne, Carmarthenshire · 1914–1994*

### BOOK VERSION
Read the name of the town backwards and you have the joke, and the whole man, in a single word. When Dylan Thomas came to invent the Welsh seaside town for his "play for voices", he first called it Llareggub — which, spelled out in reverse, is "bugger all", a filthy little schoolboy gag hidden in plain sight at the heart of what became *Under Milk Wood*, and later softened to "Llareggyb" by nervous editors who need not have bothered, since a Welsh speaker pronounces it the same either way. That is the key to Thomas: a poet of enormous, incantatory seriousness who was also, always, a man who would set a rude joke ticking under the floorboards and wait, delighted, for it to go off.

He was born on 27 October 1914 in the front bedroom of 5 Cwmdonkin Drive, a modest Edwardian semi in the Uplands district of Swansea, and lived there for his first twenty-three years. The house matters more than most writers' birthplaces, because of what happened in one small room. In a tiny back bedroom, from a desk overlooking the town and the bay, the teenage Thomas filled notebook after notebook — and by the reckoning of the Dylan Thomas Birthplace and his biographers, wrote roughly two-thirds of the poetry he would ever publish before he was twenty. His father D. J. Thomas, a frustrated, well-read schoolmaster, kept a study downstairs full of books, and it was there, sneaking volumes off the shelves, that the boy taught himself the English poetic tradition he would go on to overload with Welsh music. The house fell into semi-dereliction as a student bedsit and was rescued and restored to its 1914 condition by a local enthusiast, Geoff Haden, opening to visitors in 2008; you can now sleep in the room where the poems were written.

In the spring of 1936, in the Wheatsheaf pub in Fitzrovia, Thomas met Caitlin Macnamara, and the story of the rest of his life begins there. She was a dancer, trained in the free, barefoot Isadora Duncan style, spirited and beautiful and already the lover of the painter Augustus John, who made the introduction. By Caitlin's own account, the drunk young Welshman crossed the room, put his beer-heavy head in her lap, told her she was beautiful and that he loved her and was going to marry her — and meant, apparently, every word. They spent the next several days more or less in bed at a Fitzrovia hotel, the bill charged to Augustus John, and Caitlin described that first lovemaking, tenderly, as shy and child-like. They married on 11 July 1937 at Penzance register office, against his parents' wishes, and in 1938 settled in the small Carmarthenshire town of Laugharne — the "strangest town in Wales", he called it — which became the raw material for Llareggub.

What followed was not a tragedy that happened to Caitlin; it was a marriage two people made together and half-destroyed together, and she was more honest about it than anyone. It was, in her most famous phrase, a life of "raw, red bleeding meat" — passionate, jealous, drunken and physically violent on both sides. She said flatly that theirs was not so much a love story as a drink story, that without the "first-aid of drink" it could never have got onto its feet; the two of them were known as the Two Terrible Children. Both drank ruinously. Both had affairs — his, and then hers in furious retaliation. And Caitlin admitted, in her memoirs *Leftover Life to Kill* (1957) and the posthumous *Double Drink Story* (1997), that she instigated most of the fights and did most of the damage, and that it was extraordinary they had not killed each other outright. It is important to be fair to her, because biographers have not always been — Paul Ferris's 1993 life of her has been called a hatchet job, painting her as egocentric, calculating and to blame for driving her husband to drink. The truth the memoirs give us is harder and more human: a gifted, thwarted woman with literary ambitions and no outlet for them, married to a man whose gift was the one thing in the house that mattered, trapped for years as the mother of three children at the Boathouse — the beautiful cliff-ledge house above the Taf estuary that a patron, Margaret Taylor, bought for them in 1949 — with no running water and no way out.

The Boathouse, and the little writing shed on the cliff above it where Thomas worked among his crumpled drafts, is now the Dylan Thomas Boathouse, run by Carmarthenshire County Council. The tidy story is that he wrote *Under Milk Wood* there. He mostly did not — the research of David N. Thomas (first published in the *New Welsh Review*, spring 2001) established that fewer than three hundred lines of the play were written at Laugharne — but he wrote much of his greatest late poetry looking out at that estuary shore, and the bawdiness of *Under Milk Wood* is the town's own: Polly Garter singing of all her dead lovers, the draper Mr Waldo working through wives and scandals, Mrs Ogmore-Pritchard bullying her two dead husbands from beyond the grave. It is a dirty, tender, entirely unembarrassed portrait of a community's desires, written by a man who found sex funny and holy in about equal measure.

It ended in New York. On his fourth American reading tour, drinking hard and gravely ill, Thomas collapsed and fell into a coma; he died on 9 November 1953, aged thirty-nine. Caitlin flew out and arrived, by her own admission, "stinkin' drunk"; the scene at St Vincent's Hospital became legend — that she raged, that she fought the staff and bit an attendant, that she was restrained in a straitjacket and briefly committed. She said she could not remember the cruellest words later attributed to her. He was brought home and buried at Laugharne under a plain white cross. Caitlin's widowhood lasted forty years and most of it was spent in Italy, where she found a lasting partner, Giuseppe Fazio, eleven years her junior, gave birth to a fourth child in 1963 at the age of forty-nine, and eventually, in her sixties, stopped drinking. She died in Catania, Sicily, on 31 July 1994, and was brought back across Europe to lie beside the man she had loved and fought for fifteen years — under the same white cross, facing, at last, the water he wrote about.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The five-pound shed**

It is one of literature's great small rooms, and it survives, preserved as he left it. From its window he watched the herons and the tide over the estuary that runs through the late poems like a pulse.

**"Do not go gentle"**
Thomas wrote one of the most famous poems in English, addressed to his dying father — the fierce old schoolmaster of the Cwmdonkin Drive study, going blind and quiet. Its roaring refusal of death was a son's argument with the man who had taught him to read. D. J. Thomas died in December 1952; his son survived him by less than a year.

**A widow's request, refused**
Two years after Dylan's death, Caitlin wrote to the Home Office asking that his body be moved from what she called a "bleak extension" of Laugharne churchyard and reburied in the Boathouse garden, "facing the water that he loved and wrote about". She never paid the fee, and the plan lapsed. In the end she joined him where he already lay — the reunion happening on his terms, not hers, one last time.

## 40 — Doomed on the Page: The Wheelbarrow and the Love-Potion
*Stonehenge, Wiltshire · Tintagel, Cornwall · 1170s–1912*

### BOOK VERSION
Here is the most perfect illustration of Victorian squeamishness in the whole of English literature, and it is funny before it is anything else. When Thomas Hardy tried to get *Tess of the d'Urbervilles* into print, the magazines recoiled — one grandee complained the manuscript had "rather too much succulence" — and *The Graphic* would only serialise it in 1891 if he cleaned it up. So Hardy, with what he called "cynical amusement", set about bowdlerising his own masterpiece: he cut the seduction, cut Tess's baby, and — this is the immortal detail — rewrote a scene in which Angel Clare carries a group of dairymaids across a flooded lane in his arms, so that instead he trundles them across, one by one, in a wheelbarrow. A man carrying a woman was too much for the Victorian reader. A man pushing four women through the mud in a barrow was apparently fine. The whole absurd machinery of nineteenth-century censorship is there in that single substituted wheelbarrow, and it is worth enjoying, because it is the batch's gift.

But the reason the censorship matters is deadly serious, and it goes to the heart of what the novel is. *Tess of the d'Urbervilles* carries the subtitle *A Pure Woman Faithfully Presented*, and that subtitle was a deliberate provocation that scandalised reviewers, because the plot turns on Tess being sexually violated — the scene at the Chase, which Hardy wrote to be ambiguous between rape and seduction, and which scholars have argued about ever since. Hardy's point, the thing he was willing to be called immoral for, is that Tess remains pure regardless: that a woman's worth is not destroyed by what is done to her, and that a society which says otherwise is the guilty party. The violation must be treated as exactly that, a violation, with no gloss of romance on it; and the killing that comes much later — Tess stabbing her seducer, the blood spreading through a boarding-house ceiling — is consequence, not spectacle. Hardy keeps the murder itself offstage. What he insists we look at is the injustice.

And the novel's final movement is one of the strangest and most beautiful endings in English fiction. After the killing, Tess and Angel have a few days of fugitive happiness, and then, walking north by night, they come upon Stonehenge looming out of the dark. Tess, exhausted, lies down to sleep on one of the great flat stones — an altar, Hardy is careful to call it, a place of ancient sacrifice — and says she is "almost glad" it is ending. "One of my mother's people was a shepherd hereabout," she tells Angel; "and you used to say at Talbothays that I was a heathen. So now I am at home." At dawn the police close quietly in around the stones. She wakes and says, "I am ready." The oldest monument in England becomes the altar on which a "pure woman" is delivered up to a morality older and crueller than Christianity, and Hardy's famous, bitter closing line — that "the President of the Immortals… had ended his sport with Tess" — makes the whole cosmos complicit.

Hardy knew about love that arrives too late and love that outlives its object, because he lived it. His first marriage, to Emma Gifford, had curdled into decades of chilly estrangement under the same roof at Max Gate; after her death he found a diary she had kept, its contents said to be a long indictment of her husband, and had it destroyed. And then, undone by grief and remorse, the old man wrote for his dead wife the *Poems of 1912–13* — some of the most piercing love poetry in the language, addressed to a woman he had ignored for twenty years and could reach only now that she was a ghost. It is the exact pattern this whole chapter has been tracing: the greatest erotic writing coming from the thwarted, the estranged, the too-late.

For a love that was doomed from the very first draft, though, you must go to Cornwall, to the black cliffs of Tintagel — and here we have to separate the story from the stones, because almost everything romantic about the place is medieval invention layered onto a real but entirely un-Arthurian site. The legend is Tristan and Iseult, and it is one of the foundational adulterous love stories of Europe, older than or parallel to Lancelot and Guinevere, whose tale it partly shaped. Tristan, a knight of Cornwall, is sent to fetch the Irish princess Iseult to marry his uncle, King Mark; on the voyage the two of them drink, by mistake, a love-potion meant for the wedding night, and are bound helplessly together in an adulterous passion that destroys them all. It reaches us chiefly through the twelfth-century poems of Béroul and of Thomas of Britain, and through the great early-thirteenth-century German version by Gottfried von Strassburg — a story built, from the start, on desire that overrides law, loyalty and sense.

There may even be a real grave. A weathered sixth-century standing stone near Fowey, the Tristan Stone, carries a Latin inscription that reads, in the usual interpretation, DRUSTANUS HIC IACIT CUNOMORI FILIUS — "Drustanus lies here, son of Cunomorus" — and since "Drustanus" is an early form of Tristan, and a King Mark of Cornwall is elsewhere given the name Cunomorus, romantics have long wanted this to be Tristan's memorial. It is a genuine sixth-century monument and a genuine tantalising coincidence; it is not proof of anything, and an earlier antiquarian read the worn letters differently.

As for Tintagel Castle itself, the truth is better than the legend. The dramatic ruins on the headland are thirteenth-century, built in the 1230s by Richard, Earl of Cornwall — and here is the delicious inversion — apparently *because* of the Arthurian legend, not the other way round. Geoffrey of Monmouth had named Tintagel, around 1136, as the place where King Arthur was conceived; the site had no military value whatsoever; Richard, one of the richest men in Europe, seems to have swapped good manors for this useless, spectacular rock purely to buy himself a share of the myth. But underneath the medieval fantasy is something real and remarkable: excavations, including major digs in 2016–17 by the Cornwall Archaeological Unit, have shown that in the fifth to seventh centuries Tintagel was a wealthy, high-status settlement — very likely a seasonal seat of the kings of Dumnonia — trading directly with the Mediterranean, its ground littered with fine pottery and glass from North Africa and the eastern Mediterranean. More of this imported material has been found at Tintagel than at any other site in Britain; the 2016 season alone turned up more than two hundred pieces of Mediterranean pottery. The place was genuinely a seat of Cornish kings; it simply had nothing to do with a round table.

English Heritage now manages the headland, and its efforts to bring the legend alive have not always gone down well. A face of Merlin carved into the rock beneath the island, and then in 2016 an eight-foot bronze figure called *Gallos* — Cornish for "power" — a hooded, crowned, sword-holding spectre installed on the summit, drew accusations from Cornish nationalists and some historians of "Disneyfying" a profound site. English Heritage insists *Gallos* represents not King Arthur specifically but the whole layered history of the place, its real Dark Age kings included. It is, in its way, the perfect Tintagel row: an argument about invented tradition, staged on a rock that was mythologised once already, eight hundred years ago, by an earl who wanted a piece of a story that a Welsh cleric had made up a century before that. The desire came first. The stones came later, to match it. That is how the whole erotic map of Britain tends to work.

The novel's subtitle, *A Pure Woman Faithfully Presented*, was a deliberate provocation: its plot turns on Tess's sexual violation (a scene Hardy wrote to be ambiguous between rape and seduction, and still disputed by scholars), and Hardy insists on her purity against a society that would deny it. ### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**"Show me the exact spot"**
Tennyson, the great Victorian laureate, was a devotee of both these coasts. At Lyme he demanded to see where Louisa Musgrove fell; at Tintagel he walked the headland brooding on Arthur, and his *Idylls of the King* helped fuel the Victorian craze that turned a ruined Cornish rock into a national shrine. The tourists who fill Tintagel today are, in a sense, Tennyson's descendants — pilgrims to a story about a story.

**The diary Hardy burned**
Emma Hardy left behind a notebook, said to have been a long accounting of her grievances against her husband — decades of them. Hardy read it, and destroyed it, and then wrote her the tenderest elegies in the language. Two acts of a widower: one to make the record quieter, one to make it ache forever. The tidying and the confessing came from the same grieving man.

**A castle built for a legend**
Richard, Earl of Cornwall, was one of the richest men in thirteenth-century Europe, and the castle he built at Tintagel was militarily pointless — small, awkward, useless. He built it because Geoffrey of Monmouth had set Arthur's conception there. Within a decade the cliff was crumbling and the buildings were failing; by 1337 it was described as ruinous. He had spent a fortune to live inside a story, and the story outlasted the stones.
