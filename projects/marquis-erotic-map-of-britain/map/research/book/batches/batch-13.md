## 61 — The Discreet Square Mile: Shepherd Market
*Shepherd Market, Mayfair · London, England · c.1686–present*

### BOOK VERSION

Shepherd Market is reached by going somewhere quieter. Leave Piccadilly, slip down White Horse Street, and the traffic falls away into a knot of alleys that has spent three centuries making a business of not being noticed. Its origin was noisier. The May Fair brought cattle dealers, booths, gamblers, fighters and prostitutes to the open ground from the 1680s until it was finally suppressed in 1764. Edward Shepherd began building his market there in 1735, adding paved alleys, a duck pond and a two-storey market topped by a theatre. He removed the fair and kept the useful part: privacy.

The resulting precinct feels less like Mayfair than a village that has wandered into it by mistake. There are pubs, restaurants and small shops, with the streets pinched closely enough to make every doorway feel private. Writers noticed. Anthony Powell lodged his narrator Nick Jenkins opposite a block of flats inhabited, in the novel's phrase, almost exclusively by tarts. Olivia Manning lived at 50a and found the neighbouring women fascinating. Shepherd Market offered the rare London privilege of watching without being watched back.

The mechanics of the trade are worth setting out plainly, because they explain how it survived so long. The classic Mayfair arrangement was the "walk-up": a flat above a shop, reached by its own staircase, with a discreet card by the bell — "Model," most often, that beautifully useless euphemism — and frequently a "maid" on the premises, who was not a maid but a combination of receptionist, bookkeeper and lookout. A man walked up; hence the name. No soliciting in the street was required, no loitering on a corner, nothing for a passing constable to see. And that turned out to matter enormously, because in 1959 the law changed in a way that made the walk-up not merely convenient but standard.

The Street Offences Act 1959 was the government's response to the Wolfenden Committee, which had been asked, in the words of the Home Secretary who appointed it, to do something about a state of the West End streets that "was a scandal and a disgrace." The Act made it an offence for a "common prostitute to loiter or solicit in a street or public place," stiffened the penalties, and — as everyone including Wolfenden himself predicted — drove the trade indoors rather than out of existence. The numbers are startling: in 1958, the last full year before the Act, there were 19,663 prosecutions of prostitutes for street offences; by 1966 the figure was 2,137. The women had not vanished. They had gone upstairs. The proliferation of "call-girls" and the "thinly disguised advertisements of various kinds," as one peer put it in 1968, was the direct and foreseen consequence. Shepherd Market, with its ready-made stock of small flats over small shops, was perfectly equipped for the new dispensation. What the Georgian bagnios of Covent Garden had been to the eighteenth century, the Mayfair walk-up was to the twentieth: the same trade, licensed by nothing but discretion, taxed by nothing but rent, and tolerated precisely because it kept quiet.

Privacy attracted organised crime as well as writers. By the late 1940s the Messina brothers controlled a large network of Mayfair and Soho brothels and, by police estimates, at least two hundred women. The women surrendered most of their earnings; the brothers benefited from corruption and the difficulty of proving who controlled what behind a row of separate front doors. The operation was exposed in September 1950 by Duncan Webb of *The People*, who interviewed women, traced the flats and named the men. Several brothers fled and the organisation unravelled through arrests and deportations. The trade did not disappear. It changed management.

The single most famous scandal to run through Shepherd Market came later and involved no gangsters at all, only a novelist with a genius for self-destruction. In September 1986 a prostitute named Monica Coghlan picked up a client here; a businessman who saw them identified the client as Jeffrey Archer, then deputy chairman of the Conservative Party. When the *Daily Star* alleged that Archer had paid Coghlan for sex, he sued for libel, swore in court that he had never met her, and in July 1987 won £500,000 in damages — the highest sum ever granted in a British libel case, helped along by the judge's now-notorious summing-up, in which he invited the jury to consider whether Archer's wife had "elegance" and "fragrance." It took thirteen years to unravel. In 1999 a former friend admitted providing Archer with a false alibi; Archer was charged, and in July 2001 he was convicted at the Old Bailey of perjury and perverting the course of justice and sentenced to four years. Monica Coghlan did not live to see it. She had been killed in a car crash in April 2001, weeks before the trial began. Archer was believed first and jailed only after people in his own circle broke ranks. Coghlan, disbelieved in court and dead before his conviction, never saw that correction.

The old trade is far less visible now, and restaurants and estate agents dominate the particulars. The alleys have changed less than the businesses. Shepherd Market still feels like a place where London lowers its voice without quite knowing why.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)
*shepherd-market-mayfair*

Mayfair is named after a fair — a raucous fortnight of cattle-trading, gambling and prostitution that gathered on this ground from the 1680s until it was suppressed for public disorder, first presented by a Grand Jury in 1708 and finally killed off around 1764. The architect Edward Shepherd bought the site and built his little grid of alleys and squares between 1735 and 1746. The fair went; its reputation stayed. For roughly 250 years Shepherd Market was the most genteel red-light district in Britain — discreet, costly, and tolerated in the richest square mile in the country.

The characteristic Mayfair arrangement was the "walk-up": a flat above a shop, a card by the bell reading "Model," a "maid" inside to manage callers. It required no soliciting in the street, and so it survived the Street Offences Act 1959, which — following the Wolfenden Report — drove street prostitution indoors. Prosecutions of prostitutes fell from 19,663 in 1958 to 2,137 by 1966; the women had gone upstairs, and Shepherd Market's stock of small flats was ready for them.

The area sat at the centre of organised vice, too. The five Messina brothers controlled around thirty brothels across Mayfair and Soho by the late 1940s, taking eighty per cent of their women's earnings, until Duncan Webb of *The People* exposed them on 3 September 1950 under the headline "Arrest These Four Men." And in 1986 it was here that Jeffrey Archer met the prostitute Monica Coghlan — the encounter behind his 1987 libel win against the *Daily Star* and, ultimately, his 2001 conviction for perjury.

The walk-ups have largely gone, and Shepherd Market today is a discreet village of restaurants and boutiques, with two Grade II listed pubs — Ye Grapes and Shepherds Tavern — surviving from its long double life. Writers loved it: Anthony Powell, Michael Arlen and Olivia Manning all lived or set fiction here. It keeps its secrets still.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The novelist opposite the flats.** Anthony Powell's narrator in *A Dance to the Music of Time* rents rooms in Shepherd Market "opposite a block of flats inhabited almost exclusively by tarts," and describes the dawn light on the alleys as touched "like another Stonehenge." Powell knew the area first-hand in the 1920s and 1930s, when it was cheap, noisy and thoroughly disreputable — long before oligarchs discovered Mayfair.

**Ye Grapes.** The current pub dates from 1882, but there has been a tavern on the spot since the eighteenth century. Cluttered with taxidermy and dark wood, it has always traded on discretion: no celebrity photographs on the walls, no fuss. Locals prize it precisely because it is the sort of place where the famous can drink unbothered — a fitting survivor in a quarter built on not being seen.

**The fatal flat round the corner.** At 9 Curzon Place, a few steps away, the singer Cass Elliot of the Mamas and the Papas died in July 1974; four years later, in the same flat, Keith Moon of the Who died of an overdose. Shepherd Market has always drawn those who wanted the West End on their doorstep and privacy at their back — with occasionally tragic results.

## 62 — The Money in His Pocket: Cleveland Street, 1889
*19 Cleveland Street, Fitzrovia · London, England · 1889–1890*

### BOOK VERSION

Accounts differ over whether Police Constable Luke Hanks found fourteen or eighteen shillings in the pocket of fifteen-year-old telegraph messenger Charles Swinscow on 4 July 1889. Either sum was large enough, and sufficiently irregular, to demand an explanation. Swinscow gave one. He had earned the money at 19 Cleveland Street, a house run by Charles Hammond where he and other Post Office boys were paid to have sex with wealthy men. The scandal began with the cash in a child's pocket. Everything that followed turned on the difference between what happened to the boys who took it and the men who paid it.

The boys were children and adolescents, roughly fifteen to nineteen years old, drawn from the poorest end of the labour market. Telegraph messenger was a respectable job for a working-class boy and it paid very little. A clerk named Henry Newlove, himself still a teenager, recruited others from the Central Telegraph Office to supplement their wages at Cleveland Street. This was the sexual exploitation of poor children by rich men, and the unequal consequences are the reason the place belongs on the map.

The investigation was handed to Inspector Frederick Abberline of Scotland Yard — the same detective who, the previous year, had led the hunt for Jack the Ripper. Abberline obtained warrants under Section 11 of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885, the Labouchere Amendment, which criminalised gross indecency between men. Six years later the same section would destroy Oscar Wilde. Cleveland Street had already shown how differently it treated poor witnesses and powerful clients. When officers reached Cleveland Street on 6 July, Hammond had already fled — tipped off by Newlove — and he made his way abroad, eventually to the United States. The British government pointedly declined to seek his extradition. A brothel-keeper willing to name his clients in open court was the last thing anyone in authority wanted.

For the clients were the problem. On the way to the police station, Newlove named names, and they were extraordinary ones: Lord Arthur Somerset, a major in the Royal Horse Guards and an equerry to the Prince of Wales; Henry FitzRoy, Earl of Euston; and an army colonel named Jervois. Somerset — identified by the boys as a "Mr Brown" who had visited the house — was interviewed by police in August 1889 and then, while the authorities conspicuously dragged their feet, allowed to slip abroad. By October he was in France. He never came back, living out his life in exile on the Continent and dying at Hyères in 1926. No client was ever prosecuted.

The boys were another matter. Newlove and a co-defendant, George Veck — a man who posed as a clergyman, dog-collar and all — pleaded guilty at the Old Bailey in September 1889 to procuring; Newlove received four months' hard labour, Veck nine. The telegraph boys themselves were treated with a leniency that the radical press rightly found suspicious, and which was widely believed to have been the price of their silence. But leniency is a relative term. They were named, they were disgraced, and in December 1889 the Post Office formally dismissed Swinscow, Thickbroom, Wright and Perkins from their jobs — a fact recorded in sworn testimony at a later trial. There was also an attempt, organised through Somerset's solicitor Arthur Newton, to bribe several of the boys to emigrate to America or Australia and so remove them as witnesses. That scheme failed: the boys tipped off the police, Swinscow's own parents raised the alarm, and Newton was later convicted of obstruction and sentenced to six weeks in prison. It is a small, telling detail. The establishment's instinct was not to help these children but to ship them out of the country.

The rumour that has clung to Cleveland Street for more than a century concerns Prince Albert Victor, Duke of Clarence, the eldest son of the Prince of Wales and second in line to the throne. It must be stated plainly: there is no evidence whatever that he ever visited 19 Cleveland Street. The allegation appears to have originated in a threat by Somerset's own solicitor that a "very distinguished person" would be implicated if Somerset were prosecuted — a piece of leverage, in other words, not a piece of evidence. The British press never named the prince; only overseas papers hinted. What the rumour did was corrupt the handling of the case from the top down: the Prince of Wales's own household worked to shut the investigation off, and the fear of where the trail might lead unquestionably helped the aristocratic suspects escape. The prince was created Duke of Clarence the following year and died of influenza in 1892. The rumour has coloured his biographers ever since, and it remains exactly that — a rumour, unsupported, and repeated here only to be set aside.

The one man who tried to make the establishment answer for it was a journalist, and the establishment made him pay. Ernest Parke, the young radical editor of the *North London Press*, questioned in print why the boys had been given such light sentences while their aristocratic clients walked free, and in November 1889 named Somerset and the Earl of Euston. Euston — who, unlike Somerset, had not left the country — sued for criminal libel. At the trial in January 1890 he admitted going to the house, but claimed he had been handed a card advertising *poses plastiques* (nude tableaux of women), had realised the true nature of the place and left at once. The jury convicted Parke and he received a year in prison. The journalist who forced the aristocratic names into print went to prison. No client did.

There was a reckoning of sorts in Parliament, though it changed nothing. On 28 February 1890 Henry Labouchère — the same radical Liberal MP who had authored the very "gross indecency" law under which the boys had been snared — moved in the Commons for an inquiry into what he alleged was a government conspiracy to protect the guilty and let Somerset escape. He was rebutted by the Attorney General, Sir Richard Webster, who had himself been prosecuting the case. The debate ran for some seven hours; the motion was heavily defeated, by 206 votes to 66. Labouchère, refusing to withdraw the remark "I do not believe Lord Salisbury" — a plain accusation that the Prime Minister was lying — was named by the Speaker and suspended from the House. The state closed ranks and the matter was allowed to die.

Cleveland Street runs today between Euston Road and Goodge Street, an ordinary Fitzrovia thoroughfare. Number 19 itself was long ago swept away in the expansion of the Middlesex Hospital, which occupied the block until it closed in 2005 and was demolished in the years after; the site is now luxury flats. A few doors along, at what is now number 44, the old Cleveland Street Workhouse still stands — the last Georgian workhouse in London, and probably the building young Charles Dickens had in mind for *Oliver Twist*. The surviving workhouse is a fitting neighbour. Both buildings belonged to a city that knew how to use the poor and how to hide the people who used them. The boys lost their jobs in December 1889. No client was prosecuted.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)
*cleveland-street-scandal*

In July 1889 a fifteen-year-old telegraph messenger, Charles Swinscow, was found with an unusually large sum in his pocket during a police inquiry into a theft at the Central Telegraph Office. Published accounts disagree over whether it was fourteen or eighteen shillings. Questioned by Constable Luke Hanks, he admitted he had earned it at 19 Cleveland Street, a male brothel run by Charles Hammond where Post Office boys, aged roughly fifteen to nineteen, were paid to have sex with wealthy men. This was not a scandal of equals. It was the exploitation of poor adolescents by rich men, and the law's response proved it.

Inspector Frederick Abberline, fresh from the Ripper case, ran the investigation under the 1885 Labouchère Amendment — the same statute that would destroy Oscar Wilde six years later. Hammond was tipped off and fled abroad; the government declined to extradite him. The named clients included Lord Arthur Somerset, equerry to the Prince of Wales, who was allowed to slip to France and never returned, dying in exile in 1926. No client was ever prosecuted.

The boys were. Henry Newlove and George Veck received four and nine months; the telegraph boys were treated leniently — widely believed the price of silence — but were named, disgraced and dismissed by the Post Office in December 1889. Persistent rumours linked Prince Albert Victor, Duke of Clarence, to the house; there is no evidence he ever went there, and the claim seems to have begun as a solicitor's threat, not a fact. It nonetheless helped panic the establishment into shutting the case down.

The journalist Ernest Parke, who exposed the cover-up and named the Earl of Euston, was himself jailed for a year for libel after Euston sued. In Parliament on 28 February 1890, Henry Labouchère's motion alleging a government conspiracy was crushed and he was suspended from the House.

Number 19 was demolished with the Middlesex Hospital; the site is now flats. Nearby survives the Georgian Cleveland Street Workhouse, thought to have inspired *Oliver Twist* — an apt neighbour to a story about how London used and discarded its poor.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The clergyman who wasn't.** George Veck, Hammond's right-hand man, moved through the case dressed as a vicar, complete with dog collar, and styled himself "the Reverend." He had once worked at the telegraph office himself. Convicted of procuring, he drew nine months' hard labour. The costume fooled no one for long, but it captures the seedy theatricality of the whole affair — respectability worn as a disguise.

**The law that waited for Wilde.** The Cleveland Street boys were charged under Section 11 of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885, the "gross indecency" clause inserted by Henry Labouchère. Six years later the same section would convict Oscar Wilde and send him to Reading Gaol. Cleveland Street established the pattern the Wilde trials would follow: homosexuality framed as aristocratic corruption of the young.

**Dickens's workhouse.** A few doors from the vanished brothel, at 44 Cleveland Street, stands the last surviving Georgian workhouse in London, built in the 1770s. Charles Dickens lived nearby as a child, and historians believe this grim H-shaped building lies behind the workhouse of *Oliver Twist*. Now Grade II listed, it has been the subject of a long redevelopment fight over the human remains in its old burial ground.

## 63 — The Golden Calf: London's First Nightclub
*Cave of the Golden Calf, 9 Heddon Street · London, England · 1912–1914*

### BOOK VERSION

Eric Gill carved the calf. It stood in the foyer — a gilded, frankly phallic little idol of the biblical golden calf, the thing the Israelites worshipped while Moses was up the mountain, the very symbol of forbidden pleasure and false gods — and it told you at the door exactly what sort of establishment you had entered. Beyond it, down a staircase past a huge Wyndham Lewis painting of a carnival, you descended into a low-ceilinged basement blazing with colour: Spencer Gore and Charles Ginner had covered the walls with hunting scenes, tigers and jungles; Jacob Epstein had sheathed the iron support pillars in painted plaster caryatids, human and animal forms with the heads of hawks, cats and camels; Lewis had painted a drop-curtain depicting raw meat. This was the Cave of the Golden Calf, and it opened in a basement at 9 Heddon Street, just off Regent Street, on 26 June 1912. It was, by most reckonings, the first true nightclub in London — a place designed for artists and bohemians to drink, dance and misbehave after the theatres had closed — and it was the creation of one of the most formidable women in Edwardian London.

Frida Strindberg, born Frida Uhl in Vienna in 1872, was the divorced second wife of the Swedish playwright August Strindberg, though calling her merely that does her a disservice. She was a translator, journalist and impresario who had already helped start cabaret in Germany, and she arrived in London trailing a reputation for headlong passions — she had, among other things, twice threatened a former lover with a revolver. One acquaintance called her, with what sounds like real fear, "an amazingly masterful, intelligent, and in her way fascinating Austrian" with "a mesmeric faculty for getting people to do things for her." What she got people to do was build her a nightclub. Modelled on the Cabaret Fledermaus in her native Vienna, the Cabaret Theatre Club — to give it its official name — was to be a place "given up to gaiety," a European-style *cabaret artistique* of a kind London had never seen.

London had dance halls, theatres and private clubs. It had not had this combination: ragtime and tango in a painted basement, aristocrats beside artists, and a serious programme of new theatre and music running above the drinking and dancing. Members paid to get in, and members also had access to a genuinely serious cultural programme upstairs in the club's guise as the Cabaret Theatre Club — Sunday performances of plays by Aristophanes, Dostoevsky and Strindberg himself, and a music programme that ran to Mozart, Pergolesi and, remarkably, Schoenberg's *Pierrot Lunaire*, staged in London only months after its Berlin premiere. Ezra Pound was a fixture and reportedly got his meals free if he took them at Frida's own table; Katherine Mansfield, Ford Madox Ford, Augustus John and Wyndham Lewis all passed through. Osbert Sitwell remembered it as a "super-heated Vorticist garden of gesticulating figures." The Cave sits in the direct line of London bohemia that would later wash up at the Fitzroy Tavern in Bloomsbury — the same collision of art, money, drink and sexual latitude, a generation earlier and several notches more expensive.

The Cave's life was brief because Frida's arithmetic was hopeless. The decorations cost a fortune, the artists were not always paid, and Strindberg subsidised struggling writers out of a till that was already emptying. The club went bankrupt and closed in early 1914, less than two years after it opened. Strindberg herself left for America in 1914, the club's collapse coinciding with her departure. She died in 1943, her extraordinary two-year experiment in London nightlife long forgotten by almost everyone.

Almost none of the interior survives. Spencer Gore's murals, presumably painted on canvas, have vanished without trace; Ginner's jungle scenes are lost; Epstein's painted pillars went with the building's later gutting; Lewis's raw-meat curtain is gone. What remains is fragmentary and precious. Wyndham Lewis's *design* for the drop-curtain survives in the Victoria and Albert Museum, along with the club's printed programme and menu, now held by the Museum of London; Gore's preparatory oil studies for the murals are in public collections including the Tate. Of the actual interior — the thing itself, the blazing painted cave — there is essentially nothing. One of the most important interiors in the history of British modernism was thrown up in a basement, enjoyed for eighteen months, and thrown away.

Heddon Street, though, is still there, a short pedestrianised courtyard off Regent Street now lined with restaurants. Sixty years later Brian Ward photographed David Bowie on the same short street for the cover of *The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars*. Heddon Street helped London invent the look of its nightlife twice. Neither invention left much of itself behind.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)
*cave-golden-calf-heddon-street*

London's first true nightclub opened in a basement at 9 Heddon Street, off Regent Street, on 26 June 1912. The Cave of the Golden Calf — officially the Cabaret Theatre Club — was the creation of Frida Strindberg, the Austrian former wife of the playwright August Strindberg and a woman of legendary energy and chaos. Modelled on Viennese cabaret, it was a place for artists and bohemians to drink and dance after the theatres closed, and it was decorated by the finest avant-garde artists of the day.

Eric Gill carved the phallic golden calf that stood at the entrance and designed the club's sign; Spencer Gore and Charles Ginner painted jungle and hunting murals; Jacob Epstein sheathed the pillars in painted plaster figures; Wyndham Lewis contributed a drop-curtain and a huge carnival painting for the stairs. The name, taken from the biblical idol of forbidden worship, was deliberately blasphemous.

At night the Cave gave Londoners ragtime and the tango, danced in a basement until dawn, alongside a serious cultural programme that ran to Schoenberg's *Pierrot Lunaire* and plays by Dostoevsky and Strindberg. Ezra Pound, Katherine Mansfield, Ford Madox Ford and Augustus John all frequented it. It was fashionable, cosmopolitan and ruinously expensive to run.

It was also hopelessly managed. Strindberg subsidised artists, ran up debts, and the club went bankrupt and closed in early 1914, less than two years after opening. She left for America that year and died in 1943. Almost all the artwork is lost — Gore's and Ginner's murals vanished, Epstein's pillars and Lewis's curtain gone. Wyndham Lewis's curtain *design* survives at the V&A and the club's programme at the Museum of London, but of the interior itself, nothing.

Heddon Street survives as a pedestrian courtyard of restaurants — and, by a remarkable coincidence, is where David Bowie was photographed for the cover of *Ziggy Stardust* in 1972, marked by a plaque at number 23 since 2012. One short street produced two revolutions in British style, sixty years apart.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The calf that offended.** Eric Gill's golden calf was, by every account, unmistakably phallic — a sculptor's joke about idolatry and appetite planted in a Mayfair doorway. Gill designed both the free-standing statue in the foyer and the relief version on the sign outside, flanked by figures. Like nearly all the Cave's art it is lost, but it set the tone: this was a temple to pleasure, and it said so at the door.

**Pierrot Lunaire in a basement.** For all its reputation as a den of ragtime and champagne, the Cave took art seriously. Its cultural programme staged Schoenberg's *Pierrot Lunaire* only months after the work's 1912 Berlin premiere — an astonishing thing to find in a London nightclub. Frida Strindberg wanted gaiety "stimulating thought, rather than crushing it," and for eighteen months, improbably, she got it.

**Ziggy's street.** Sixty years after the Cave closed, the photographer Brian Ward shot David Bowie as Ziggy Stardust outside 23 Heddon Street on a rainy January night in 1972. The band declined to pose in the cold, so Bowie stood alone under the K. West sign. A plaque unveiled in 2012 marks the spot — one of very few in London honouring a fictional character.

## 64 — If It Moves, It's Rude: Clubs and the Censors
*Windmill Theatre, Raymond Revuebar and Stringfellows · Soho and Covent Garden, London · 1932 to present*

### BOOK VERSION

Soho performed one of its familiar disappearances in 2025. In February, Westminster approved a new sexual-entertainment venue for the old Madame Jojo's premises in Walker's Court. In September, the company operating the Windmill went into liquidation and the doors closed after a dispute with its landlord. By April 2026 the Windmill was open again, now as Rhino at the Windmill under Spearmint Rhino. Nothing had vanished except an operator. The address, the licence and the argument carried on.

The old argument turned on a rule of such perfect British absurdity that it hardly needs improving. A nude woman on a public stage was permissible provided she did not move. A living statue was art; a moving one was obscene. The Lord Chamberlain's Office licensed the stage, examined proposed tableaux and required the performers to hold their poses. Decency had become a matter of velocity, and an industry grew up around keeping the body still while everything around it moved.

The Windmill got there first. Laura Henderson bought the former Palais de Luxe cinema and opened it as a theatre in 1931. Her manager, Vivian Van Damm, launched the continuous-variety programme Revudeville in February 1932. Later that year the motionless nude tableaux arrived and changed the business. Britannia, goddesses and other classical excuses stood under subdued light while comedians worked around them. During the war the theatre adopted the boast "We Never Closed", apart from the compulsory closure imposed on London theatres in September 1939. London immediately improved the slogan to "We Never Clothed". The Windmill remained a nursery for comedians until it closed as a theatre in 1964. It later returned to nude revue, table dancing and other forms of nightlife, lost its sexual-entertainment licence in 2018, reopened under a different concept, closed with its operator in 2025 and returned again in 2026. A venue famous for never closing has become very good at reopening.

Paul Raymond found the more profitable loophole. In 1958 he opened the Raymond Revuebar in Walker's Court as a private members' club. The Lord Chamberlain licensed public theatres, not private clubs, so inside Raymond's basement the women could move. Membership reached tens of thousands and the neon announced the "World Centre of Erotic Entertainment". A 1961 conviction for keeping a disorderly house did little to slow him. The money went into Soho property, which proved more durable than any show. The Revuebar closed in 2004. Raymond died four years later, having made a fortune less from defeating censorship than from owning the ground beneath it.

The Theatres Act 1968 abolished the Lord Chamberlain's stage censorship, but regulation did not disappear. It became local. Sexual-entertainment licences, no-touching conditions, planning rules and residents' objections replaced the courtier and his rubber stamp. Peter Stringfellow's club, opened in Covent Garden in 1980 and converted to table dancing in the 1990s, belonged to this new world. Its trade operated openly, subject to council paperwork rather than a fiction about motionless art. Stringfellow called himself a feminist. The claim need not detain us. The important change was administrative: what had once been obscenity became a licensed use of premises.

That is where the story stands now. Walker's Court has a newly granted licence. The Windmill has a new operator and a new sign. The Lord Chamberlain has gone, but committees still decide hours, visibility, touching, security and the precise route by which performers leave at night. Soho has not become respectable or disreputable. It has become regulated in a different vocabulary, which is one of its oldest tricks.

### WEB VERSION — stringfellows-club-scene
**The Windmill Theatre**

The Windmill Theatre on Great Windmill Street was where Britain learned to undress on stage while keeping just inside the law. Laura Henderson bought the former Palais de Luxe cinema and, with her manager Vivian Van Damm, launched the continuous variety show Revudeville in 1932. From 1933 it added the nude tableaux vivants that made it famous: the "Windmill Girls," posed as motionless living statues. The Lord Chamberlain's rule was that a stationary nude was art and a moving one was obscene — so the Windmill's women simply held very still.

Its wartime boast was "We Never Closed" — it stayed open through the Blitz — which London promptly turned into "We're Never Clothed." It was also a celebrated nursery for British comedians, who honed their acts before distracted audiences. The theatre closed in 1964, unable to compete with Soho's newer and racier venues.

In 1974 it passed to the Soho impresario Paul Raymond, who ran nude revues and, later, table dancing. The club lost its adult-entertainment licence in 2018 after an undercover investigation found dancers breaking "no-touching" rules, reopened under a different concept and closed again in September 2025 when its operator went into liquidation amid a dispute with its landlord. By April 2026 it was trading again as Rhino at the Windmill under Spearmint Rhino; Westminster renewed and varied its premises licence in July.

The Windmill's real significance is as the first and cleverest exploitation of Britain's absurd stage-nudity rules — the idea, seriously enforced by the Lord Chamberlain's Office until 1968, that decency was a question of movement. Photographs of proposed poses were submitted for approval and marked "YES" or "NO"; the lighting was kept dim; the performers were not allowed to smile. The 1968 Theatres Act abolished theatre censorship altogether, and the whole ingenious system became history. A venue famous for never closing has instead become very good at reopening.

**The Raymond Revuebar**

Paul Raymond opened the Raymond Revuebar in Walker's Court, Soho, on 21 April 1958, and built an empire on a legal loophole. The Lord Chamberlain's Office censored *public* theatres, where nude performers were forbidden to move. A private members' club fell outside that jurisdiction — so Raymond made the Revuebar a members' club, signed up 45,000 members within two years, and legally showed the full-frontal, moving nudity available nowhere else in London. Its neon sign called it the "World Centre of Erotic Entertainment," and it became a Soho landmark.

Born Geoffrey Quinn in Liverpool in 1925, Raymond was a former mind-reader and variety promoter with a genius for publicity and property. The Revuebar drew a smart early crowd and regular trouble from the authorities — in 1961 he was convicted of keeping a "disorderly house" and fined — but the real money went into Soho real estate. As police closed other premises, Raymond bought them, street by street; *The Sunday Times* named him the richest man in Britain in 1992.

The irony is exquisite: the censorship Raymond had so cleverly evaded was abolished in 1968 by the Theatres Act, which stripped the Lord Chamberlain of powers held since 1737. His fortune, in the end, rested on property, not nudity. He largely withdrew from public life after his daughter Debbie died of a drug overdose in 1992, and became a recluse.

The Revuebar closed on 10 June 2004 and passed through several names — Too2Much, the Soho Revue Bar — before reopening in 2011 as The Box Soho, a "theatre of varieties." Raymond died in 2008. His life became the 2013 film *The Look of Love*, with Steve Coogan. The Revuebar's story is Soho's central lesson in miniature: nudity in Britain was never simply banned or allowed, but endlessly regulated — and endlessly, profitably, evaded.

**Stringfellows**

Peter Stringfellow opened Stringfellows on Upper St Martin's Lane, Covent Garden, in 1980 — first as a glossy celebrity disco, restaurant and cocktail bar. The son of a Sheffield steelworker, he had begun in the 1960s booking bands (the Beatles, the Kinks, Jimi Hendrix) into northern clubs before moving to London, and his Covent Garden venue was an immediate hit with film stars, models and rock musicians. Clubs in New York, Miami and Beverly Hills followed.

In the 1990s Stringfellow reinvented the club as a "table-dancing" venue, importing the format from his New York operation, and became the gold-throned, long-haired public face of upmarket British striptease. By then the business worked quite differently from Paul Raymond's era: the Lord Chamberlain's stage censorship had been abolished by the 1968 Theatres Act, and adult venues operated under council-issued licences and conditions rather than in defiance of a royal official. Regulation had shifted from the courtier to the committee.

Stringfellow defended his trade in the language of the sexual revolution, claiming his clubs had "broken down the fear a lot of people have about sex." Critics disagreed sharply; when friends later sought to honour him on Sheffield's Walk of Fame, a campaign group objected that he was "synonymous with the total glamorisation of the strip industry." He married three times, had children in his seventies, and described himself, without evident irony, as a feminist and a Conservative.

He revealed in 2015 that he had secretly fought cancer since 2008, and died on 7 June 2018, aged 77, still styled the "King of Clubs." His eponymous Covent Garden club continued to operate. Stringfellows completes Soho and Covent Garden's long argument about nudity and the law: what the Lord Chamberlain once forbade unless the performer stood perfectly still, and what Paul Raymond made legal through a members'-club loophole, had become, by Stringfellow's day, simply another licensed trade — regulated, taxed, and entirely out in the open.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The photographs marked "NO".** The Lord Chamberlain's examiners required photographs of every proposed nude tableau, submitted in advance and annotated by hand. Many survive in the Lord Chamberlain's Plays Correspondence at the British Library, some neatly marked "YES" or "NO." The lighting had to be subdued, the poses static, and the performers forbidden to smile. It is bureaucratic censorship at its most gloriously ridiculous — decency reduced to a filing exercise.

**Comedy's greenroom.** Behind the Windmill's nude tableaux ran a serious variety bill, and it launched an extraordinary number of British comedians who cut their teeth playing to impatient houses. Learning to hold an audience that had come for something else was, by common consent, the hardest school in show business — and produced some of the toughest and best comic performers of the post-war years.

**The Comic Strip in the Revuebar.** In 1980 the Boulevard Theatre inside Raymond's Revuebar was hired by Peter Richardson to stage The Comic Strip, the alternative-comedy revue that launched Rik Mayall, Adrian Edmondson, Dawn French, Jennifer Saunders and Alexei Sayle. Later Eddie Izzard ran a stand-up room there. Soho's temple of striptease was also, briefly, the cradle of a comedy revolution — the two trades sharing a staircase.

## 65 — The Living Site: Hampstead Heath
*West Heath, Hampstead · London, England · nineteenth century–present*

### BOOK VERSION

Hampstead Heath did not out George Michael. A public lavatory in Beverly Hills did that in 1998. The Heath entered his story eight years later, when a tabloid photographed him leaving the West Heath at night and presented the discovery that he cruised as a fresh scandal. Michael refused the role offered to him. In 2006 he called the matter private, defended anonymous no-strings sex and said the police pragmatically accepted what happened there at night. In 2009 he described the Heath as a nicer place for "quick and honest sex" than a bar. In 2011 he put the principle in capitals: "I HAVE NEVER AND WILL NEVER APOLOGISE FOR MY SEX LIFE." The statements belong to different moments, but the position never changed.

The ground is the West Heath, a wooded corner of Hampstead Heath that has been used as a gay cruising place since at least the nineteenth century. Historic England describes it as one of the most notable such sites in Europe. Before legal meeting places were available, parks, alleys and dark ground offered men a chance to find one another beyond the eyes of families, employers and police. The West Heath endured because it was large, wooded and close to the city while feeling outside it.

For much of the twentieth century there was no accommodation, only surveillance and arrest. Police operations continued through the 1970s and 1980s, and exposure could cost a man his work, home and family. Attitudes changed unevenly. By 2006 Michael could describe a practical tolerance in which the police knew what happened at night and did not treat every encounter as a case to be manufactured. Cruising itself is not an offence. Public sexual conduct can still fall within the criminal law, so the Heath's settlement has always been one of discretion rather than permission.

The truce is not permanent, and it is not uncontested, as 2025 made clear. In February that year anonymous posters appeared on the West Heath urging "dog walkers and residents" to "take back" the area, one of them reading, "Cruising for sex? Homosexuality is legal. Use a hook-up app like Grindr or Sniffies and get a room." The signs were torn down, furiously, by locals who took a live-and-let-live view, and the row made national news. In response, on 2 March 2025, a group of campaigners staged a satirical protest march to defend the site's history and its continued use — and they called it the Sodomites' Walk.

The name is a deliberate, learned borrowing, and it reaches back to the very beginning of this book's story of queer London. In the early eighteenth century a path in Moorfields, running along the wall that separated the Upper Field from the Middle Field, was known — the term is contemporary, not invented — as the Sodomites' Walk. It was one of Georgian London's most notorious cruising grounds, documented in the work of the historian Rictor Norton, whose book on the molly houses is a foundation of the subject. It was there, in 1726, that a man named William Brown was arrested by an agent provocateur for "attempted sodomy," and it was there that he gave the answer that ought to be carved somewhere permanent: asked why he had taken such liberties, he said "I think there is no crime in making what use I please of my own body." The wall came down in 1752; the path survives as the south side of Finsbury Square. When campaigners borrowed the name in 2025, they linked the Heath to an older claim made under much greater danger: that a person's body is not the state's property.

This is the right place to finish the numbered map because it is not a ruin, a plaque or a prosecution file. The West Heath is still used, defended and disputed in the present tense. The molly house vanished under a road and the Caravan survives in police photographs. Here the space itself remains. The final entry is not something Britain once did. It is somewhere people still go tonight.

### WEB VERSION — hampstead-west-heath

The West Heath, a wooded corner in the south-west of Hampstead Heath, has been one of the best-known outdoor gay cruising grounds in Britain since the nineteenth century — when homosexuality was illegal and cruising was, for many men, the only way to meet. Historic England's LGBTQ heritage project records it as a site of national, indeed European, significance, part of a London tradition that once took in St James's Park and Hyde Park.

For decades there were only arrests. Through the 1970s and 1980s the Heath saw police operations and entrapment, and men were charged and disgraced. The community resisted — from the Gay Liberation Front onward — and from the 2000s an informal accommodation grew up with the City of London Corporation, which has managed the Heath since 1989, over lighting, undergrowth and policing. Raids largely became a thing of the past.

The truce is fragile. In February 2025 anonymous posters urged residents to "take back" the West Heath, telling gay men to "use a hook-up app… and get a room." Locals tore them down, and on 2 March 2025 campaigners staged a satirical protest march in defence of the site, naming it the Sodomites' Walk — after a notorious eighteenth-century cruising ground in Moorfields, documented by the historian Rictor Norton, where in 1726 William Brown declared "there is no crime in making what use I please of my own body."

The Heath is also bound up with George Michael, though the details are often muddled: his 1998 arrest was in Beverly Hills, not Hampstead. In 2006 he was linked to cruising on the Heath and defended his private sex life; in 2009 he called the Heath "a much nicer place to get some quick and honest sex than standing in a bar."

The West Heath is unique in this book: the one erotic-history site that is not history at all, but living, still in use, and still contested — the long thread of queer London running unbroken into the present.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**"No crime… of my own body."** The single most quotable line in the history of British cruising was spoken not on the Heath but in Georgian Moorfields, by William Brown, arrested in the Sodomites' Walk in 1726. Charged with attempted sodomy, he told the constable: "I think there is no crime in making what use I please of my own body." Rictor Norton's research rescued the line, and the Hampstead campaigners of 2025 revived his path's very name.

**The men's pond.** Apart from the West Heath itself, the Hampstead men's bathing pond has long been a place of gay sociability — sunbathing, posing and the occasional assignation on the surrounding lawns, alongside the serious business of cold-water swimming. It is a gentler, more open cousin of the West Heath's night-time trade, and a reminder that queer space on the Heath has never been only about sex.

In 2009 he defended the Heath as a place for "quick and honest sex"; in 2011 he tweeted, in capitals, that he would never apologise for his sex life.
