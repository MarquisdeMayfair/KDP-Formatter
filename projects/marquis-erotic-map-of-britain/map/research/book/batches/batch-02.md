## 6 — Victoria, Twice Over
*Osborne House, East Cowes, Isle of Wight & Balmoral Castle, Crathie, Aberdeenshire, Scotland · 1845–1901*

### BOOK VERSION

The woman we picture when we say "Victorian" — small, black, unamused, a national grandmother carved from suet — spent her wedding night writing in her diary like a girl who has just discovered what all the fuss is about. "To lie by his side, and in his arms, and on his dear bosom," she recorded on 10 February 1840, "was bliss beyond belief!" She and Albert did not, she noted, sleep much. In the morning she watched him shave and thought him the most beautiful thing she had ever seen. She was twenty; she was head over heels; and she had nine children in seventeen years to show for how the marriage continued.

None of this fits the legend, which is rather the point. The legend was built later, partly by Victoria herself in widowhood and partly by the daughter who edited her.

Osborne is where the private Victoria lived. She and Albert bought the estate on the Isle of Wight in 1845 — she called it "quite a paradise" and loved that she could "walk anywhere without being followed or mobbed" — and pulled down the existing house to build something better. The result is an Italianate villa with two campanile towers, designed by the London builder Thomas Cubitt with Albert acting as architect in all but name. Albert chose the style because the sea light over the Solent reminded him of the Bay of Naples. He laid out the terraces, planted the grounds, and filled the house with the thing that most upsets the suet-pudding version of these people: art of the frankly sensual kind.

The couple gave each other nudes. For Albert's birthday in 1852 Victoria bought him *Florinda* by Franz Xaver Winterhalter, a large canvas of semi-clothed women preparing to bathe while a king spies on them from the bushes, and hung it opposite their writing desks, where it still hangs. She gave him William Edward Frost's *Una among the Fauns and Wood Nymphs*, more bare flesh, and *The Disarming of Cupid*. English Heritage's own curator has pointed out the irony: Victoria was the less prudish of the two, "open to nudity and the sensuous," rather more than Albert, who was surprisingly buttoned-up about it.

The most telling object is smaller and quieter. In 1843 Victoria commissioned Winterhalter to paint her in secret as a birthday surprise for Albert: not the Queen, but the wife, her hair unpinned and falling loose over one bare shoulder, her head against a red cushion, looking at the viewer with unmistakable warmth. She called it her "secret picture." Albert thought it so intimate he kept it in his private writing room and it became, by her account, his favourite painting of her. It was not shown to the public for well over a century. When the BBC finally aired it, the response was mild surprise that a queen had ever looked like that.

The estate is a monument to a very Victorian idea of family privacy, which is to say it was elaborately staged. The royal children each had a plot in the grounds and a scaled-down Swiss Cottage, imported in sections and rebuilt, where they learned to cook and garden and keep a small museum of their own curiosities. Down through the woods was the family's private beach, and there Victoria kept a bathing machine — a wheeled wooden hut with a changing room, a veranda, curtains and a plumbed-in lavatory, from which she could be trundled into the Solent and emerge to swim with her dignity intact. "I thought it delightful," she wrote of her first dip in 1847, "till I put my head under water, when I thought I should be stifled." The machine survives, having spent part of the twentieth century as a chicken coop, and now sits back on the beach, which reopened to the public in 2012.

For all the bliss, Victoria was clear-eyed about the price of it. She loathed being pregnant, disliked newborn babies, and called the whole business the "shadow side" of marriage. On 7 April 1853, giving birth to her eighth child, Prince Leopold, she accepted chloroform from Dr John Snow and recorded the result in her journal: "that blessed Chloroform" whose effect was "soothing, quieting & delightful beyond measure." It was a royal endorsement that did more to make pain relief in childbirth respectable than any medical paper could. Do the arithmetic her biographers recommend: nine children in seventeen years is not the record of a woman enduring her duty with her eyes shut.

Then, on 14 December 1861, Albert died at Windsor, probably of typhoid, aged forty-two. Victoria was thirty-two years a wife and would be nearly forty years a widow. She wore black for the rest of her life and died at Osborne in 1901 in the same devout grief.

Which is where the record starts to be tampered with — and it is worth being precise about who did the tampering. Victoria wrote roughly two thousand words a day for most of her life; 141 volumes of journal survive in one form or another. She instructed her youngest daughter, Princess Beatrice, to act as her literary executor. Beatrice spent about forty years transcribing the diaries covering 1837 to 1901 into fair copies — 111 volumes in all — omitting anything she judged might wound the living or the family's reputation, and then burning the originals. Of Victoria's own hand only thirteen early volumes survive, covering 1832 to 1836; everything after the accession we read in a daughter's expurgated version, made against the explicit objection of King George V and Queen Mary. What we have of the most documented woman of the nineteenth century is, for the crucial decades, a censored draft. Remember that when we get to Balmoral.

Balmoral is the other Victoria, and the more interesting evidential puzzle. Albert leased the Aberdeenshire estate in 1848 and bought it outright in 1852; the small existing castle was too cramped, so a new one was built a hundred yards off in pale local granite, designed by William Smith of Aberdeen to Albert's amendments and finished in 1856. It was Albert's Scottish idyll. After his death it became the stage for Victoria's most talked-about relationship.

John Brown was a Highland ghillie, a Balmoral outdoor servant, blunt to the point of rudeness with everyone including the Queen. From 1865 she made him her permanent personal attendant, and for the next eighteen years he was the closest person to her — organising her days, bullying her gently, going everywhere with her. The court, appalled, called her "Mrs Brown" behind her back. Her children loathed him. And when he died in March 1883 she grieved with an openness that unsettled everyone: she commissioned a life-sized statue of him, wrote warmly of him in the published *More Leaves from the Journal of a Life in the Highlands*, drafted a laudatory memoir of him that her household persuaded her to suppress, and asked to be buried with mementoes of him, including, it is recorded, a lock of his hair and his photograph placed in her hand.

Then the rumours: that they had secretly married, that there was even a child. This is where a popular-history book has to decide whether it is honest, so let us be. What is documented is the extraordinary closeness, the public grief, the memorials, the suppressed memoir. What is not documented — anywhere — is a marriage.

The single load-bearing piece of "evidence" for a wedding is a diary entry by Lewis Harcourt, later Viscount Harcourt, for 17 February 1885. Harcourt recorded that Lady Ponsonby, wife of the Queen's private secretary, had told his father, the Home Secretary, that a Miss Macleod said her brother — the Reverend Norman Macleod, one of Victoria's chaplains — had confessed on his deathbed that he had married the Queen to John Brown, and had "always bitterly regretted it." Count the handoffs. Macleod died in 1872; Harcourt was nine at the time and never heard the man; the story reaches him fourth-hand, from the sister, to Lady Ponsonby, to his father, to his diary, thirteen years later. Even Harcourt hedged: Miss Macleod "could have no object in inventing such a story, so that one is almost inclined to believe it, improbable as it sounds." That is not a witness. That is a man writing down a rumour and admitting he half-believes it. Historians treat it accordingly.

The secret-child story is thinner still, resting on a Victorian pamphleteer, and has no evidence behind it at all. Raymond Lamont-Brown's biography and later television documentaries have combed the same ground and turned up devotion in abundance and a marriage certificate never.

But here is the part that makes the story genuinely about power rather than gossip. After Victoria died in 1901, her son Edward VII — who had detested Brown all his life — set about destroying everything connected with him: busts smashed, photographs removed, and a file of Brown's letters to the Queen burned. Edward VII also had Victoria and Albert's private rooms at Osborne sealed. So the reason we cannot settle the John Brown question is not that nothing was written down. It is that two people, Beatrice and Edward, took it upon themselves to make the record go quiet, exactly as they made it go quiet elsewhere.

Set the two houses side by side and you have the whole argument of this book in one reign. Osborne is a queen who enjoyed sex within marriage and said so, in her own hand, in ink her daughter later burned. Balmoral is a widow whose intimacy with a servant the establishment could not file under any acceptable heading, and therefore tried to erase. In both cases the desire was ordinary and human. In both cases somebody with a poker and a grate decided we shouldn't know.

Forget the widow in black. **balmoral-victoria-brown**

Did they marry? The evidence is one fourth-hand diary entry by Lewis Harcourt (1885), relaying an alleged deathbed confession by a chaplain who died in 1872. No certificate exists; the secret-child tale is thinner still. What is certain is the grief, the statue, the suppressed memoir — and that Edward VII burned Brown's letters after Victoria died.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The birthday tables** Victoria kept a childhood custom all her life: on each birthday a "present table" was laid out at Osborne, gifts arranged for the recipient to find. The royal children would haul Albert from his dressing room to his own table while the band played beneath the window. The nudes Victoria gave him were unwrapped in exactly this domestic, sentimental setting — sensuality and family life folded together without the slightest sense of contradiction.

**Chloroform and the bishops** When Victoria took chloroform for the birth of Prince Leopold on 7 April 1853, some clergy still held that women were scripturally obliged to suffer in childbirth. A reigning queen accepting "that blessed Chloroform" — she used it again in 1857 — did more than any pamphlet to break that idea. Dr John Snow, better remembered for tracing cholera to a Soho water pump, administered it.

**The room sealed for 53 years** On Edward VII's orders, the private apartments where Victoria and Albert had lived at Osborne were locked after her death in 1901. Special iron gates were fitted. They stayed shut until 1954, when Elizabeth II gave permission to open them, and the public first saw the rooms where the couple actually lived rather than the state rooms built for show.

## 7 — The House Where a King Gave It All Up
*Fort Belvedere, Windsor Great Park, Surrey, England · 1929–1936*

### BOOK VERSION

Fort Belvedere is not open to the public, has never been open to the public, and — since it is a private lease on the Crown Estate, currently a family home — is not going to be open to the public. You cannot visit. This is worth stating plainly at the outset, because it is the one house in this book where the single most consequential act of British erotic history took place, and you will have to imagine it from the gates.

The act was a signature. On the evening of 10 December 1936, in a house he loved more than any other, King Edward VIII signed the Instrument of Abdication, renouncing the throne "for Myself and for My descendants," so that he could marry a twice-married American named Wallis Simpson. His three younger brothers witnessed it. The document survives at The National Archives; it is short, and it is the only time in British history a monarch has voluntarily walked away from the crown. He did it for a woman the establishment had decided was impossible, and he did it in his own drawing room.

To understand why the house matters, you have to understand what Edward had made of it. Fort Belvedere began as an eighteenth-century folly, a mock-castle built for the Duke of Cumberland around 1750 and remodelled by Jeffry Wyatville for George IV in the 1820s — battlements with no battle, a toy fort on Shrub's Hill overlooking Virginia Water. When it was offered to Edward, then Prince of Wales, in 1929, his father George V was baffled: "What could you possibly want that queer old place for? Those damn weekends I suppose." He signed it over anyway.

Edward adored it. "I came to love the Fort," he wrote later in his memoir *A King's Story*, "as I loved no other material thing — perhaps because it was so much my own creation." He tore out the undergrowth with his own hands, roped weekend guests into the gardening, put in a swimming pool where a lily pond had been, added a tennis court and a steam bath and central heating and en-suite bathrooms. He mixed the cocktails himself. Guests came for the weekend and found not a stiff royal household but a country-house party that ran late, with the heir to the throne in a kilt or a dressing gown doing needlepoint. To the old court it was scandalous informality. To Edward it was the only place he could breathe.

Into this circle, around 1931, came Wallis Simpson and her husband Ernest, introduced through Edward's then-mistress, Thelma, Viscountess Furness. For a while the two women were friends. Then, in January 1934, Thelma sailed for New York and — by her own rueful account in her memoirs — asked Wallis to look after the Prince while she was away. Wallis, she wrote drily, "looked after him exceedingly well." Thelma returned that spring to a cooling lover and a private joke she wasn't part of; at dinner she watched Wallis playfully slap the Prince's hand and understood the whole thing in a single cold glance. She left the next morning. The displacement was complete.

What actually passed between Edward and Wallis is the part where a responsible book slows down, because the lurid version is enormous and the evidence is small. For decades the relationship has been narrated through a fog of sexual speculation: that Wallis had learned exotic techniques in a Shanghai brothel, that she held some carnal power over a sexually inadequate prince, that a document called the "China Dossier" catalogued her depravities for a horrified Prime Minister. Almost none of this survives contact with a fact. The China Dossier has never been produced; no one has ever seen it; the historian Paul French, who went looking, concluded it never existed and that the stories in it were real scandals about other people, transposed onto Wallis by an intelligence officer to break up the romance. The "Shanghai grip" is a saloon-bar legend. What we actually have are two memoirs — his *A King's Story*, her *The Heart Has Its Reasons* — and a mass of letters showing an infatuation that ran, on his side, close to dependence. Their contemporaries and later biographers describe his need for her as obsessive. That is a real and documented dynamic, and it is far stranger and sadder than the brothel stories, which were largely hostile invention aimed at explaining why a king would do something so mad.

And it was, constitutionally, mad. Edward became king on his father's death in January 1936. Wallis was still married; her second divorce was heard at Ipswich that October, timed to clear the way. As king, Edward was Supreme Governor of a Church of England that did not then remarry divorced people while a former spouse lived, and Wallis had two living former husbands. Prime Minister Stanley Baldwin told him plainly that the country and the Dominions would not accept her as queen. The British press, by a gentlemen's agreement extraordinary to modern eyes, said nothing at all for months while the American and Continental papers roared; the silence broke only in early December, and then the crisis ran in days. Offered a choice between the throne and the woman, Edward chose the woman, signed the paper at the Fort, and broadcast to the nation the following night that he could not do the job "without the help and support of the woman I love." He had reigned 325 days.

Stand back and a pattern from earlier in this book snaps into focus. A century and a bit before, another Prince of Wales — the future George IV — had fallen for another unacceptable twice-married woman, Maria Fitzherbert, and had actually gone through a secret wedding with her in 1785. That marriage was void under the Royal Marriages Act 1772, which required the sovereign's consent, and George went on to his throne and his official, catastrophic marriage to Caroline of Brunswick, keeping Maria at the edge of his life. Two princes, two forbidden, twice-married women, the same 1772 Act hanging over both. The difference is the outcome. George IV kept the crown and sacrificed the woman. Edward VIII kept the woman and sacrificed the crown. You may decide for yourself which was the more honest, but only one of them signed away a throne for it, and he did it here, in a folly on a hill you cannot visit.

The Fort's later life is an odd coda. It was used by Crown Lands offices in the war, leased to a grandson of George V in the 1950s, briefly held by the son of the ruler of Dubai, and has for decades been a private home. The swimming pool Edward built is reportedly still there. The rest you must picture from the road.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)

On the evening of 10 December 1936, King Edward VIII sat in the drawing room of the house he loved best and signed away the throne — "for Myself and for My descendants" — so he could marry Wallis Simpson. His three brothers witnessed it. It remains the only voluntary abdication in British history, and the Instrument survives at The National Archives.

Fort Belvedere was an eighteenth-century mock-castle, a folly built for the Duke of Cumberland and remodelled by Jeffry Wyatville for George IV. Given to Edward as Prince of Wales in 1929 — his father asked why he wanted "that queer old place" — it became his private paradise. He gardened it himself, installed a swimming pool, mixed the cocktails, and hosted weekend parties whose informality scandalised the old court. "I came to love the Fort," he wrote, "as I loved no other material thing."

Wallis Simpson entered the circle around 1931 and, by 1934, had displaced the Prince's mistress Thelma Furness — who had unwisely asked Wallis to "look after him" during a trip to New York, and returned to find she had. The relationship has been buried ever since under lurid, thinly evidenced gossip: the mythical "China Dossier," the "Shanghai grip." Historians who have looked find no dossier ever existed. What survives is two memoirs and a heap of letters showing an infatuation close to obsession.

As king from January 1936, Edward could not marry a twice-divorced woman and remain head of the Church of England. Baldwin told him the country would not accept her. Offered the choice, he chose Wallis, signed at the Fort, and broadcast his farewell the next night after 325 days on the throne.

The echo is exact: a century earlier the future George IV secretly "married" the twice-widowed Maria Fitzherbert, void under the Royal Marriages Act 1772, then took his crown and kept his official marriage. Two princes, two forbidden women, opposite endings. Fort Belvedere is a private home today and is not open to the public.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The needlepoint king** Away from parental scrutiny at the Fort, Edward took up gardening, flying and, more surprisingly, needlepoint, working cushion covers in the evenings — a habit his mother Queen Mary had taught him. The image sits oddly beside the "playboy prince" of the newspapers, and rather better beside the man who chose domestic contentment with one woman over the loneliest job in the empire.

**Weaning a brother off drugs** It was at Fort Belvedere, according to several accounts, that Edward nursed his younger brother Prince George, Duke of Kent, through withdrawal from cocaine and morphine addiction in the late 1920s, keeping him at the house under his own eye. Whatever else the Fort was, it was where this family conducted the business it could not conduct in a palace.

**The press that agreed not to look** For months in 1936, British newspaper proprietors simply declined to report the King's affair, even as the foreign press splashed it everywhere. Readers who travelled abroad came home to find their own country's papers had told them nothing. The self-censorship collapsed only in the first week of December, and the abdication followed within a week — a reminder of how much erotic history is really a history of who controls the story.

## 8 — Georgiana, Bess and the Duke
*Chatsworth House, Derbyshire, England · 1774–1811*

### BOOK VERSION

A duke, his duchess, and the duchess's best friend, all under one enormous roof for the better part of twenty-five years. The duke and the best friend are lovers, and have two children together. The duchess, meanwhile, has a daughter by a different man entirely, borne in secret in France and given away. Everybody more or less knows. The arrangement holds, through gossip and gambling debts and a revolution across the Channel, because at this level of society the rules about who slept with whom were startlingly elastic — provided you did not get caught in a way that could not be smoothed over, and provided you were a man.

Georgiana Spencer was seventeen when she married William Cavendish, fifth Duke of Devonshire, on her birthday in June 1774. He was the most eligible bachelor in England, master of Chatsworth in Derbyshire and Devonshire House in London, and about as warm as the marble he owned. She was dazzling — a fashion leader before the phrase existed, a political hostess who could fill a room, a genuine celebrity in the first modern sense, mobbed in the street and reported in the papers for the height of her hair and the droop of her ostrich feathers. The marriage was a mismatch of temperature from the start. He wanted an heir and his clubs; she wanted to be loved and, failing that, to be adored by everyone else.

She threw herself into Whig politics, and here we meet the first of this chapter's cases of the lurid version outrunning the evidence. In the Westminster election of 1784 Georgiana campaigned hard for Charles James Fox, going out into the streets to canvass tradesmen — extraordinary conduct for a duchess. The Tory press destroyed her for it. According to the art historian Amelia Rauser, at least eighty-nine different political prints survive from the month of April 1784 alone, many of them showing her trading kisses for votes, kissing butchers, selling herself for Fox. The image has stuck for two centuries. What is documented underneath it is real but far smaller: she certainly canvassed, certainly charmed, and there is a letter, quoted by her biographer Amanda Foreman, in which Georgiana herself refers ruefully to the kissing. But the butcher-kissing duchess of the caricatures is largely a construction of misogynist print culture, a way of punishing a woman for doing politics in public by recasting it as prostitution. The kiss became, as one scholar puts it, political theatre. Fox won his seat. Georgiana paid for it in reputation.

The debts were real and enormous. Georgiana gambled catastrophically — faro, mostly — and concealed the scale of it from her husband for years, borrowing to cover losses and losing to cover borrowing, in a spiral that would have ruined a lesser house. This mattered more than it should because of the other pressure on her: the duke needed a son, and for a long time she produced daughters and miscarriages. "Little G" arrived in 1783, "Harryo" in 1785, and only in 1790 the longed-for heir, William. Aristocratic wives were valued for fertility as much as dowry, and Georgiana felt every year of the wait.

Into the middle of this, in 1782, walked Lady Elizabeth Foster — "Bess" — separated from her husband, cut off from her children, clever, charming and effectively penniless. Georgiana adored her on sight and brought her into the household. Bess proceeded to make herself indispensable to both Devonshires: bosom friend to the wife, and mistress to the husband. The ménage à trois that resulted was singular even by Georgian standards and lasted, in one form or another, until Georgiana's death. Bess bore the duke two illegitimate children, Caroline St Jules and Augustus Clifford, who were folded into the Chatsworth nursery alongside the legitimate ones. Whether Georgiana tolerated this out of love for Bess, or dependence, or because Bess had leverage over her debts, her contemporaries could not agree and neither can we; her own mother thought Bess a con artist of the first order.

The double standard arrives with brutal clarity when Georgiana finally takes a lover of her own. Having secured the succession, she fell for Charles Grey, the young, rising Whig — the same Grey who would decades later, as Earl Grey and Prime Minister, carry the Great Reform Act, and who has a blend of tea named after him. In 1791 she became pregnant by him. The duke's response tells you everything about whose desires were permitted. A mistress in the house was tolerable; a straying duchess was not. He gave her a choice: divorce and disgrace, or exile. To keep contact with her legitimate children she chose exile, and in February 1792, in Aix-en-Provence, she gave birth to Grey's daughter, Eliza Courtney. The baby was handed to Grey's parents to raise as their own. Georgiana was allowed to know Eliza only as a kind of family friend, a godmother; Eliza learned who her mother really was only after Georgiana died. Grey, for his part, married someone else in 1794. He kept his career. Georgiana kept her guilt.

She came home in 1793 on the duke's terms, having promised to withdraw from the fray, and lived out her remaining years in the ménage that everyone had by now stopped pretending not to see, her health wrecked by illness and by the medicines and drink she used to get through the days. She died in 1806, aged forty-eight. With what looks like her blessing, Bess became the next Duchess of Devonshire three years later, marrying the duke at last in 1809 — the mistress promoted to wife over the body of the friend who had loved them both.

What survives of Georgiana at Chatsworth is worth being precise about, because the house is run by the Devonshire family still and the archive is a real one. The Devonshire Collection holds her papers — notebooks, correspondence, the material historians actually work from — and the house shows portraits, including Gainsborough's, and personal relics down to a lock of her hair. It is important to separate that documentary base from the two things that made Georgiana famous again in our own time: Amanda Foreman's 1998 biography, which is superb and which mined the family papers properly, and the 2008 film *The Duchess*, which mined Foreman and Keira Knightley's cheekbones and which pressed hard on the parallel with Diana, Princess of Wales — Georgiana's own descendant, and another woman who observed that there were three people in her marriage. The parallel is genuine but it flattens Georgiana into a victim, and she was a great deal more than that: a political operator, a writer, a scientist of sorts, and a woman who ran, and was run by, one of the most openly unconventional households in England.

That openness is the point. The Devonshire ménage worked precisely because it belonged to a class that could afford not to care, at the exact moment when the middle class beneath it was busy inventing the idea that such things were unthinkable. Chatsworth in the 1780s and the moralising drawing rooms of the 1830s are only a few decades apart. What changed was not human desire. What changed was who got to be exempt from the rules about it.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)

Picture the household: a duke, his duchess, and her best friend, all under one roof for twenty-five years. The duke and the friend are lovers with two children; the duchess has a secret daughter by another man. Everyone knows. It holds — because at this level of society the rules bent, provided you weren't caught in a way that couldn't be smoothed over, and provided you were a man.

Georgiana Spencer married the fifth Duke of Devonshire in 1774, aged seventeen. She became the most famous woman in England — fashion leader, Whig political hostess, mobbed celebrity — married to a cold man who wanted an heir. Campaigning for Charles James Fox in the 1784 Westminster election, she was destroyed in the Tory print shops as a duchess who traded kisses for votes. She did canvass, and once admitted the kissing in a letter; the butcher-kissing caricatures were largely misogynist invention, punishing a woman for doing politics in public.

In 1782 Lady Elizabeth "Bess" Foster joined the household — Georgiana's dearest friend and soon the duke's mistress, bearing him two children raised alongside the legitimate ones. The double standard turned savage when Georgiana took her own lover, Charles Grey (the future Reform prime minister). Pregnant by him in 1791, she was given a choice by the duke: divorce or exile. She chose exile, bore Eliza Courtney in France in 1792, and surrendered the baby to Grey's parents. Eliza learned the truth only after Georgiana's death in 1806. Grey married elsewhere and kept his career.

Three years after Georgiana died, the duke married Bess. The mistress became duchess over the body of the friend who had loved them both.

At Chatsworth today the Devonshire Collection holds Georgiana's papers, portraits and personal relics, including a lock of her hair. Distinguish that real archive from Amanda Foreman's 1998 biography and the 2008 film *The Duchess*, which pressed the parallel with her descendant Diana. Georgiana was more than a victim: operator, writer, and mistress of England's most openly unconventional house — exempt from rules the rising middle class was declaring unthinkable.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The gambling she hid** Georgiana's faro losses ran to sums that could have embarrassed even the Cavendish fortune, and she concealed their true scale from her husband for years, borrowing from bankers, friends and moneylenders to cover them. The secrecy gave Bess, and others, quiet leverage over her. It is one of history's neater ironies that the age's most watched woman spent so much energy hiding something.

**Two duchesses, one portrait** The Wallace Collection holds a double image of Georgiana and Bess, the friends whose lives twined for a quarter-century around the same man. Their contemporaries endlessly speculated about the exact nature of the women's bond as well as their rivalry; the surviving letters show real affection alongside real damage. They were, whatever else, inseparable to the end.

**Eliza's quiet revenge** The daughter Georgiana had to give away, Eliza Courtney, was raised by Charles Grey's parents believing her mother was a distant family friend. When Eliza grew up and had daughters of her own, she named the first Georgiana. It is the only memorial the erased child could leave to the mother the arrangement had taken from her.

## 9 — The Arrangement the Nation Agreed Not to See
*Merton Place, Merton, Surrey (now London Borough of Merton), England · 1801–1815*

### BOOK VERSION

Emma Hamilton was handed from one man to another to pay off a debt, and the paperwork survives. That sentence ought to be shocking, and the fact that it usually isn't — that Emma is remembered as a scandal, a schemer, a fallen woman — is itself the scandal this chapter is about.

She was born Amy Lyon in 1765 at Ness in Cheshire, the daughter of a blacksmith who died when she was two months old. Raised by her mother and grandmother in relative poverty across the Welsh border, she went into service young, drifted to London, and by her mid-teens was surviving on her looks and her wits in a city that had very few honest uses for a poor and beautiful girl. She modelled; she was mistress to a baronet who threw her out when she fell pregnant; she became the mistress of the Hon. Charles Greville, who set her up, renamed her Emma Hart, and had her painted again and again by George Romney until her face was famous across the country.

Then Greville needed money and a rich wife, and Emma was in the way of both. So in 1786 he shipped her to Naples, to his widowed uncle Sir William Hamilton, the British envoy — telling Emma it was a holiday and that he would follow. He never followed. The arrangement between the two men was, in effect, a transaction: Sir William would take Emma off Greville's hands, and Greville would remain his uncle's heir, undisturbed by any late remarriage. Emma worked it out slowly, in a foreign city, and her letters as the truth dawned are unbearable. "I love you," she wrote to Greville; and, realising: "if I was with you I would murder you and myself both." She was twenty-one, and she had been sold to clear another man's accounts.

Here is where the ordinary story would have her disappear. Instead Emma did something remarkable: she became, on her own terms, one of the most celebrated women in Europe. She learned Italian and French, sang well enough to be offered an opera contract, befriended the Queen of Naples, and invented a performance art all her own — her "Attitudes," in which, draped in shawls, she moved through a sequence of poses from classical sculpture and painting so vividly that Goethe travelled to see them and wrote about them. Sir William, to his credit, fell genuinely in love with the woman he had been handed, and in 1791 he married her. The blacksmith's daughter became Lady Hamilton.

And then, in 1798, Horatio Nelson came to Naples after the Battle of the Nile, and the most famous love affair in British naval history began under the roof, and with the knowledge, of Emma's husband. It is easy to be arch about this, and pointless. The three of them lived together and defended the arrangement in public as a pure friendship — Emma liked to call the household *tria juncta in uno*, "three joined in one," which is the motto of the Order of the Bath both men happened to belong to. Whether Sir William believed his own line or simply loved them both enough to look away, the trio held together until his death in April 1803, and he died holding Emma's hand with Nelson in the room.

Their daughter arrived in the middle of it. Horatia was born in late January 1801, and the machinery of concealment around her is a small masterpiece of Georgian hypocrisy: Nelson wrote to Emma about the baby in code, casting himself and Emma as a sailor named "Thomson" and his wife, so that letters could be read aloud or intercepted without exposing them. The child was farmed out to a nurse in Marylebone and only later brought into the household as an "adopted" ward. Everyone who mattered understood exactly what was going on, and everyone agreed, for the sake of the man winning Britain's war at sea, not to say so out loud.

The house at the centre of all this was Merton Place, in what was then rural Surrey. Nelson bought it in 1801 for £9,000 — a substantial sum, well into the low millions in today's money — while he was away at sea, which meant Emma chose it, inspected it, and furnished it on his behalf. He never haggled; against his solicitor's advice he refused to offer less. It became, in his own worn phrase, "dear, dear Merton," the only house he ever owned and the nearest thing to a home he ever had. Emma filled it with guests and dinners and improvements, dug a branch of water in the grounds that the household nicknamed "the Nile," and made it a paradise the three of them shared until Sir William died and, after him, the two of them and their small daughter.

In August 1805 Nelson came home to Merton for twenty-five days, his last spell of happiness, and then left it "to go to serve my King and Country." On 21 October, in sight of the combined fleets off Cape Trafalgar, he wrote the codicil that is the moral heart of this story. Reciting Emma's services to the nation, he wrote: "I leave Emma Lady Hamilton, therefore, a legacy to my King and Country, that they will give her an ample provision to maintain her rank in life." He commended their daughter Horatia to the country's care too. "These," he added, "are the only favours I ask of my King and Country, at this moment when I am going to fight their battle." Hours later he was dead.

The nation took the victory and ignored the codicil entirely. Nelson's clergyman brother William was created Earl Nelson and granted a perpetual pension of £5,000 a year; Parliament later voted £90,000 to buy him an estate, which became Trafalgar Park in Wiltshire. His sisters were handsomely provided for; the state paid for a funeral of imperial splendour at St Paul's, from which Emma — as a mistress — was excluded. Emma herself received nothing from the Crown. She had money from Sir William and from Nelson and the house at Merton, and with care it might have lasted; but she had never been taught care, and grief and generosity and debt did the rest. She was forced to try to sell Merton, could not find a buyer, and it passed out of her hands. She was twice arrested for debt. In 1814, broken and drinking, she crossed to Calais with Horatia to escape her creditors, and there, on 15 January 1815, she died in poverty. She was forty-nine. The daughter Nelson had "left to his country" was raised by his relations and went to her own grave still denying that Emma had been her mother.

Merton Place itself is gone — demolished by 1823, the estate broken into building lots, the "Nile" filled in, the paradise buried under Victorian terraces and, later, twentieth-century council housing and lock-ups. This is why the Marquis's camera has to work harder here than anywhere. What survives is a scatter of names and markers: streets called Nelson, Trafalgar, Victory, Hardy and Hamilton laid over the old grounds; a small Lord Nelson memorial with two cannon beside the church of St John the Divine, near South Wimbledon; Nelson Gardens, where local schoolchildren long raised flags each Trafalgar Day; a plaque near Merton High Street marking where the house stood; and the Merton council's Nelson Trail stitching the fragments together. It is a curiously honest kind of ruin — a great man's happiness reduced to street signs, and beside it, unmarked, the erasure of the woman who made the happiness and was thrown away the moment its guarantor was gone.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)

Emma Hamilton was handed from one man to another to settle a debt, and the paperwork survives. In 1786 her lover Charles Greville, needing a rich wife, shipped her to his widowed uncle Sir William Hamilton in Naples — telling her it was a holiday — so that Sir William would take her on and Greville would stay his heir. Emma, born a blacksmith's daughter at Ness in Cheshire in 1765, worked out the truth alone in a foreign city. She was twenty-one.

She refused to vanish. She learned languages, befriended the Queen of Naples, and invented her famous "Attitudes," classical tableaux that drew Goethe to watch. Sir William fell genuinely in love and married her in 1791. Then, in 1798, came Nelson, fresh from the Nile — and the most famous affair in naval history unfolded under her husband's roof, the three of them living openly together as what Emma called "three joined in one."

Their daughter Horatia was born in 1801, concealed behind coded "Mrs Thomson" letters and farmed out to a nurse. Nelson bought Merton Place that year for £9,000 while at sea; Emma chose and furnished it. It became his "dear, dear Merton," the only home he ever owned, shared by the trio until Sir William died in 1803.

Before Trafalgar, on 21 October 1805, Nelson wrote a codicil leaving "Emma Lady Hamilton... a legacy to my King and Country." The nation took his victory and ignored his last wish. His brother got an earldom, a £5,000 pension and £90,000 for an estate; Emma, barred from the St Paul's funeral, got nothing from the Crown. She sank into debt, was imprisoned for it, fled to Calais, and died there in poverty on 15 January 1815.

What remains is a scatter of traces the map records: streets named Nelson, Trafalgar, Victory, Hardy and Hamilton; a small memorial with cannon by St John the Divine; Nelson Gardens; a plaque near Merton High Street; and the borough's Nelson Trail. A tolerated household, because the man at its centre was too useful to disgrace — and a woman discarded the day he died.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The "Mrs Thomson" letters** To hide Horatia's parentage, Nelson wrote to Emma about their own child as though relaying news from a sailor named Thomson and his wife, a couple in difficulty whom the Hamiltons were kindly helping. The letters could then be read openly. It is a tender, absurd, heartbreaking piece of subterfuge — two of the most recognisable people in Britain pretending, on paper, to be someone else's servants.

**The canal called the Nile** In the grounds of Merton, Emma had a serpentine stretch of water dug and the household dubbed it "the Nile," after the battle that had made Nelson a legend and, indirectly, made their affair possible. Guests were rowed on it. Like everything at Merton it was demolished and filled; the name survives only in the historians' accounts of the vanished paradise.

**Excluded from the funeral** When Nelson was buried at St Paul's in January 1806 with a pageantry the country reserved for its greatest, Emma was not invited and did not attend. As his mistress she had no standing to mourn him in public, whatever the codicil said. She learned of his death when a messenger arrived at Merton with the news; by her account she could not speak for hours.

## 10 — Lakeland Notoriety: The Maid of Buttermere and Wicked Jimmy
*The Fish Inn, Buttermere & Lowther Castle, near Penrith, Cumbria (historically Cumberland and Westmorland), England · 1792–1814*

### BOOK VERSION

Mary Robinson's misfortune was to be beautiful in the exact decade that beauty in a remote place became a tourist attraction. In 1792 a retired army captain named Joseph Budworth published *A Fortnight's Ramble to the Lakes*, one of the first guidebooks to the newly fashionable Lake District, and in it he devoted an embarrassing quantity of prose to the teenage daughter of the innkeeper at Buttermere in Cumberland — her dark hair, her fine oval face, her lips "as red as vermilion," crowning her the beauty of the valley. It was, in effect, a review, and it worked as reviews do. Sightseers began arriving at the Fish Inn specifically to look at Mary, who had to go on carrying their ale while they gawped and the Lake Poets composed remarks about her. She had become a destination before she had done anything at all.

Ten years of this had passed when, in the summer of 1802, the trap arrived in the shape of a charming stranger. He called himself the Hon. Colonel Alexander Augustus Hope, brother of the Earl of Hopetoun and a Member of Parliament, and he courted Mary with the confidence of a man who was none of those things. He was in fact John Hatfield, a serial impostor and undischarged bankrupt with a string of abandoned wives and forged bills behind him, working the Lakes for whatever credit a borrowed title could raise. He married Mary at Lorton church on 2 October 1802.

And here the story turns on a piece of writing, which is why it belongs in a book about how the record is made. The Keswick correspondent of the *Morning Post* filed a charmed little item about the match — a real colonel's brother marrying a humble Lakeland beauty — under the heading "Romantic Marriage." The correspondent was Samuel Taylor Coleridge. His report, published on 11 October, was meant as a piece of picturesque human interest; instead it detonated the fraud, because people who knew the genuine Colonel Hope was abroad read it and asked how he could be honeymooning in Cumberland. Coleridge, realising what he had set off, followed up with further accounts as the impostor was unmasked. Hatfield fled, was hunted down near Swansea, and was brought back for trial.

He was hanged at Carlisle on 3 September 1803 — and here is a detail that says everything about the age's priorities. Hatfield was not executed for the bigamy that made him a monster in the public mind, nor for breaking Mary's heart. He was hanged for forgery, then a capital offence, for faking bills and franking letters with an MP's signature. The state did not much care whom he had deceived into bed; it cared that he had defrauded the mail and the banks. Mary, pregnant by him, bore a child that was stillborn.

She survived it. Wordsworth, who had known of her before the scandal, wrote her into Book Seven of *The Prelude* as the "Maid of Buttermere," an emblem of natural virtue wronged by metropolitan vice, and the public, having gawped at her beauty and then at her ruin, raised subscriptions for her. In 1808 she married a Caldbeck farmer, Richard Harrison, had four children, and lived quietly until her death in 1837. She is buried at St Kentigern's, Caldbeck. Melvyn Bragg, a Cumbrian himself, novelised the whole affair in 1987. The Fish Inn is still there, still a working inn at the foot of Honister Pass, still trading gently on the legend of the maid who once carried the drinks.

Twenty miles east, and cut from entirely different cloth, sat the man who casts the longest shadow over the Lakeland of this period: Sir James Lowther, first Earl of Lonsdale, known the length of two counties as "Wicked Jimmy" and "Jemmy Grasp-all." He was, by broad agreement, the richest and most detested man in the north of England, a coal and land magnate who controlled a bloc of parliamentary seats so completely that his tame Members were nicknamed his "Ninepins." The Dictionary of National Biography, no scandal sheet, records Alexander Carlyle's verdict that Lowther was "more detested than any man alive" and, in Carlyle's phrase, "truly a madman, though too rich to be confined."

He touches this book, and the Wordsworth circle, through money and through a corpse. The money first, because it is solid. Lowther employed John Wordsworth — the poet's father — as his law-agent, and over two decades ran up a debt to him of some £4,000 in expenses which he simply declined to pay. When John Wordsworth died in 1783, the debt died unpaid with him, and the five orphaned Wordsworth children, William among them, grew up in reduced and anxious circumstances directly because the wealthiest man in the region would not settle his bill. Only after Wicked Jimmy's death in 1802 did his heir pay the sum, with interest, freeing William to marry and to write. The poet's whole early life was shaped by one rich man's meanness.

Now the corpse, and here we must attribute rather than assert, because the story is famous and the evidence is thin and late. The tale runs that Lowther loved the daughter of one of his tenant farmers, made her his mistress, and, when she died young, could not bear to bury her — that he kept the body until it could no longer be kept, and finally had it embalmed under glass. Its earliest respectable source is Thomas De Quincey, who set it down in his *Recollections of the Lakes and the Lake Poets*, not as fact but expressly as Cumberland hearsay: it was, he wrote, a story "I have often heard repeated by the country people of Cumberland." And De Quincey's own version is markedly gentler than the one that circulates now. He says only that Lowther loved a young woman of humble parentage, that she died young, and that in his grief he had her embalmed with a glass placed over her features, which he visited. The lurid accretions — that he refused burial outright, dined beside a rotting body, drove the servants out with the smell, kept her seven weeks, stowed her in a cupboard — appear nowhere in De Quincey and nowhere in any reputable biography. The Dictionary of National Biography, which cheerfully records his tyranny and his madness, does not mention the woman at all. She is never reliably named; the "Betsy" of some modern retellings has no source behind it. So: an early, named witness reporting a genuine piece of local folklore about an embalming; and, layered on top over two centuries, a horror story that owes more to the tellers than to Lowther. The truth is grim enough without the cupboard.

The castle you can actually visit belongs to the aftermath, not to Wicked Jimmy. He died in 1802 leaving no legitimate son; his heir, William Lowther, pulled down the old hall and had Robert Smirke build a vast Gothic pile between 1806 and 1814. That is Lowther Castle — and it too came to grief, abandoned and unroofed in the twentieth century, so that what stands today is a magnificent shell, a ruined stone face fronting restored gardens, open to visitors who wander through rooms that have no ceilings.

Put the two Lakeland stories together and the same landscape holds both. The same hills that made Mary Robinson a tourist attraction made the Lake Poets who wrote her down and who suffered under Lowther's thumb; Coleridge exposed the impostor who ruined the innkeeper's daughter, and Wordsworth's childhood was mortgaged to the tyrant up the road. Beauty and money, the powerless and the powerful, and running between them a thread of poets who turned all of it into literature — and who return, later in this book, to Dove Cottage and Greta Hall to do it again.

The bigamy and the broken-hearted bride, which gripped the public and filled the London theatres, were legally beside the point. He is a neat, grim illustration of what the Georgian state actually punished, and what it merely found entertaining.

Rather than vanish, it survives as a deliberate ruin: visitors walk through grand rooms open to the sky, the restored gardens spread below, a monument to money that outlasted the family that made it.
