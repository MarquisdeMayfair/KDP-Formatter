## 11 — A YMCA With Extras
*17 Danube Street · Stockbridge, Edinburgh, Scotland · c.1946–1977*

### BOOK VERSION
She wore a fur coat, a twinset and pearls, kept a blue rinse, spoke in an accent she had manufactured herself, and when the police arrived to raid her brothel she is said to have opened the door and asked, sweetly, "Business or pleasure, gentlemen?" Dora Noyce was the most famous madam in Scotland, and almost nothing about her was quite what it appeared, beginning with the name.

She was born Georgina — "Georgie" — Hunter Rae in 1900, in Rose Street, then one of the poorer and rougher lanes tucked behind the douce Georgian frontages of Edinburgh's New Town. Her father was a cutler. She went into prostitution young, before she was twenty, and somewhere along the way acquired both a daughter, Violet, born in 1923, and the surname of the child's father, Ernest Noyce, whom she seems to have regarded chiefly as a friend. She also acquired the plummy vowels of Morningside, Edinburgh's most self-consciously refined suburb, on the sound commercial principle that a better accent brought a better class of customer. Her first conviction for living on immoral earnings came in 1934.

The house at 17 Danube Street opened for business shortly after the Second World War and ran until Dora's death, an elegant terraced property on the northern edge of the New Town, a few minutes' walk from the water of Leith. She owned two floors. According to Tom Wood, later Deputy Chief Constable of Lothian and Borders Police, who walked the Stockbridge beat as a young constable in Dora's final years, she kept the upstairs flat for herself and her guests and sublet the basement to her women, where it was divided into small cubicles. Peter Ritchie, a former detective superintendent, recalled the arrangement more bluntly: the top floor for the "high heid-yins", the bottom for "you and me".

What the record documents about the interior is modest and, refreshingly, unglamorous. Customers arriving upstairs were offered coffee, sandwiches and biscuits while they decided which woman took their fancy — this from David McLean's account in The Scotsman. The writer Roddy Martine, who was taken there on a stag night in the early 1970s and used no services, remembered it as "rather scruffy": a big television, women on sofas, drunken men, and Dora circulating with glasses of dry white wine, asking the suddenly sobered party whether they were sailors. That is the closest we have to an eyewitness inventory, and it is worth holding on to, because the estate-agent language of "lavishly furnished A-listed rooms" attached to the house belongs to its 2020 sale particulars, not to Dora's tenure.

She employed fifteen women in permanent residence and could draw on up to twenty-five more at short notice — arriving, the story goes, by taxi from Glasgow — when trade was brisk. The women had regular medical checks, a point Dora made much of. She hated the word "brothel" and preferred "house of leisure and pleasure", or, in her single most quoted line, "a YMCA — with the one little difference". She called the women her "business girls". Her busiest fortnights, she liked to tell reporters with a straight face, were the Edinburgh Festival and the annual General Assembly of the Church of Scotland.

The prices are where the record goes frustratingly quiet. No tariff or price list for Dora's survives in any openly available source; the fullest scholarly treatment, Roger Davidson's chapter "'An Open and Notorious House of Lewdness'" in the Edinburgh University Press volume Illicit and Unnatural Practices (2018), promises detail on "sexual transactions" but sits behind a paywall, and the exact figures charged remain, for now, undocumented in public. What we do have are the folkloric takings. When the American aircraft carrier USS John F. Kennedy was in the Firth of Forth in 1970, the house is said, in the words repeated by Wikipedia and the Edinburgh Evening News, to have done "£4,000 of business in one night" before "the ship's captain declared the house off-limits" — something like £60,000 in today's money — the queue reputedly stretching round to neighbouring Ann Street. Every source hedges this with "reportedly" and "it is said", and so should we; it is a very good story that has been polished by long handling. A word of caution on one further claim: the connection sometimes made to the 1961 arrival of the US Polaris fleet at the Holy Loch is not supported by any source I could find — the loch is on the west coast, a long way from Leith, and the only documented US Navy episode is the Kennedy.

The law's relationship with Dora was a study in pragmatism. She was charged exactly forty-seven times for living off immoral earnings across her career, mostly paying fines — up to £250, roughly £2,500 today — and lying low for a few days before resuming. Genuine prison was rare; her last sentence, four months, came in 1972 when she was seventy-one. Tom Wood explained the arrangement without embarrassment: you can ignore the sex trade, which is foolish, or try to suppress it, which is impossible, or you can regulate it and at least know who the players are. Danube Street would be raided roughly every six months; in return Dora passed the police useful information about stolen goods and the wider underworld. It was toleration by handshake. Tellingly, when she was jailed in 1972 the council received more complaints about the house, not fewer — because it was run less well without her.

She was, incongruously, a Conservative, and hung party posters in her windows at election time, an act of respectability-by-association that nobody has ever satisfactorily explained. She went to Kirk. She embarrassed her MP by turning up at garden fêtes. And she understood publicity better than most: leaving court, she would remind the reporters to print the correct address, "because in my profession there is no such thing as bad publicity".

Dora Noyce died in 1977, aged seventy-seven. The business limped on briefly without her and closed; the house was divided into flats and, as the Scottish Daily Express records, sold for £1.57 million in August 2020 — against a street average of £1,449,000. Half a century on, the New Town's most notorious address is worth almost exactly what its respectable neighbours are, which is a joke Dora, connoisseur of Edinburgh's talent for keeping its appetites and its appearances in separate rooms, would have appreciated. She belongs to a longer line of British brothel-keepers who prospered precisely by looking like the last people on earth who would; the London madams of later chapters ran the same trick with more velvet and worse manners.

### WEB VERSION
Dora Noyce ran the most famous brothel in Scotland from an elegant terraced house at 17 Danube Street, in the genteel heart of Edinburgh's New Town, from just after the Second World War until her death in 1977. She was born Georgina Hunter Rae in 1900 in poor, rough Rose Street, went into prostitution young, and reinvented herself with a manufactured Morningside accent, a fur coat, a twinset and pearls — the very picture of Edinburgh respectability, which was rather the point.

She hated the word "brothel". Her house was a "house of leisure and pleasure", or a "YMCA with the one little difference", and her staff were "business girls". Customers were greeted upstairs with coffee, sandwiches, biscuits and dry white wine; the basement was divided into small cubicles. She kept fifteen women in residence and could call up to twenty-five more from Glasgow by taxi when trade surged — and it surged, she claimed with a straight face, during the Edinburgh Festival and the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland.

The famous story is the USS John F. Kennedy in the Firth of Forth in 1970, after which the house was said to have done "£4,000 of business in one night" with a queue round the block — a tale every source hedges with "reportedly", and so should you. She was charged exactly forty-seven times, usually just paid the fine, and served real prison only rarely. The police tolerated her by arrangement: a raid every six months, information passed the other way. A Conservative who hung party posters in her windows at election time and attended Kirk, she understood publicity perfectly, reminding reporters to print the correct address because there was "no such thing as bad publicity". She died in 1977; the house is now flats, and in 2020 sold for £1.57 million — worth what its respectable neighbours are.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**The wine and the sobering question.** The writer Roddy Martine was taken to Dora's on a stag night in the early 1970s and recalled it not as a den of luxury but as "rather scruffy" — a big television, women on sofas, drunken men. Dora moved among them handing out glasses of dry white wine and asking, pointedly, whether they were sailors. It is the only real eyewitness description of the interior we have, and it is gloriously ordinary.

**Business or pleasure, gentlemen?** Dora's court appearances were performances. She wore her pearls to the dock, admitted to twenty-eight previous convictions on one 1973 occasion alone, and told the sheriff she had "no intention of closing down". A correspondent to The Scotsman, after her death, confessed to "something of affection" for her, noting she at least accepted responsibility for what happened under her roof — a rare thing said of anyone in her trade.

**The council preferred her open.** When Dora was jailed for four months in 1972 at the age of seventy-one, the local authority found itself fielding more complaints about 17 Danube Street, not fewer. Without her steady hand the house was run carelessly. It is the neatest possible proof of the strange truth of her career: a well-run brothel was, for the neighbours and the police alike, the least troublesome option available.

## 12 — Respectable Façades
*Castle Ward, Strangford, Co. Down · The Crown Liquor Saloon, Belfast · Tiger Bay, Butetown, Cardiff · Llandudno, Conwy · c.1762–1901*

### BOOK VERSION
Walk up to Castle Ward today from the landscaped side and you are looking at a sober, correct, Palladian country house: a pediment, four engaged Ionic columns, the architecture of reason and restraint. Walk round to the front facing Strangford Lough and you are looking at a different building entirely — pointed Gothic windows, battlements, pinnacles, the whole Strawberry Hill fancy-dress. It is the same house. It was built that way on purpose, in County Down in the early 1760s, and the interiors keep up the quarrel room by room: classical on his side, Gothic on hers, the split running down the middle of the plan like a line drawn on a map by two people who have stopped speaking.

The couple were Bernard Ward, later 1st Viscount Bangor, and his wife Lady Anne, daughter of the 1st Earl of Darnley. He liked the classical; she preferred, in the withering word of their contemporary Mrs Delany, the "whimsical" Gothic. Delany, watching the project rise, doubted both their judgements — "he wants taste, and Lady Anne is so whimsical that I doubt her judgment" — and a compromise was struck: his taste for the entrance front, hers for the garden front. It is the most photogenic marital disagreement in the National Trust's portfolio, and the temptation to read the architecture as a diagram of a failing marriage is almost irresistible. Lady Anne did leave Bernard, in 1770, shortly after the house was finished, and never came back.

Here honesty is required, because the story has hardened into something the evidence does not quite support. The Wards were married in 1747 and had, by most counts, seven or eight children together; Anne stayed a great many years before she left. Whether the divided house caused the separation, expressed it, or simply coincided with a couple who wanted a fashionable home incorporating both of the age's competing styles, we cannot say. The National Trust's own historians now lean towards the less dramatic reading — two tastes under one roof — while acknowledging how much later storytelling has done to marry the bricks to the breakup. What is genuinely documented, and more interesting, is Lady Anne herself: an independent-minded woman whose family were thought eccentric to the point of derangement, and who, before her marriage, had a years-long and tenderly written attachment to another woman, Letitia Bushe. The house divided is a wonderful metaphor. It is just not quite a proven one.

Fifty-odd miles north, in Belfast, stands a building whose reputation runs the opposite way — a place where the salacious story is the invention and the truth is quieter. The Crown Liquor Saloon on Great Victoria Street, refitted to its present High Victorian glory in 1885 by the Flanagan family, is the finest surviving gin palace in these islands: polychrome tiles, stained glass full of fairies and pineapples and clowns, a red granite altar of a bar, gaslight, and ten carved wooden snugs. The snugs are the thing everyone wants to talk about. Each has a door that latches, a gunmetal plate for striking matches, and a bell-push that once summoned a waiter without the occupants having to show their faces. And the tourism copy, with a nudge, will tell you they were "rumoured" to be used by prostitutes and their clients.

They were not — or at least, nothing whatever supports it. The claim appears nowhere in the statutory listing or in the National Trust's own account of the building; it lives only in the hedged, winking language of guidebooks. What the snugs were actually for is both documented and, if you think about it, more human: privacy. They let respectable Victorians — women of standing, courting couples, clergy, anyone who wanted a drink unobserved in an age that policed being seen far more fiercely than being — enjoy themselves out of the public eye. The doors, the frosted glass, the bell that meant you never had to stand at the bar: this is the architecture of discretion, not of vice. The Crown is a monument to the Victorian genius for keeping the appearance of propriety intact while quietly doing as one pleased, which is a subtler and truer erotic history than the one the postcards are selling.

Cardiff's Tiger Bay demands a different register altogether, because here the moral panic did real harm to real people. Butetown grew up from the 1830s around the coal docks built by the Marquess of Bute, and by the late nineteenth century it was one of Britain's first genuinely multi-ethnic communities — seafarers and their families from Somalia, Yemen, the Caribbean, West Africa, China, Greece and dozens of other places, settled among the boarding houses, pubs and dance halls, with Loudoun Square as its heart. By 1894, according to Cardiff's maritime records, there were 178 lodging houses in Butetown, four times as many as in any other British seaport, and a quarter of them were kept by foreigners — some of them "crimps" who ensnared sailors into debt. There was a dockside sex trade, as there is in every great port. But what the Cardiff and national press fixed upon, obsessively, was not the trade so much as the sight of interracial relationships — white women with Black and Arab men — which contemporaries wrote about in language of open disgust.

That framing turned lethal in June 1919, when returning white servicemen and local crowds attacked Butetown's minority households over four days of rioting. Boarding houses were besieged and burned; families armed themselves and barricaded their doors; broken glass covered Bute Street; and the violence and nearly all the arrests fell on the very residents who had been attacked. Official accounts recorded three deaths across the South Wales disturbances, though eyewitnesses in Butetown put the number higher, at six. The trigger, tellingly, was reported as an incident involving a white woman and a Black man. The lesson the authorities drew was not to protect the community but to restrict it. Loudoun Square's fine Victorian houses, which could have been saved, were swept away in the slum clearances of the 1960s and replaced with tower blocks. The racism belonged to Tiger Bay's accusers, never to Tiger Bay; the district's own memory of itself, preserved now by the Heritage and Cultural Exchange, is of a place its people called home.

And then, for light relief that is also entirely serious, there is Llandudno and the great Victorian panic about mixed bathing. On the beaches of the Conwy resort, as at scores of others, men and women were kept strictly apart in the water by byelaw — separate ladies' and gentlemen's bathing grounds, and the wheeled bathing machines that trundled the bather down to the sea so that no stretch of costumed body need be glimpsed crossing open sand. The costumes themselves, it should be said, covered practically everything: neck-to-knee flannel for women, drawers-and-vest for men, garments that grew heavier and more concealing the wetter they got. What the rules governed, in other words, was not nakedness. It was proximity — the mere possibility that a clothed man and a clothed woman might enter the same water within sight of one another.

The absurdity is best captured by a case the Yorkshire Evening Post reported in 1896: a man visiting Llandudno was summonsed for "unlawfully bathing within 200 yards of the ladies' bathing ground" — the ladies in question being his own three sisters. He had gone for a swim with his family and committed a crime by doing so. Legal segregation of Britain's beaches ended in 1901, and the bathing machines rattled into obsolescence within a couple of decades. The panic connects to a much wider nineteenth-century project of policing the body, which runs from these beaches to the surveillance of the poor and the sex worker; and it makes the argument that binds these four places together. From a divided Irish house to a Belfast snug, from a slandered Cardiff dockland to a Welsh beach, the nineteenth century reserved its heaviest punishments not for the act but for the appearance of impropriety — and, as ever, the punishment fell hardest on women and the poor.

Approach from the garden and it's sober, correct Palladian; walk round and it's exuberant Georgian Gothic — battlements, pointed windows and all. ### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**Lady Anne and Letitia Bushe.** Long before Castle Ward divided down the middle, Lady Anne — then Anne Bligh — carried on a years-long, ardently written attachment to another woman, Letitia Bushe, whose surviving letters ache with the pain of separation: "I seldom am without your Idea." The correspondence continued even after Anne's marriages. It is a far better-documented romance than the architectural one the house is famous for.

**The crown on the floor.** At the Crown's entrance, a crown is set into the mosaic so that everyone crossing the threshold treads on it. The story goes that the Catholic owner, Patrick Flanagan, placed it there deliberately, so his customers would trample the symbol of the British monarchy on their way in. Whether true or embellished, it is a very Belfast way to decorate a doorway.

**The pub workers who ignored the ban.** During the Second World War, local authorities tried to stop Black American GIs mixing in Cardiff's pubs. The pub workers of Butetown simply refused to enforce it. In a district the outside world had spent decades slandering, the people who actually lived and worked there quietly declined to draw the colour line the state was demanding of them.

**The bathing machine as tiny palace.** Not every bathing machine was grim. A luxury one described in 1847 had walls of snow-white enamel, bevel-edged mirrors, a green Japanese rug, a rubber-lined silk bag for wet flannels and a toilet shelf of appliances. It was, in effect, a wheeled changing-boudoir, engineered so that a lady might reach the sea without a single square inch of her being witnessed in transit.

## 13 — The Dark Walks
*Vauxhall Pleasure Gardens · Lambeth, London · 1661–1859*

### BOOK VERSION
Fanny Burney knew exactly what happened at the far end of the gardens, and in 1778 she put it in a novel. In Evelina, her heroine strays with the wrong companions into Vauxhall's unlit avenues and is set upon, seized and very nearly assaulted before she can struggle free. Burney's readers did not need the geography explained. Everyone knew about the Dark Walks. They were the whole reason a certain sort of person went to Vauxhall, and the whole reason a certain sort of parent forbade it.

For most of two centuries Vauxhall was the most famous pleasure garden in the world. It opened as the New Spring Gardens before the Restoration — Samuel Pepys was there in 1662 — and became something extraordinary after 1729, when the entrepreneur Jonathan Tyers took the lease and reinvented it. His grand opening, a masked ridotto al fresco, was held in June 1732 in the presence of Frederick, Prince of Wales. Over the following decades Tyers filled twelve acres on the south bank of the Thames with supper boxes decorated by Francis Hayman, a great Rotunda, an orchestra, triumphal arches, a statue of Handel, thousands upon thousands of oil lamps, and — the young William Wordsworth, visiting at eighteen, was struck by it — a "wilderness of lamps, dimming the stars".

The genius of the place, and the point of it for this book, was the mixing. Admission was a single shilling for most of the eighteenth century, and a shilling bought entry to everyone: royal dukes, duchesses, penurious servants, apprentices on an illicit night out, merchants, soldiers, pickpockets and prostitutes, all promenading the same gravel walks under the same lamps. That was genuinely novel. Nowhere else in Georgian London did the classes rub shoulders so freely, and the erotic charge of the gardens came partly from exactly that — from the possibility of an encounter, romantic or transactional or somewhere between, with someone you would never meet in your ordinary walled-off life. The London Museum's Beatrice Behlen offers a useful yardstick: in the 1760s a housemaid earned about two shillings a week, so a shilling at the gate was half her wages and no small outlay — but it was payable, and pay it she might.

The food was famously terrible and expensive, which only added to the raffish glamour. The ham was the standing joke: sliced, it was said, so thin you could read a newspaper through it, and a single ham was reckoned enough to cover the whole site. In 1817 a small dish of that ham and two tiny chickens cost eleven shillings, with tarts and custards another four. The wine, at least, was good, and the arrack punch — arrack, rum and sugar — was potent enough to explain a good deal of what happened later in the evening.

And then there were the Dark Walks. Tyers, who otherwise lit his gardens like a fairground, deliberately left certain tree-lined avenues at the far edge of the site unlit — the Close Walks, the Lovers' Walk, the Druids' Walk, known collectively as the Dark Walks. This was not an oversight. Darkness was an amenity. Here is where the documentary difficulty begins, and where honesty matters, because the Dark Walks are one of those places whose reputation vastly outruns what can be firmly proved. That prostitutes worked the gardens is not in doubt; the fullest modern account, David Coke and Alan Borg's Vauxhall Gardens: A History (Yale, 2011), treats the erotic possibilities of the place as central to its appeal, and Tyers's very decision to charge a shilling was, in part, an attempt to keep pickpockets and prostitutes out — with, as one historian dryly notes, varying degrees of success. What is harder to document is precisely who did precisely what among the trees, because the vivid scenes we have tend to be either fiction, like Burney's, or the outraged testimony of visitors.

The best of that testimony is genuinely first-hand. The German traveller Karl Philipp Moritz, writing of his supper at Vauxhall in the 1780s, was startled less by any furtive activity in the shadows than by the sheer front of the women working the open tables: he described "the boldness of the women of the town, who often rushed in upon us by half dozens" and importuned the diners for wine. This is the documented voice of the gardens' sexual commerce — not a whisper in the dark but a brisk, public solicitation over supper. The management's own struggle tells the same story. By the later 1750s there were complaints of "dangerous terrors", shouts and screams, coming from the Dark Walks; and around 1764 the proprietors introduced a modest amount of lighting into the main Lovers' Walk. The lamps were resisted and, by some accounts, torn down — because a great many visitors had paid their shilling precisely for the dark, and did not thank Tyers for shining a light on it.

The famous rakes belong in this story, but they must be placed carefully. James Boswell, whose London Journal of 1762–63 is the most candid sexual diary of the age, certainly frequented Vauxhall — he records "parading" there among the aristocracy — and his journal is thick with casual encounters with prostitutes across the city, in the Strand, in St James's Park, on Westminster Bridge. Giacomo Casanova was in London in 1763–64 and knew the pleasure gardens well. But the specific, cinematic image of either man picking up a woman in Vauxhall's Dark Walks is not something the record I consulted actually pins down; their documented adventures happen elsewhere in London, and the Dark Walks' fame rests more on Burney's fiction, Moritz's supper and the management's lamp wars than on a signed confession from a famous libertine. It is a distinction worth keeping, because the whole argument of this chapter is that the pursuit of pleasure in Georgian England was half theatre — and the Dark Walks were, above all, a stage. Their reputation for transgression was itself an attraction, marketed by rumour, sustained by novels, and quite possibly more titillating than the fumbling reality among the damp trees, which — a detail the guidebooks omit — also served the crowd as an open-air latrine.

Vauxhall drew towards its close through the early nineteenth century, undercut by newer, brasher entertainments, and shut for good in 1859. The site was built over. But a corner survives: a public park in Lambeth called Spring Gardens keeps the old name and something of the old ground, and if you stand in it on a summer evening you are standing where duchess and apprentice and business-girl once queued at the same gate for the same shilling. Vauxhall's descendants are the subject of later chapters — the Covent Garden bagnios and the printed catalogue of women that was Harris's List — where the transaction the Dark Walks only hinted at was conducted with a frankness, and a price list, that Jonathan Tyers would never have permitted at his gate.

### WEB VERSION
For most of two centuries Vauxhall was the most famous pleasure garden in the world. It opened as the New Spring Gardens before the Restoration — Pepys was there in 1662 — and became something extraordinary after 1729, when Jonathan Tyers took the lease, staged a masked ridotto al fresco in 1732 before the Prince of Wales, and filled twelve acres on the Thames with supper boxes, an orchestra, a Rotunda, a statue of Handel and thousands of oil lamps.

Its genius was the mixing. Admission was one shilling for most of the eighteenth century, and a shilling admitted everyone — royal dukes, duchesses, servants, apprentices, soldiers, pickpockets and prostitutes, all on the same gravel walks. The food was a joke: the ham sliced so thin you could read a newspaper through it. The arrack punch was not a joke, and explains much.

At the far edge lay the Dark Walks — tree-lined avenues Tyers left deliberately unlit, and the reason a certain sort of person came. Fanny Burney set a near-assault there in Evelina (1778); the German traveller Karl Philipp Moritz recorded the "boldness of the women of the town" importuning diners for wine. Prostitutes certainly worked the gardens, and around 1764 the management tried lighting the Lovers' Walk — only for the lamps to be resisted by patrons who had paid precisely for the dark. The scholarly authority is David Coke and Alan Borg's Vauxhall Gardens: A History (Yale, 2011). Much of the Dark Walks' fame, though, rests on fiction and rumour as much as on record: the reputation for transgression was itself the attraction. Vauxhall closed in 1859; a Lambeth park called Spring Gardens keeps the name.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**The ham you could read through.** Vauxhall's catering was legendarily bad and dear. The ham was carved so fine that a diner claimed to be able to read a newspaper through a slice, and wits reckoned one ham could cover all twelve acres. In 1817 a small dish of it with two tiny chickens cost eleven shillings — a scandal that became, perversely, part of the fun of the place.

**The lamps that were torn down.** By the 1750s the Dark Walks were generating complaints of screams and "dangerous terrors", and around 1764 the proprietors lit the main Lovers' Walk. It did not take. Many visitors had paid their shilling precisely for the darkness, and the new lamps were resented and, by some accounts, pulled down — a rare instance of customers rioting for less illumination.

**A wilderness of lamps.** The young William Wordsworth visited Vauxhall at eighteen and never forgot the twenty thousand oil lamps twinkling in the trees — "a wilderness of lamps, dimming the stars". For a Georgian Londoner stepping off the river into that glow, the gardens must have felt like walking into another world. The magic and the menace were the same darkness, only differently lit.

## 14 — Do What Thou Wilt
*Hellfire Caves, West Wycombe & Medmenham Abbey · Buckinghamshire · c.1748–1766*

### BOOK VERSION
The source of almost everything you think you know about the Hellfire Club is a novel narrated by a coin, written by a man with a grudge and repeated ever since by people who would rather it were true. That is the correction this piece exists to make, and making it is more fun than the legend, because the legend — Satanism, orgies, black masses in a chalk cave — turns out to be a masterclass in how eighteenth-century England manufactured a scandal.

The facts first. Sir Francis Dashwood, born in 1708 and later 11th Baron le Despencer, was a wealthy, well-travelled, genuinely irreverent man who around 1750 founded a private club he variously called the Order of the Friars of St Francis of Wycombe and the Monks of Medmenham. The name "Hellfire Club" was pinned on it later by outsiders. From 1751 the friars met at Medmenham Abbey, a ruined Cistercian house on the Thames in Buckinghamshire that Dashwood leased and rebuilt in fashionable Gothic, with the Rabelaisian motto Fais ce que tu voudras — "Do what thou wilt" — set in stained glass over the door. The membership was a roll-call of Georgian power and wit: John Wilkes, the radical journalist; John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich; Thomas Potter, son of an Archbishop of Canterbury; the poet Paul Whitehead as steward. Benjamin Franklin, a friend of Dashwood's from their days as the two nations' postmasters, is recorded staying at West Wycombe, though almost certainly as a guest rather than a member.

What did they actually do? On the honest, documented evidence: they dressed as monks, addressed each other as "brother", drank a great deal of very good claret, dined enormously, made mock-monastic toasts to Bacchus and Venus, and enjoyed the company of women — sometimes wives, sometimes courtesans, some of whom the members playfully styled "nuns". They satirised the Catholic Church, which Dashwood had come to despise on his travels, and they satirised monastic piety by inverting it. The History of Parliament's own historians, weighing what can be proved, land on something like posh, clever men having elaborately themed boozy weekends by the river, with a little light paganism for atmosphere. Sex was undoubtedly part of it. Human sacrifice, devil-worship and orgiastic black masses were not.

So where did all that come from? From two directions, both traceable. The first is Charles Johnstone's Chrysal, or the Adventures of a Guinea, a sprawling satirical novel published between 1760 and 1765 in which a magic coin passes from hand to hand narrating the vices of its owners. Chrysal contained thinly veiled, luridly embroidered episodes set unmistakably at Medmenham — including the story that Lord Sandwich, during some mock ritual, mistook a baboon for the Devil and was terrified out of his wits. Readers took the fiction for reportage, and the Medmenham legend was born. The second direction is pure politics. When Dashwood became a spectacularly incompetent Chancellor of the Exchequer in 1762 and pushed through a hated tax on cider, his former friend John Wilkes — denied office and out for revenge — used his anti-government paper the North Briton to leak, embroider and weaponise stories about the club and its now-respectable members. The baboon prank is itself usually attributed to Wilkes, who is said to have smuggled a dressed-up baboon into a ceremony to leap out at Sandwich. Whether Wilkes really did it or merely told everyone he had is, fittingly, impossible to establish. The reputation was, in the most literal sense, propaganda.

The caves are the other half of the myth, and they too dissolve under examination. Beneath West Wycombe Hill, Dashwood had a network of chalk and flint tunnels excavated between 1748 and 1752 — a quarter of a mile of chambers running deep under the hill, entered through a mock-Gothic church front. The legend says they were dug as a subterranean temple for unspeakable rites. The documented reason is almost aggressively wholesome: three successive harvest failures in 1748, 1749 and 1750 had thrown the local labourers out of work, and Dashwood, who owned most of West Wycombe, paid the destitute a shilling a day to dig, using the excavated chalk to build a badly needed new straight road from West Wycombe to High Wycombe. It was a Georgian public-works relief scheme. The club may have held a meeting or two underground; the elaborate association of caves and orgies came later, much of it invented or embroidered by the guides who ran the caves as a tourist attraction from 1951, because ghost stories and Black Masses earn better tips than road-building.

And here is the part the legend cannot accommodate at all: the same Sir Francis Dashwood who supposedly presided over Satanic rites spent his last years, in 1773, producing on his own press an abridged Book of Common Prayer, in collaboration with Benjamin Franklin — a sincere, rather Enlightenment-minded effort to shorten Anglican services so that busy or elderly worshippers might actually get through them. Franklin trimmed the catechism; Dashwood did the heavy cutting, excising repetitive Psalms and, with a certain dark comedy given his reputation, the line "Cursed is he that lieth with his neighbour's wife". He also helped fund London's first Unitarian chapel. The founder of the Hellfire Club, in other words, ended up as a liturgical reformer. It is the least demonic biography imaginable, and it is the true one.

That is really the whole lesson of the place, and of this chapter's argument. The organised pursuit of pleasure in Georgian England was theatre, and the Hellfire Club was its most accomplished production — a troupe of powerful men playing at wickedness in monks' habits, whose performance of transgression was so much more elaborate than the transgression itself that two and a half centuries of visitors have preferred the costume to the man inside it. Fifty years later a young poet would restage almost exactly the same play, in monks' habits, at another ruined abbey a hundred miles north; and both were, at heart, dressing up as the Restoration rakes of Charles II's court, who had at least had the decency to mean it.

### WEB VERSION
Almost everything lurid you have heard about the Hellfire Club comes from a satirical novel and a political vendetta, not from the club itself. Sir Francis Dashwood (1708–1781) founded his Order of the Friars of St Francis of Wycombe — the "Monks of Medmenham" — around 1750, meeting from 1751 at Medmenham Abbey on the Thames, rebuilt in Gothic with the motto Fais ce que tu voudras, "Do what thou wilt", over the door. Members included John Wilkes, the Earl of Sandwich and Thomas Potter; Benjamin Franklin was a visiting friend.

The documented reality: monks' habits, "brothers", enormous quantities of claret, mock-monastic toasts to Bacchus and Venus, and the company of women some styled "nuns" — clever, powerful men satirising the Church over long boozy weekends. The Satanism, orgies and black masses come from Charles Johnstone's novel Chrysal (1760–65), which set embroidered scenes at Medmenham, and from John Wilkes, who leaked and inflated stories after falling out with the club. The famous prank — Sandwich mistaking a dressed-up baboon for the Devil — is Wilkes's, if it happened at all.

The Hellfire Caves under West Wycombe were dug between 1748 and 1752, not as a temple but as famine relief: after harvest failures, Dashwood paid the poor a shilling a day to excavate chalk for a new road. Later cave-guides invented much of the sinister lore for tourists. And Dashwood himself ended up co-producing, with Franklin, an abridged Book of Common Prayer. Today the caves, the church with its golden ball, and the Dashwood mausoleum at West Wycombe are open; Medmenham Abbey is private.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**The novel narrated by a coin.** The engine of the whole legend is Charles Johnstone's Chrysal, or the Adventures of a Guinea (1760–65), in which a magic gold coin passes between owners recounting their vices, several unmistakably set at Medmenham. Georgian readers took the satire literally, and fiction hardened into "fact". A later 1766 edition piled on more, elaborating on Wilkes's leaks. The Hellfire Club's infamy is, quite precisely, a publishing phenomenon.

**The best globe tavern.** Dashwood rebuilt St Lawrence's Church atop West Wycombe Hill with a great hollow golden ball on the steeple, large enough to seat about six inside. John Wilkes, no admirer by then, conceded it was "the best globe tavern I was ever in". Members are said to have climbed up to drink and talk within the golden sphere — a detail more genuinely eccentric than any invented orgy.

**The Chancellor who couldn't add up.** Dashwood's spell as Chancellor of the Exchequer in 1762–63 was a disaster. His tax on cider was so unpopular it helped bring down the government and got him removed within months. It also handed his enemies their motive: the smearing of the Hellfire Club was, in large part, revenge politics aimed at a man who had briefly and calamitously held the nation's purse.

## 15 — Byron at Home
*Newstead Abbey · Ravenshead, Nottinghamshire · 1798–1818*

### BOOK VERSION
The object was a human skull. A gardener turned it up while digging in the grounds of Newstead — the bones, Byron guessed, of "some jolly friar or monk of the Abbey" from the days before Henry VIII emptied the place — and the young lord, seized by what he called "a strange fancy", sent it to a jeweller in Nottingham to be cleaned, polished and mounted in silver as a drinking cup. It came back, he wrote, "a mottled colour like a tortoiseshell". At his parties it was filled with claret and passed around, and he liked it well enough to write a poem about it, the Lines Inscribed upon a Cup Formed from a Skull, which cheerfully advises the reader that it is "Better to hold the sparkling grape / Than nurse the earth-worm's slimy brood." A skull cup, an inheritance he could not afford, and a genius for turning his own scandals into literature: this is Byron at home.

George Gordon, 6th Baron Byron, inherited Newstead Abbey in 1798, aged ten, from his great-uncle the "Wicked Lord". It was a ruined Augustinian priory converted after the Dissolution into a family seat, and by the time it came to Byron it was falling down — the roof leaking, whole wings uninhabitable, the estate mortgaged to the hilt. He could never afford to restore it properly. Using borrowed money he made a few rooms habitable, hung velvet curtains, and left the rest semi-ruinous, which suited both his purse and his taste for Gothic gloom. He kept a menagerie worthy of the setting: a tame bear he had installed at Cambridge (there being, he noted, no college rule against bears), and at Newstead a wolf and assorted other animals. He used one hall as a pistol gallery and flooded a cloister cell to make a plunge bath.

The parties are documented, and they are exactly as theatrical as they sound. In 1809, before leaving on his travels, Byron gathered his Cambridge friends — John Cam Hobhouse, Charles Skinner Matthews, Scrope Davies — at Newstead, where, in the words of a 1939 county history drawing on their own accounts, "they found amusement in dressing up as monks". They wore habits and cowls, drank the cellar dry of claret and burgundy, and passed the skull cup round after dinner, playing at being the very monks whose home they had inherited and whose bones they were drinking from. It is the same performance the Hellfire Club had staged fifty years earlier and a hundred miles south: aristocrats in monastic fancy-dress, pastiching the piety their ancestors had swept away. Byron even knew it was a performance; the pose was half the pleasure.

His actual sexual life, as opposed to the costume drama, is unusually well documented, because Byron wrote about it himself in letters that survive in many volumes and are long out of copyright. He desired women and men, and said so — to his confidante Lady Melbourne and others — in terms his correspondents understood perfectly, even when the law of the day made the male half of it a hanging matter. Modern scholarship reads him plainly as bisexual. He was frank, too, about the women who worked at Newstead: the servants were not off-limits in his letters, and he wrote about them with a candour that later editors did their best to soften. It is a part of the story that has to be handled without either prurience or false delicacy. These were women in his employ, and the power ran all one way; Byron's own breeziness about it should not be mistaken for their consent to being written about.

Then came 1812, and Byron woke, as he put it, to find himself famous — the first two cantos of Childe Harold's Pilgrimage had made him the sensation of the London season. Into the sensation walked Lady Caroline Lamb, married, clever, reckless, who conducted an affair with him so public it appalled even a society that thrived on affairs. It is Caroline who gave him his epitaph, writing in her journal after their first meeting that he was "mad, bad, and dangerous to know" — a verdict she then spent years proving on herself rather than him. When he cooled and withdrew, as he always did, she pursued him, and is documented to have disguised herself as a page boy to get past his servants and into his rooms. The cross-dressing was not merely a stratagem; it was of a piece with the whole theatrical register of Byron's world, where transgression was performed as much as committed.

The rumour that has clung hardest concerns his half-sister, Augusta Leigh. It must be stated as precisely what it is: a persistent, contemporaneously believed, never-proven allegation that Byron and Augusta were lovers, and that Augusta's daughter Elizabeth Medora, born in 1814, was his. Byron's own letters flirt with it darkly — of the baby he wrote to Lady Melbourne a line that has haunted the question ever since, that "it is not an Ape, and if it is, that must be my fault". His wife came to believe it, his enemies broadcast it, and it did more than anything else to destroy him socially. But it was never established, and honest history leaves it where the evidence leaves it: unproven, and central precisely because it was believed.

The marriage that the rumour helped to wreck was brief and wretched. In January 1815 Byron married Annabella Milbanke — intelligent, mathematical, earnest, whom he had nicknamed the "princess of parallelograms" — and within the year it was over. Their daughter Ada, the future computer pioneer Ada Lovelace, was born in December 1815; weeks later Annabella left, taking the baby, and never returned. In the separation proceedings she gave voice to her suspicions of incest and of sodomy, and the scandal — debts, rumours, the whispers of Augusta — made England impossible for him. In April 1816 Byron left the country for good. He sold Newstead in 1818, to a school friend, Colonel Thomas Wildman, and never saw it again. He died in 1824, aged thirty-six, at Missolonghi, having gone to fight for Greek independence.

Newstead today belongs to Nottingham City Council, and you can walk through the rooms Byron made habitable, see his private apartments and the gilded bed he brought from Cambridge, and wander the gardens laid out among the old monastic ruins. There is one memorial there he built himself, and it is not to a lover or a friend. In the garden stands a monument to his Newfoundland dog, Boatswain, over an inscription mourning an animal who possessed, Byron wrote, "all the Virtues of Man without his Vices". Of all the epitaphs at Newstead — the skull's, Caroline's, his own — it is the only one he wrote without a trace of performance.

### WEB VERSION
George Gordon, 6th Baron Byron, inherited Newstead Abbey — a ruined Augustinian priory turned family seat in Nottinghamshire — in 1798, aged ten. It was falling down and mortgaged to the hilt, and he could never afford to restore it; he made a few rooms habitable, hung velvet curtains, kept a wolf and other animals (a tame bear lived at Cambridge), and left the rest romantically ruinous.

The parties are real. In 1809 he gathered his Cambridge friends, who "found amusement in dressing up as monks", drank the cellar dry, and passed round a cup made from a human skull a gardener had dug up in the grounds — polished, silver-mounted, and celebrated in Byron's own poem Lines Inscribed upon a Cup Formed from a Skull. It was aristocratic monastic fancy-dress, the same game the Hellfire Club had played fifty years earlier.

His sexual life is documented in his own letters, long out of copyright: he desired women and men, and modern scholarship reads him as bisexual. In 1812 he became famous overnight and began a scandalous affair with Lady Caroline Lamb, who called him "mad, bad, and dangerous to know" and is documented to have dressed as a page boy to reach him. The persistent, contemporaneously believed but never-proven rumour that he and his half-sister Augusta Leigh were lovers helped destroy his marriage to Annabella Milbanke — mother of Ada Lovelace — and drove him into exile in 1816. He sold Newstead in 1818 and died in Greece in 1824. The house, his rooms and bed, the gardens and the memorial to his dog Boatswain survive under Nottingham City Council.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**"Without his Vices".** The one monument Byron built at Newstead honours his Newfoundland dog, Boatswain, who died of rabies in 1808. Byron reportedly nursed him without fear of the disease, and raised over the grave an inscription praising a creature with "all the Virtues of Man without his Vices". He wanted to be buried beside the dog. It is the least ironic thing he ever did.

**The bear at Cambridge.** Forbidden to keep a dog at Trinity College, Byron found there was no rule against bears, and installed one — suggesting, straight-faced, that it should sit for a college fellowship. The animal later joined the menagerie at Newstead alongside a wolf. The gesture was pure Byron: an elaborate, well-publicised joke at authority's expense, staged for maximum audience.

**The skull cup's disappearance.** Byron's silver-mounted skull cup was real, inscribed with his own verses, and produced after dinner brimming with claret. Its later fate is murky: it is said to have been quietly reburied around a century after his death, and the versions shown at Newstead now are replicas. A cup made from a monk's skull, filled with wine by a poet drinking in the monk's own ruined house, was perhaps too perfect a relic to keep on a shelf.
