## 26 — Water Will Take Anything You Bring It
*Ffynnon Elian · Ffynnon Fair · St Winefride's Well · Ynys Llanddwyn · North Wales · c.5th century–present*

### BOOK VERSION

In 1925, someone clearing the site of a demolished well near Llanelian-yn-Rhos, Conwy, turned up a small piece of slate about three inches long. Scratched around its border were crossed lines; in the centre, the initials R.F.; in the corners, more letters. Pinned to the middle of the slate was a little wax figure — head, body, legs, one arm, the left arm broken off. Nobody made this to be found. Somebody made it to kill a neighbour.

This was Ffynnon Elian, the most feared well in Wales, and for the better part of a century it did a brisk trade in hatred. Like almost every holy well in the country it had begun respectably, a healing spring supposedly called forth by a sixth-century saint, visited by the sick who left a groat or its worth in bread and tried, as part of the ritual, to empty the well three times. Then, in the last quarter of the eighteenth century, the reputation curdled. Thomas Pennant, whose *Tours in Wales* recorded the well around this time, noted drily that people came "to imprecate their neighbours, and to request the saint to afflict with sudden death, or with some great misfortune, any persons who may have offended them." Pennant added, with the air of a man who had learned to keep his opinions to himself, that a local had once threatened him with the vengeance of St Elian.

The mechanism was precise and it was not free. To curse someone, you gave the keeper the victim's name; it was written in a register and scratched onto slate, or on parchment folded into lead, and dropped into the water while imprecations were spoken. Sometimes a wax effigy was stuck with pins, sometimes a frog was skewered and floated, corks and pins bobbing on the surface. The curser was given well-water to drink and to pour over his head, three times. A shilling laid the curse. Lifting one cost considerably more — which was, of course, where the real money lay, because the cursed came too, sick with fright, and paid to be released. One keeper was reckoned to be earning close to £300 a year, a startling sum, from selling misfortune and then selling the cure for it.

The keepers are the human comedy here. The most notorious was a tailor, John Evans, who styled himself Jac Ffynnon Elian. When the local Methodists, appalled, had the well destroyed around 1829, Evans simply diverted the spring onto his own land and carried on. He was jailed twice for fraud and eventually confessed that the whole business was bogus — that he had invented the terror and sold it — before, in the neat manner of the reformed, joining the Baptists. By about 1850 the well was finished as a going concern. It survives today only as a filled-in site on private farmland with no public access; a slate plaque marks it, and a spring feeds a house. What you can visit is the church of St Elian nearby, whose late-medieval painted rood-screen panels — now fixed to the north wall of the nave — are among the best surviving church decoration in this part of Wales. The best modern account is Richard Suggett's *A History of Witchcraft and Magic in Wales*.

Twenty-odd miles south-west, near St Asaph in Denbighshire, is the opposite of a cursing well: a well people married at. Ffynnon Fair — St Mary's Well — sits beside the ruined, cruciform chapel on the banks of the River Elwy, its spring rising into a beautiful eight-pointed star-shaped basin, closely modelled on the one at Holywell. It is often called "the Welsh Gretna Green," and this needs stamping on. The label rests almost entirely on a single manuscript, *Y Cwtta Cyfarwydd*, the chronicle kept by a local notary, Peter Roberts, between 1607 and 1646. Roberts records seven marriages at the chapel between 1611 and 1640, two of them described as "clandestine" — the first, in February 1611, being that of his own brother-in-law. The romantic cluster people point to is really four sisters of the Lloyd family of nearby Wigfair marrying at the family's convenient local chapel over some years; only one of those, Elizabeth Lloyd's in 1626, is recorded as clandestine. As the well historians who have gone back to the source point out, clandestine marriages happened all over the place, in private houses as much as chapels, and seven weddings in thirty years does not make a wedding factory. The "Welsh Gretna Green" is Victorian romance laid over an ordinary parish record. The Victorians did rather more than romanticise it: they rebuilt the site as a landscaped picnic walk, and the little bath by the spring is a Victorian addition, an architectural flourish rather than anything a pilgrim used. The poet Felicia Hemans, who lived near St Asaph, wrote her "Our Lady's Well" about the place. Like Ffynnon Elian, it is on private farmland; permission is needed to visit.

The star-shaped basin at Ffynnon Fair copies the one at Holywell because Holywell is the mother of them all. St Winefride's Well in Flintshire — the "Lourdes of Wales" — has been a place of pilgrimage for something like 1,300 years, the only site in Britain with an unbroken tradition of public pilgrimage across the Reformation. Its founding story must be handled with care, because it is a story of attempted rape and a beheading, and it should be read as documented medieval hagiography rather than anything else. According to the two twelfth-century Latin Lives — one written by Robert, prior of Shrewsbury, some five hundred years after the events they describe — Winefride (Gwenffrewi) was a seventh-century noblewoman who refused the advances of a chieftain's son, Caradog. Enraged, he cut off her head; where it came to rest a spring burst from the ground. Her uncle, St Beuno, replaced the head and prayed, and she was restored to life, marked forever by a thin white scar. Caradog, in the telling, was swallowed by the earth and effectively vanishes from the story, while Winefride goes on to become an abbess, a saint, and the patron invoked against unwanted advances. It is worth noticing what the medieval church did with this: it turned a woman's refusal, and her violent punishment for it, into a cult of healing and holiness, and quietly disposed of the man. She becomes everything; he becomes a hole in the ground.

The physical practice is unbroken and specific. The shrine building, a superb Perpendicular Gothic structure over the spring, was built around 1500 and is usually credited to Margaret Beaufort, mother of Henry VII, which is very likely why the shrine escaped the worst of the Reformation. The spring rises into a star-shaped inner pool. The traditional method of bathing, still followed, is to pass three times through this inner pool while reciting a decade of the Rosary, then to move to the outer pool and kneel on a submerged stone — St Beuno's Stone — for as long as the prayer takes. The triple immersion comes straight out of Robert of Shrewsbury's Life, in which Beuno prophesies that whoever asks Winefride's aid will obtain his wish "at the first, or the second, or certainly the third petition." The well is Grade I listed and a Scheduled Monument; in November 2023 the Catholic Bishops' Conference of England and Wales elevated it to National Shrine status, and it attracts around 30,000 visitors a year. It is run by the Roman Catholic Diocese of Wrexham and welcomes anyone. Pilgrims still line up to kiss a fragment of the saint's finger bone.

The fourth well is the tenderest. Off the south-west corner of Anglesey lies Ynys Llanddwyn (Llanddwyn Island), a tidal islet named for St Dwynwen, the Welsh patron saint of lovers, who according to legend lived here in the fifth century. The story exists in several versions but runs, broadly, like this: Dwynwen, a daughter of the prince Brychan Brycheiniog, loved a young man called Maelon Dafodrill; the match was refused, or she rejected him, and in her distress she prayed to be released from her love. An angel brought a potion that turned Maelon to ice. God then granted her three wishes: that Maelon be thawed, that all true lovers should find happiness through her, and that she herself should never marry. She became a hermit and died here around AD 460. On the island are the ruins of a sixteenth-century chapel, built on the site of her original one, and Ffynnon Dwynwen, her well. The divination practised there is charming and precise: a woman would scatter breadcrumbs on the surface of the water and lay her handkerchief on top, and if the sacred eels living in the well disturbed the cloth, her lover would be faithful. So many pilgrims came, and left so much, that in Tudor times this was among the richest parishes in the region — pilgrim offerings supplied the great bulk of the church's income in 1535. Dwynwen's feast, Dydd Santes Dwynwen on 25 January, is the Welsh St Valentine's Day, and unlike a great deal of "ancient" custom it is genuinely being revived, with cards, gifts and a busy modern trade in Welsh romance.

Four wells, four uses. The Welsh took their marriages to the water, and their curses, and their divinations, and their broken hearts, and the church absorbed the lot — building chapels over springs, appointing saints to eels, charging a groat here and a shilling there. Water was one of the two great folk technologies of desire in Wales; the standing stones of an earlier chapter were the other. What is striking, looking at all four together, is how little the difference between blessing and cursing really was. The same triple immersion cured a child at Holywell and killed a neighbour at Llanelian. It depended entirely on what you brought to the water — and, as Jac Ffynnon Elian understood perfectly, on who was standing beside it holding out his hand.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)

**ffynnon-elian-cursing-well**
For roughly a century, from the late 1700s to about 1850, Ffynnon Elian near Llanelian-yn-Rhos was the most feared well in Wales — not a healing well but a cursing well. To curse an enemy you gave the keeper their name; it went into a register and onto a scrap of slate, dropped into the water with the appropriate imprecations, sometimes accompanied by a wax figure stuck with pins. The curser drank the water and poured it over his head three times. A shilling laid a curse; lifting one cost more — which was the real trade, because the terrified cursed came and paid to be released. One keeper reputedly earned close to £300 a year. The most famous, the tailor John Evans ("Jac Ffynnon Elian"), was jailed twice for fraud and eventually confessed the whole thing was invented. When Methodists destroyed the well around 1829 he simply rerouted the spring onto his own land. Thomas Pennant recorded the cursing custom around 1775; a 1925 excavation found a pinned wax effigy on slate. The well is gone, filled in on private farmland with no access. What survives is nearby St Elian's Church, with remarkable late-medieval painted rood-screen panels now on the nave's north wall.

**ffynnon-fair-secret-weddings**
Ffynnon Fair (St Mary's Well) near St Asaph is a spring rising into an eight-pointed star basin beside a ruined chapel — and it is not, whatever you read, "the Welsh Gretna Green." That label rests on one manuscript, *Y Cwtta Cyfarwydd*, kept by the notary Peter Roberts (1607–1646), which records seven marriages at the chapel between 1611 and 1640, two called "clandestine." The romantic story is really four Lloyd sisters of Wigfair marrying at their handy family chapel; only Elizabeth's, in 1626, is recorded as secret. Clandestine weddings happened everywhere, in private houses as much as chapels. The "runaway marriage" reputation is Victorian invention; so is the pretty little bath by the spring. Felicia Hemans wrote "Our Lady's Well" about the site. It stands on private farmland — permission required.

**st-winefrides-well**
Holywell is the "Lourdes of Wales," a place of unbroken public pilgrimage for some 1,300 years — the only British site whose pilgrimage survived the Reformation. Its founding legend, first written down in two twelfth-century Latin Lives some 500 years after the events, is grim: the seventh-century Winefride refused the chieftain's son Caradog, who beheaded her; a spring rose where her head fell, and her uncle St Beuno restored her to life, leaving a white scar. Caradog is swallowed by the earth and vanishes; Winefride becomes a saint and the patron invoked against unwanted advances. The medieval church turned a woman's refusal into a healing cult. The shrine, a Perpendicular Gothic building over the spring, is credited to Margaret Beaufort around 1500 — the Tudor connection probably saved it. Pilgrims still bathe by passing three times through the star-shaped inner pool reciting the Rosary, then kneeling on St Beuno's submerged stone. Grade I listed, Scheduled Monument, National Shrine since November 2023, drawing around 30,000 visitors a year.

**st-dwynwen-llanddwyn-island**
Ynys Llanddwyn, a tidal island off Anglesey, is named for St Dwynwen, the Welsh patron saint of lovers. Legend makes her a fifth-century princess who loved Maelon Dafodrill; refused or rejecting him, she prayed to be freed of her love, an angel's potion turned him to ice, and God granted three wishes: that Maelon be thawed, that true lovers find happiness through her, and that she never marry. She became a hermit and died here around AD 460. The island holds a ruined sixteenth-century chapel and Ffynnon Dwynwen, her well, where a woman would scatter breadcrumbs and lay a handkerchief on the water: if the sacred eels disturbed it, her lover was faithful. Offerings made this one of the richest parishes in the region in Tudor times. Her feast, 25 January, is the Welsh Valentine's Day, and genuinely thriving.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The cyff Elian.** The cursing at Llanelian had a companion piece inside the church: an old nail-studded oak alms chest called the cyff Elian, into which a person seeking revenge dropped silver pennies. Church and well worked as a single machine of grievance — pay at one, curse at the other. The chest survives, a rare physical relic of a business that traded almost entirely in fear.

**A well that mended warts and hearts.** Ffynnon Dwynwen was not the only wishing-water on Llanddwyn. The island had several springs, including one whose waters were said to cure warts — less romantic than eels foretelling faithfulness, but a reminder that a "holy" well was expected to be a general-purpose chemist, dispensing cures, curses and love-omens from the same modest puddle.

**The saint who kept greyhounds off the land.** A separate St Eilian's well sits near Llaneilian on Anglesey, tangled in its own legend: the saint, granted land measured by how far his pet doe could run in a day, watched it killed by a greyhound and cursed the ground so no greyhound could ever be kept there. Welsh saints, it turns out, could curse as fluently as any nineteenth-century tailor.

**Gerard Manley Hopkins at the well.** The Jesuit poet Gerard Manley Hopkins, who trained at nearby St Beuno's, visited Holywell and left a short prose account, and drafted a verse drama on Winefride. The star-shaped basin and the cold, ceaseless water clearly worked on him — proof that the well's pull on the imagination long outlasted its medieval heyday, and reached well beyond the Catholic faithful.

## 27 — Caru yn y Gwely, and a Job Application in Wood
*Bundling and the love spoon · rural Wales · 17th–19th century*

### BOOK VERSION

Almost everything you have read about Welsh bundling is from Massachusetts. The board down the middle of the bed, the girl sewn into a bundling sack, her legs bound so that nothing could happen below the waist — these are the details that make the custom sound both hilarious and faintly sinister, and most of them come from New England, or from English travel-writers who had crossed into Wales specifically to be scandalised. If you are going to write about a chapter's worth of invented tradition, as this one is, it will not do to invent a composite Welsh custom out of American parts. So let us be careful, and separate what is actually evidenced for Wales from what is merely famous.

The Welsh custom had a Welsh name: *caru yn y gwely*, "loving in the bed," though the strictly correct form was *caru ar y gwely*, "loving *on* the bed," and it was also called *caru'r nos*, the night-courting. The practicalities that are genuinely documented for Wales are modest. A young man, usually from another farm, would come after dark and knock at the young woman's window with small stones — a practice called *cnoco*, knocking. If admitted, a couple might sit up in the kitchen, or they might go up to the woman's bedroom and spend the night together, clothed, sometimes with a blanket or a bolster between them. The Welsh definitions of the custom are insistent on one point: the couple lay *on* the bed, dressed, and as long as they kept talking, those downstairs assumed nothing improper was happening. The bundling stocking and the plank are much harder to pin to Wales specifically; the most vivid tie-them-up accounts belong to the American tradition catalogued in Henry Reed Stiles's 1871 book on bundling, and to scandalised English visitors like William Bingley, whose *North Wales* gave readers exactly the frisson they wanted.

Why did it exist at all? The dull, sensible answer is distance, cold and poverty. Farms were far apart, houses were small and had no private room, courting happened after a full day's work when there was nowhere warm and unwatched to do it, and — as one contemporary account of the northern English version cheerfully admitted — it saved time, candles and fuel. It was a courtship conducted under the parental roof, with the parents a wall away, and it was widely regarded, by the people who practised it, as neither immoral nor indecent. An official Poor Law inquiry of 1834 recorded that in parts of Wales this was simply "the way in which all courtships amongst the lower class have been carried on from time immemorial."

And then the chapel came for it. Welsh Nonconformity, gathering force through the nineteenth century, disapproved thoroughly, and the seiat — the chapel society — kept discipline, with members hauled up to confess fornication and be punished. The great set-piece of outrage was the 1847 *Reports of the Commissioners of Inquiry into the State of Education in Wales*, the notorious "Blue Books," compiled by three English commissioners — R. R. W. Lingen, Jellynger C. Symons and H. R. Vaughan Johnson — who spoke no Welsh and leaned heavily on Anglican clergy; their report was presented on 1 July 1847. They declared pre-marital unchastity "the scandal of the Principality" and claimed the majority of Welsh women were pregnant before marriage. This was a moral assertion, not a measured one, and it was furiously rebutted at the time — the Welsh minister Ieuan Gwynedd demonstrated that Welsh illegitimacy ran only fractionally above the English rate.

Here is where the temptation to reach for a startling statistic must be resisted, because the honest position is more interesting than a number. There is no reliable figure for how many Welsh brides were pregnant at the altar. The leading modern historian of Welsh illegitimacy, Angela Muir, has shown that the standard demographic technique for calculating prenuptial pregnancy simply cannot be applied to Welsh parish registers — the pool of Welsh surnames is so small, thanks to the patronymic naming system, that you cannot reliably match a baptism to a marriage. The famous "about a third of all brides were pregnant" figure is English (from P. E. H. Hair's studies of English registers) and American (rural New England after independence); it is not a Welsh measurement, and it should never be presented as one. What can be said about Wales is narrower and better sourced: illegitimacy *ratios* in mid-Wales rose across the eighteenth century, and by the nineteenth, Cardiganshire had among the highest illegitimacy rates in England and Wales. Bundling frequently ended, in other words, exactly as anyone would expect a night in bed to end — but the lurid national statistic that everyone quotes was borrowed from somewhere else. The custom got the blame; the numbers were imported. The standard scholarly authority remains Trefor M. Owen's *Welsh Folk Customs*, with Catrin Stevens's *Welsh Courting Customs* the fullest modern treatment.

If bundling is the courtship that has been over-decorated, the love spoon is the courtship object that has been decorated for real. The *llwy garu* is a wooden spoon, carved from a single piece of wood and given by a young man to the woman he hoped to court. The oldest dated Welsh example, from 1667, is at St Fagans National Museum of History near Cardiff, and the tradition is certainly older than that. What matters is what the spoons were *for*. A young man in a poor rural society had no money for jewellery; what he had was long winter evenings, a pocket-knife and something to prove. So he proved it. The symbols accumulated meanings — hearts for love, keys and keyholes for a home and security, links of chain for binding faithfulness, a wheel for the promise to work, an anchor for a sailor, and, most tellingly, wooden balls carved loose inside a pierced cage, one for each child the carver hoped they might have. To carve a chain, or a ball in a cage, from a single unbroken piece of wood takes patience, skill and an extremely steady hand — which was precisely the point. The spoon was not really a love letter. It was a job application in wood: proof that this man had the patience and the fine motor control to make a good and careful husband, submitted for inspection to the girl and, crucially, to her father.

One last thing, because it is nearly always got wrong. The word "spooning," for canoodling, is very widely said to come from the love spoon, and it is a lovely story, and it is not true. The *Oxford English Dictionary* traces "spoon" as a verb meaning to court, sentimentally and foolishly, to 1831, and it derives from an older sense of "spoon" as a simpleton — a shallow, silly person, like the shallow bowl of the utensil. The cuddling sense, lying together "spoon-fashion," is later still, from the 1880s. The Welsh love spoon and the English verb "to spoon" are, disappointingly, unrelated; the connection is folk etymology, which is itself a very good example of exactly the kind of invented tradition this chapter is about.

Keep the love spoon and the bundling bed together in your mind, though, because they are two ends of the same thing: a poor, cold, closely watched society working out how young people could get close enough to marry. And keep them in mind for one more reason, because the story runs straight on into the next chapter. A Warwickshire girl called Anne Hathaway was three months pregnant when she married an eighteen-year-old glover's son in the winter of 1582, and everything the Welsh understood about courtship — that sex came first, that the wedding regularised it, and that this was normal — was understood in Shottery too.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)

**bundling-and-love-spoons**
Most of what circulates about Welsh "bundling" is actually American. The board down the middle of the bed, the girl sewn into a bundling sack, the tied legs — these belong largely to New England, or to English travel-writers touring Wales to be shocked. The genuinely Welsh custom, *caru yn y gwely* ("loving in the bed"; strictly *caru ar y gwely*, "on the bed"), was simpler: a young man came after dark and knocked at the woman's window with pebbles (*cnoco*), and a courting couple might spend the night together, clothed, sometimes with a bolster between them, under the parents' roof. It existed because farms were far apart, houses had no private space, and it saved fuel and candles. The practitioners considered it neither immoral nor indecent; an 1834 inquiry called it the immemorial way courtship was done.

The Welsh Nonconformist chapels campaigned against it, disciplining members through the seiat for fornication, and the 1847 "Blue Books" — compiled by three English commissioners (Lingen, Symons and Vaughan Johnson) who spoke no Welsh — declared unchastity "the scandal of the Principality." That was moral panic, not measurement. In fact no reliable figure exists for Welsh bridal pregnancy: historian Angela Muir has shown the calculation can't be done from Welsh registers, because patronymic surnames make it impossible to match baptisms to marriages. The famous "a third of brides were pregnant" is an English and New England figure, not a Welsh one.

The love spoon (*llwy garu*) is the courtship object that really was elaborate. The oldest dated Welsh example (1667) is at St Fagans. Carved by a young man from a single piece of wood, it demonstrated patience and skill — a job application in wood, submitted to the girl and her father. Symbols acquired meanings: hearts for love, keys and keyholes for a home, chain-links for faithfulness, a wheel for hard work, anchors for sailors, and loose balls in a carved cage for the number of children hoped for. And "spooning"? Despite the charming story, the OED traces the verb to "spoon" meaning a sentimental fool — not to the love spoon at all.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**Knocking up, literally.** The courtship began with *cnoco* — the young man throwing small stones at the woman's window after dark to be let in, also called *cnoco lan*, "knocking up." The phrase has since travelled a long way and acquired a very different meaning, but in rural Wales it meant only the polite, gravel-rattling opening move of a night's supervised courting.

**The spoon that became a soup spoon again.** The earliest love spoons were working utensils, given as tokens but still used to eat cawl. Only over generations did the carving grow so elaborate that the spoon lost its function entirely and moved to the wall as pure decoration. The love spoon's history is a small parable of a practical object slowly consumed by its own symbolism.

**A custom Nonconformity could not quite kill.** Even as chapel elders denounced night-courting, Welsh love songs kept singing about it, some in frank sexual metaphor. The custom lingered in rural areas into the late nineteenth century, outlasting decades of pulpit disapproval — proof that a warm bed on a cold night is a more persuasive preacher than any minister of the seiat.

## 28 — The Green Men Who Were Named in 1939
*Rosslyn Chapel (Collegiate Chapel of St Matthew) · Roslin, Midlothian · founded 1446*

### BOOK VERSION

On any given morning now, the small car park at Roslin fills with people who have come to a fifteenth-century Scottish chapel because of a twenty-first-century American thriller. In the year before Dan Brown published *The Da Vinci Code*, Rosslyn Chapel received 38,141 visitors. The book came out in 2003, ends here, and the following year the figure had risen to 79,916. The film followed in 2006, and that year 175,074 people came through the door; the chapel would later record 181,700 paying visitors in 2017. The money paid for a badly needed conservation programme, which is the chapel's own dry, forgiving verdict on the whole business: the nonsense saved the building. It is a useful place to end a chapter about invented tradition, because Rosslyn is where two entirely different inventions — one recent and commercial, one genteel and academic — sit side by side on the same carved stone.

The chapel itself needs no invention. It was founded in 1446 by William Sinclair (or St Clair), 3rd Earl of Orkney, as a collegiate church to pray for his family's souls; building seems to have got properly under way around 1456, and stopped when Sinclair died in 1484, leaving only the choir of what was planned as a far larger cruciform church. What was finished is one of the most densely carved interiors in Europe — every surface worked into foliage, figures, biblical scenes and geometric patterns of bewildering intricacy. Its showpiece is the Apprentice Pillar, four vines spiralling up out of dragons' mouths, around which clings a legend of the kind that attaches itself to any object too good to be explained: the master mason went abroad to study the design, the apprentice carved it perfectly in his absence, and the master, consumed by jealousy, killed the boy with a blow of his mallet. Two carved heads are pointed out as the pair, condemned to stare at each other across the chapel forever. It is a wonderful story and there is no evidence for a word of it.

But the carvings that concern us are the Green Men. Rosslyn has an extraordinary density of them — foliate heads, human faces with vegetation issuing from the mouth, and sometimes the eyes and ears. The chapel's own fact sheet states there are "over 100," and the Trust's guides cite more than 110 throughout the building; the visitor is invited to hunt them out and to notice that they are said to progress in age around the chapel, young in the east and old in the west, as if aging with the seasons. They are genuinely medieval, genuinely mysterious, and genuinely beautiful.

They are also not called "Green Men" by anyone before 1939. This is the point on which the whole chapter turns. Until the twentieth century these carvings were simply "foliate heads," and no medieval source anywhere explains what they were for or what they were called. Then, in March 1939, an amateur folklorist named Julia Somerset, Lady Raglan, published a short article in the journal *Folklore* titled "The 'Green Man' in Church Architecture." She had been struck by a foliate head in a church at Llangwm in Monmouthshire, and she proposed that these heads were the survival of a pagan vegetation deity — the same figure, she argued, as the Jack-in-the-Green, the May King and Robin Hood, a nature-god worshipped for fertility and hiding in plain sight on Christian walls. It was a bold, romantic idea, arriving at the high tide of Frazer's *Golden Bough* and Margaret Murray's theories of a surviving pagan witch-cult, and it was enormously influential. It is also almost certainly wrong.

The historian Ronald Hutton has been the most careful critic of the fertility-god reading, and the case against it is simple and devastating. There is no medieval evidence that the foliate heads were pagan, or fertility symbols, or worshipped, or even named. Worse, several of the folk figures Lady Raglan lassoed to her theory postdate the carvings by centuries: the Jack-in-the-Green, her key witness, is an eighteenth-century invention — there is no trace of him before 1795. In other words, a genteel folklorist looked at genuinely ancient carvings, connected them to genuinely modern customs, and produced a "pagan survival" that was younger than the carvings by four or five hundred years. To their considerable credit, Rosslyn Chapel's own custodians say so plainly in their published material, conceding that Lady Raglan "had not quite got her facts right" and that many of the folk figures she invoked were invented long after the leaf mask appeared. A heritage body debunking the myth on which half its gift-shop merchandise depends is a rarer and finer thing than any Templar treasure.

Which brings us back to the treasure, and the maize. Rosslyn is the beating heart of a whole industry of invented significance — Knights Templar, Freemasonry, the Holy Grail hidden in a vault, secret musical scores encoded in the carved cubes, and, most enjoyably, the claim that certain carvings depict New World maize and aloe, plants supposedly unknown in Europe when the chapel was built, and therefore proof that a Sinclair sailed to America before Columbus. Botanists are unconvinced that the carvings are maize at all; the "pre-Columbian voyage" rests on the "if such it is" of a stone that might just be stylised foliage. None of it is any older than the enthusiasm that needs it to be true.

What Rosslyn actually is, when you strip the layers away, is a working Episcopal church, still holding services, cared for by the Rosslyn Chapel Trust, containing some of the finest late-medieval carving in Britain — carving whose meaning we honestly do not know. The Green Men peer down from the vaulting, sprouting their leaves, aging from east to west, keeping their secret. And that is the joke of the whole chapter, made in stone: the carvings are five hundred years old, and their famous pagan meaning is younger than the electric light bulb.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The pillar and the mallet.** The Apprentice Pillar — four vines spiralling from dragons' mouths — carries a murder legend: the master mason, away studying the design, returned to find his apprentice had carved it perfectly, and killed the boy with a mallet in a jealous rage. Two carved heads are shown as master and apprentice, doomed to face each other forever. There is no evidence any of it happened.

Its survival owes as much to sheer luck as to any of the mystical protections later attributed to it.

**A grey chapel and a bad idea.** A 1950s "restoration" coated the stonework in a cement-like render meant to protect it. It did the opposite, sealing moisture in and slowly rotting the carvings, and turning the interior its present grey. Undoing that mistake was a central task of the modern, Da Vinci-funded conservation — a reminder that the chapel's worst enemies have usually been its would-be rescuers.

## 29 — The Parsonage Full of Desire
*Haworth Parsonage · Top Withens · Haworth, West Yorkshire · 1820–1855*

### BOOK VERSION

A woman in Brussels took the pieces of a torn-up letter out of a wastepaper basket and sewed them back together with a needle and thread. Her husband had ripped it up; she mended it, in case a scandal ever needed proving. Four of the letters Charlotte Brontë wrote to Constantin Heger survive because of that, and you can still see the stitching. They are the best-documented erotic fact in the whole Brontë story — and they were written by a woman whose life, viewed from outside, appeared to contain nothing of the kind at all.

The Brontës came to Haworth Parsonage in 1820, when Patrick Brontë was appointed perpetual curate. There were six children. The two eldest, Maria and Elizabeth, died in 1825 of the consequences of a brutal school, a loss that shadowed everything after and fed directly into the Lowood chapters of *Jane Eyre*. That left Charlotte, Branwell, Emily and Anne, who grew up inventing elaborate imaginary kingdoms — Angria, Gondal — and filling the loneliness of the moor with worlds of their own making.

Emily's single novel is the strange, dark heart of the family. *Wuthering Heights*, published in 1847 under the name Ellis Bell, is not a love story in any comfortable sense; it is a book about obsession that survives death. Heathcliff's passion for Catherine does not end when she dies — it runs on for eighteen years, and culminates in one of the most disturbing scenes in the English novel, when he bribes the sexton to open her grave so that he can see her face again and arranges for his own coffin to be laid against hers with the adjoining sides removed. Contemporary reviewers were appalled and impressed in roughly equal measure. They called it coarse and brutal; one American magazine wondered how any human being "could have attempted such a book… without committing suicide," and another dismissed it as "a compound of vulgar depravity and unnatural horrors." Running under all the disgust was disbelief: surely a retiring, consumptive parson's daughter could not have imagined this. When Charlotte later revealed that Ellis, Currer and Acton Bell were three sisters, the shock was genuine and, for some, faintly indecent.

But the best-documented erotic fact in the entire Brontë story is not Emily's fiction. It is Charlotte's heartbreak, and it survives on paper. In 1842 Charlotte and Emily went to Brussels to study at the Pensionnat Heger, and Charlotte fell in love with Constantin Heger, the school's brilliant, exacting director of studies — a married man with children, the first person outside her family to take her seriously as an intellect. She returned to Haworth in 1844 and wrote him letters of extraordinary, naked longing. He did not reply. He tore them up. And here the story becomes almost unbearably poignant, because his wife, Zoë Heger, retrieved the pieces from the wastepaper basket and stitched them back together with needle and thread, mending the torn paper so that a scandal could be proved if one were ever needed. Four of those repaired letters survive, and you can see the stitching. They were donated to the British Library in 1913 by Heger's son, Dr Paul Heger, and his sisters, and printed in *The Times* on 29 July 1913 — described and translated by Marion H. Spielmann — and they are the raw material out of which Charlotte built her fiction: Heger is written across Rochester in *Jane Eyre*, and openly across Paul Emanuel in *Villette*, the novel she set explicitly in Brussels. In one of the letters she tells him, plainly, that if he withholds his friendship entirely she will be "altogether without hope." He had, at least once, scribbled a Brussels shoemaker's address in the margin of her anguish.

Charlotte did marry, eventually, and late. In June 1854 she married her father's curate, Arthur Bell Nicholls — a steady, unglamorous man her father initially opposed. She was happy, by the surviving accounts, for a few months. She died in March 1855, at thirty-eight, in the early stages of pregnancy. It is a bleak arithmetic: the woman who wrote the most famous marriage plot in English literature got less than a year of her own.

Three miles across Haworth Moor stands the ruin that the world insists on calling Wuthering Heights, and it is the perfect place to end, because the people who look after it tell the truth about it. Top Withens is a roofless farmhouse in a superbly bleak position, long associated with Emily's novel. In 1964 the Brontë Society fixed a plaque to its wall which is a small masterpiece of honesty. The building, it says, "even when complete, bore no resemblance to the house she described, but the situation may have been in her mind when she wrote of the moorland setting of the Heights." And then, drily: "This plaque has been placed here in response to many inquiries." The association was never Emily's; it seems to have come from her sister's friend Ellen Nussey, who suggested Top Withens to a publisher's artist illustrating the novels in the 1870s, and the illustration did the rest. Scholars think the actual building described in the novel owes more to the now-demolished High Sunderland Hall near Halifax. Top Withens is not the house. But the walk out to it — along the Pennine Way and the Brontë Way, across the moor the sisters actually walked, with the wind doing precisely what "wuthering" means — gives you the one thing that really was Emily's, which was never a building at all.

You can still climb up there, past a few wind-bent trees, to a ruin that was never the thing it is famous for. It is oddly fitting. The Brontës' whole achievement was to build enormous interior worlds out of almost nothing — out of a parsonage, a graveyard, a moor and a great deal of longing that had nowhere else to go. The plaque tells the truth, and the truth is better than the legend: the house was never real, and it never needed to be.

## 30 — The Bond at Worcester
*Anne Hathaway's Cottage · Shottery, Warwickshire · 1556–1623 (marriage 1582)*

### BOOK VERSION

On 28 November 1582, two Warwickshire farmers named Fulk Sandells and John Richardson rode to Worcester and put their names to a bond for £40 — a serious sum, the price of guaranteeing that a marriage could go ahead. The bond indemnified the Bishop of Worcester against any legal trouble arising from the union of "William Shagspere" and "Anne Hathwey of Stratford… maiden." It permitted the banns to be read only once, rather than the usual three times. This document, dry as a mortgage, is very nearly all we have of the beginning of the most famous marriage in English literature, and almost everything people think they know about it is either an insult or a mistake.

Start with the mistake, because it is a good one. The day before the bond, on 27 November, the Bishop's register recorded a licence issued for the marriage of "Willelmum Shaxpere" and — here is the problem — "Annam Whateley de Temple Grafton." Anne Whateley, of a different village, on a different day. For more than a century this has fuelled a small romantic industry: that Shakespeare loved one Anne and was forced to marry another, that Anne Whateley was the true beloved, the Dark Lady, the road not taken. It makes a lovely story and the scholarship is firmly against it. The overwhelming consensus, following the analysis of J. W. Gray, is that "Whateley" is a clerical error — the register clerk was demonstrably careless, a Whateley appears elsewhere on his pages, and the surnames are close enough to muddle. No independent evidence has ever been found that an Anne Whateley of Temple Grafton existed at all. She is, in one biographer's lovely phrase, a ghost haunting the edges of Shakespeare's story. The likelier truth is duller and warmer: William, aged eighteen and a minor, and Anne, aged twenty-six, needed to marry quickly and quietly, before the Advent season closed the church to weddings, and the single reading of the banns bought them the speed.

Why the hurry? Because Anne was pregnant. Their daughter Susanna was baptised on 26 May 1583, roughly six months after the wedding, which puts Anne about three months gone at the altar. And here the Shakespeare story runs straight back into the Welsh hills of the previous chapter, because this was entirely ordinary. In Elizabethan Warwickshire, as in rural Wales, sex commonly came before the wedding and the wedding regularised it. Betrothal — the troth-plight, the handfast — was itself a binding promise, and a couple who had pledged themselves and then slept together were not thought to have done anything scandalous, provided they married before the child arrived. Anne Hathaway pregnant at her wedding is not evidence of disgrace, or of a reluctant boy trapped by an older woman. It is evidence that she and William did what courting couples across Britain did, from the bundling beds of Cardiganshire to the farmhouses of Shottery.

Anne had grown up at that farmhouse — Hewlands Farm, in the hamlet of Shottery a mile west of Stratford, the twelve-roomed house now known to the world as Anne Hathaway's Cottage. Her father Richard was a prosperous yeoman farmer; the oldest timbers of the house have been dated to 1463, and thirteen generations of Hathaways lived in it. It is where William courted her, and it is where, presumably, Susanna was conceived. Two years after the marriage, in 1585, came the twins, Hamnet and Judith. And then Shakespeare went to London, and largely stayed there, for the better part of two decades, coming home to a wife who kept his household in Stratford while he built a career and a reputation a hundred miles away.

Which brings us to the insult — the second-best bed. Shakespeare's will of 1616 leaves Anne, almost as an afterthought interlined into the document, "my second best bed with the furniture" — the hangings and linen that went with it. For centuries this was read as a deliberate snub, a husband's final cold shoulder to a wife he had come to despise. The best modern reading is very nearly the opposite. In a well-appointed Elizabethan house, the best bed was the guest bed, kept for show; the second-best bed was the marital bed, the one the couple actually slept in, in which their children had been born. To leave Anne that bed was to leave her the most intimate object in the house. And she was in any case entitled by common law to a widow's third of the estate, so the will's silence about the rest is not the abandonment it looks like. Germaine Greer built her 2007 book *Shakespeare's Wife* around exactly this rehabilitation — arguing that Anne was a capable, independent woman who ran a business and a household, not the dreary millstone that four centuries of male biographers, irritated that Shakespeare should have a wife at all, had made of her.

The cottage itself is now the gentlest of the Shakespeare Birthplace Trust's properties, which has owned it since 1892. Its famous garden is a 1920s creation, laid out on the advice of the horticulturist Ellen Willmott; the real Elizabethan plot would have been herbs, vegetables, pigs and mud. In 1969 a fire badly damaged the building, and the Trust restored it. Inside are genuine Hathaway heirlooms, including an ornately carved oak bed whose frame dates to Anne's lifetime — and which visitors, and generations of guides, have longed to identify as the second-best bed. The Trust is honest enough to say there is no evidence for it. Which leaves Anne Hathaway roughly where the documents leave her: pregnant at her wedding like many a bride across Britain, married in a hurry to a teenager who became the greatest writer in the language, left behind while he went to work, and remembered, unfairly, for a bed. She outlived him by seven years, and was buried beside him in Holy Trinity in 1623, under a stone that asks, in Latin, for her not to be moved.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The shepherd's will that proves her name.** One of the surest pieces of evidence tying "Anne" to Shakespeare is not romantic at all. In 1601 Thomas Whittington, a Shottery shepherd who had worked for Anne's father, left in his will forty shillings "in the hand of Anne Shaxspere wyf unto Mr Wyllyam Shaxspere." A shepherd's bookkeeping, four centuries on, quietly confirms the marriage the poets have argued about ever since.

**A garden that never was.** The cottage's celebrated flower garden, all colour and mown paths, was largely created in the 1920s on the advice of the horticulturist Ellen Willmott, after a new sewer destroyed the earlier plot. The Elizabethan garden Anne knew would have been strong-smelling herbs, a cabbage patch, and pigs and chickens at the front — practical, pungent and nothing like a postcard.

**The courting bench.** Guides at the cottage have long pointed out a settle beside the parlour fire as the "courting bench," where William supposedly sat with Anne. There is no proving it, but the Hathaways told the story to visitors for generations. Like the second-best bed, it is a piece of family memory that hardened, over centuries, into a shrine.
