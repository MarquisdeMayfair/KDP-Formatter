## 46 — Julian, in a Bandage
*Knole & Sissinghurst Castle · Kent, England · 1892–1962*

### BOOK VERSION

The document was in a Gladstone bag, locked, in the tower room at Sissinghurst, and it had been there for the best part of forty years. When Vita Sackville-West died in 1962 her younger son, Nigel Nicolson, found it and read what his mother had written in 1920, when she was twenty-eight and had just come back from the edge of throwing her whole life away. He sat on the discovery for a decade. Then, in 1973, he published it, wrapped in three chapters of his own, as *Portrait of a Marriage* — one of the most extraordinary sexual confessions in the English language, and certainly the frankest ever written by a woman of her class about loving other women.

Start with the wound that never healed, because Vita never stopped pressing it. She was born at Knole, in Kent, a house so vast it was said to have a room for every day of the year, held by the Sackvilles since Elizabeth I gave it to a cousin in 1566. Vita grew up there, the only child, showing visitors round it, loving it the way other people love a person. And she could not have it. The estate was entailed to the male line; when her father died in 1928 it went to her uncle and then her cousin, and Vita — who by every measure of feeling was Knole's true heir — got nothing but the ache. She put it exactly once, in a letter, and never bettered it: her exile from Knole, she wrote, was like having had a liaison for years with a beautiful woman who, from force of circumstances, never wholly belonged to her. She turned her disinheritance into a metaphor for lesbian longing. Only Vita would have done that.

In 1913 she married Harold Nicolson, a diplomat, in the chapel at Knole. Both of them were, in the language of the time, homosexual; both knew it; and the marriage they built lasted almost fifty years and outlasted every affair either of them had, which was a great many. It has become fashionable to call it an open marriage, but that undersells the strangeness and the tenderness of it. They wrote to each other nearly every day when apart. They simply did not accept that loving someone else meant they had stopped loving each other. Harold had his young men. Vita had her women. The centre held.

Then came Violet.

Violet Keppel — later, unwillingly, Violet Trefusis — had known Vita since they were children; the daughter of Alice Keppel, who happened to be Edward VII's mistress, so the whole thing ran in a straight line of magnificent unrespectability. In 1918 the friendship became an affair, and the affair became a conflagration. They went to Cornwall, then to France, and it is here that the most astonishing figure in the whole story appears: Julian.

Julian was Vita. In France and in London, Vita dressed as a young man — a wounded soldier home from the war — and walked out with Violet on her arm. The disguise was specific and it was documented, and the best contemporary account of it comes from the critic John Richardson, reviewing the book for the *New York Review of Books* in 1973, drawing directly on Vita's manuscript: she wore khaki, and a khaki bandage round her head that conveniently hid her hair, and in this get-up she escorted Violet up Piccadilly and around the Palais Royal in Paris. "I never felt so free," she wrote. In Monte Carlo the performance was so complete that a French couple tried to interest the handsome young officer in marrying their daughter, and Vita had to improvise some reminiscences of the front to account for her injury. Her own boast was the thing that carries the whole episode: she felt, stepping off the kerb into Piccadilly, that if she met her own mother face to face her mother would not know her. She was never once caught out.

It is worth pausing on how much nerve this took, and how little it had to do with mere disguise. This was a woman discovering, in wartime London and on the Riviera, that she could move through the world as her true, wanted self — free of the drawing room, free of "Mrs Harold Nicolson," free of Knole and its rules — for the length of an evening. The name Julian she loved enough to give it to the hero of her novel *Challenge*, which she wrote with Violet and which her mother had suppressed in Britain as too obvious.

It nearly ended two marriages. In February 1920 the two women were together in Amiens, on the point of leaving their husbands for good, when Harold Nicolson and Violet's husband Denys Trefusis pursued them across the Channel — flying, in a small two-seater aeroplane, in an era when that was itself an act of some derring-do — and there were terrible scenes, and the women were talked home. Vita went back to Harold. Violet, who had married Denys on the explicit condition that the marriage never be consummated, went back to a life she had never wanted, and it is important to say plainly that of the four people in this story Violet was the one who paid most. Vita could be imperious, possessive and cruel; she wrote of the affair in the language of conquest, of having Violet, of not caring. Violet loved harder and had less power, and history, which forgave Vita everything and turned her into a National Trust heroine, has been meaner to her.

There was one more great love, and it was gentler. In 1925 Vita began an affair with Virginia Woolf, and out of it Woolf wrote *Orlando* (1928), the novel whose hero changes sex across four centuries and which is, transparently, a portrait of Vita and of Knole — the house Woolf gave her in fiction because the law would not give it to her in life. Nigel Nicolson called it the longest and most charming love letter in literature, and the original manuscript now lives, with a neatness that would have made Vita weep, at Knole.

And Sissinghurst. In 1930, still raw from losing Knole, Vita and Harold bought a wrecked Elizabethan castle in the Kent Weald — a ruined tower, a moat, some derelict farm buildings, weeds to the horizon. It had once belonged, by marriage, to her own Sackville ancestors, which she felt as a kind of restitution. They made it habitable and then, over thirty years, they made the garden: a series of outdoor rooms divided by hedge and old brick, laid out across barely five acres, which the National Trust reports draws more than 150,000 visitors a year and is the most visited garden in England. Harold drew the bones of it, the sightlines and the walls. Vita filled it, with a planter's ruthlessness and a poet's eye. She wrote in the tower, up a spiral stair, in a room almost nobody was allowed to enter.

She had wanted a house she could not have. She built one instead, room by room, out of hedges — walls of yew for the walls of stone she had lost. The confession stayed in its bag until she was safely dead. Her son let it out, and in doing so did for his mother exactly what she had spent her life refusing to do for herself: he said it out loud.

Vita Sackville-West was born at Knole in 1892 and loved it the way most people love a person — and could not inherit it, because she was a woman. The great house, held by the Sackvilles since 1566, passed at her father's death in 1928 to her uncle and then her cousin. It was the wound of her life. She turned it, characteristically, into a metaphor for desire, writing that her exile from Knole felt like a liaison with a beautiful woman who never wholly belonged to her.

Knole shaped everything she wrote. When Virginia Woolf, her lover from 1925, wrote *Orlando* (1928) — the novel whose hero lives for centuries and changes sex — she gave the fictional Vita the house the real Vita had been denied. The original manuscript now hangs at Knole, inscribed to her. Her son Nigel Nicolson called the book the longest and most charming love letter in literature. Knole, meanwhile, still keeps a Sackville family lease, and still shows the rooms where the child Vita once led visitors by the hand.

### WEB VERSION — sissinghurst-vita-sackville-west

Vita and her husband Harold Nicolson bought Sissinghurst in 1930: a ruined Elizabethan castle in the Kent Weald, a tower and a moat and a great deal of weed. It had belonged, generations back, to Vita's own ancestors, and she treated buying it as a kind of homecoming — a replacement for Knole, the house she had lost for the crime of being female.

They built the garden together over thirty years. Harold laid out the structure, the vistas and walls; Vita did the planting, room by hedged room, including the famous White Garden. On just five acres it is the most visited garden in England, with the National Trust reporting over 150,000 visitors a year. She wrote up in the tower, in a sanctum few were allowed to enter, having quietly locked away, in a Gladstone bag, the 1920 manuscript in which she confessed her elopements with Violet Trefusis and her nights out as "Julian," a young man in a khaki bandage. Her son found it after her death and published it as *Portrait of a Marriage* (1973).

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The two-seater to Amiens.** In February 1920 Vita and Violet were in Amiens, poised to leave their husbands for good. Harold Nicolson and Denys Trefusis gave chase across the Channel in a small two-seater aeroplane — genuine derring-do in 1920 — and retrieved their wives amid furious scenes. Vita went home to Harold. Violet, married on the condition the marriage stay unconsummated, went back to a life she had never chosen.

**The novel her mother banned.** Vita and Violet wrote a novel together, *Challenge*, in which the hero is called Julian — Vita's own male alter ego — and the heroine, Eve, is Violet. Vita's mother, Lady Sackville, found the portrait so nakedly obvious that she had publication in Britain blocked. It came out in America in 1923 and was not published in the UK until 1974.

**The garden as consolation.** Sissinghurst's head gardener has said Vita, in despair at losing Knole, realised she could redeem the loss by making something beautiful — not in brick, but in planting. The outdoor "rooms" divided by yew and old wall have been read as an attempt to rebuild Knole's architecture in living green: hedges for walls, lawns for carpets, flowers for furnishings.

## 47 — Two Quiet Households
*Millthorpe & Brackenburn · Derbyshire and Cumbria, England · 1883–1941*

### BOOK VERSION

A man touched another man on the backside, gently, just above the buttocks, and out of it came a novel that could not be published for fifty-seven years. That is not a joke, and the man it happened to did not treat it as one. It is one of the strangest and most touching moments in English literary history, and it happened in a smallholding in Derbyshire, and to understand it you have to meet the couple who lived there.

Edward Carpenter was born in 1844 into comfortable Hove respectability, took holy orders, held a Cambridge fellowship, and then, in his late thirties, walked away from all of it. He had read Whitman; he had decided that the whole apparatus of Victorian middle-class life was a kind of disease; and in 1883, with money inherited from his parents, he bought seven acres at Millthorpe, near Sheffield, and set about living by hand — growing vegetables, making sandals, writing. What he was really doing, though it took him years to say so plainly, was building a place where he could love men without hiding.

In 1891, on a train, he met George Merrill. Merrill was from the Sheffield slums, working-class to the bone, no formal education, plain-spoken, flirtatious, fond of a drink — about as far from a Cambridge fellow as it was possible to get. In 1898 Merrill moved into Millthorpe, arriving in a blizzard, wheeling all his worldly goods over the hills in a handcart. He stayed for thirty years. The two men lived openly as a couple, cooking, keeping house, receiving visitors, for the better part of four decades — and they did it in plain sight, in the years on either side of Oscar Wilde's trial, when other men were burning their letters and fleeing the country. Officially, for the census and the electoral roll, Merrill was the servant. Everyone who visited understood perfectly well that he was not.

And they came in numbers, up the lane to Millthorpe, to see the prophet — socialists, vegetarians, suffragists, poets. Carpenter had put into print what almost no one else dared: *Homogenic Love* in 1894, and *The Intermediate Sex* in 1908, among the first works in English to argue calmly and in public that love between people of the same sex was natural, valuable, and no crime against anything. He wrote it in the very decade Wilde was jailed for living it. The two men make a matched pair across the chapter — Wilde broken by the law, Carpenter quietly evading it, both of them saying, in their different registers, the same forbidden thing.

Among the pilgrims, in 1912, came a shy novelist named E. M. Forster. And George Merrill, who by all accounts touched most people, touched him — laid a hand on him just above the buttocks. Forster remembered it for the rest of his life, and described it, in the Terminal Note he wrote for *Maurice* in 1960, with a precision that has become famous: the touch, he said, "seemed to go straight through the small of my back into my ideas, without involving my thoughts." He went home and began to write *Maurice*, a novel about a middle-class Englishman who finds love, and lasting happiness, in the arms of a working-class gamekeeper. Carpenter and Merrill are Maurice and Alec Scudder; the cross-class household at Millthorpe is the model for the ending, the happy ending, the one that says two men of different classes could simply walk off into the greenwood together and live.

And Forster put it in a drawer. He showed it to trusted friends — Sassoon, Strachey, Isherwood — and refused, for the rest of his life, to publish it, because it ended happily and he knew that a happy ending was the one thing the law and the public would not forgive. *Maurice*, written in 1913–14, appeared in 1971, the year after Forster died, fifty-seven years after he wrote it. That suppression is the point. The most hopeful gay novel in early English modernism existed the whole time, complete, and its author dared not let it out. The record did not go quiet by accident. Forster silenced it himself, out of fear that was entirely rational.

Fifty miles north and forty years on, another man built another quiet household, and could not speak of it either. Sir Hugh Walpole was, in the 1920s and 1930s, one of the best-selling novelists in the English-speaking world — thirty-odd novels, lecture tours of America, a knighthood in 1937. In 1923 he bought Brackenburn, a house of local slate on the slopes of Catbells above Derwentwater, near Keswick in Cumberland, and called it his little paradise. He filled it with books — some fifteen thousand of them — and paintings, and he wrote there his best-remembered work, the Herries novels, a great family saga rooted in the Borrowdale landscape just across the water.

At the end of 1924 he met Harold Cheevers. Cheevers was a policeman — a former revolver champion of the British Isles, as it happens — married, with children. He left the force and entered Walpole's service as chauffeur, and became, in Walpole's own phrase, his secretary, chauffeur, friend and rock. He was the "perfect friend" Walpole had spent his whole life looking for, and the two were inseparable until Walpole died. The arrangement was discreet to the point of invisibility; Cheevers's wife and children were housed, and the men were careful, because Walpole knew that indiscretion would have cost him the knighthood and very likely his readership. When Walpole died at Brackenburn in 1941, of a heart attack after over-exerting himself at a Keswick war-fundraising march, his obituary in the local paper described him as a bachelor and named his surviving brother and sister. Harold Cheevers, the man who had been beside him for seventeen years, the man to whom he left the house, was not mentioned. That is what the record looked like when the men themselves could not speak: a companion of two decades, edited out of a death notice, present only as an unnamed figure in a photograph.

Walpole is buried at St John's, Keswick, up the hill above the lake. Carpenter and Merrill are buried together in Surrey; when Merrill died in 1928 Carpenter said there was only the end to be desired, and followed him within the year. Two households, two counties, two ways of loving in a country that forbade it. One man wrote it down and dared not publish; another lived it and dared not name it. The difference between Millthorpe and Brackenburn is the difference between a life lived almost openly and a life lived almost in code — and the thing to hold onto is that both households lasted, both were happy, and both were real.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The handcart in the blizzard.** When George Merrill moved into Millthorpe in February 1898, he arrived in a snowstorm, trundling everything he owned over the hills in a handcart with the help of two boys. Carpenter marked the arrival with a poem, "Hafiz to the Cupbearer," folded into his long work *Towards Democracy*. It was the beginning of a partnership that lasted until Merrill's death thirty years later.

**The novel that fed another novel.** D. H. Lawrence read the manuscript of *Maurice*, and Carpenter and Merrill's cross-class household — the gentleman and the gamekeeper — is widely thought to have helped shape *Lady Chatterley's Lover*, in which a lady of the manor takes her gamekeeper as her lover. Two of the century's frankest novels of desire trace back to one Derbyshire smallholding.

**The revolver champion.** Harold Cheevers was no ordinary chauffeur. Before he entered Walpole's service he had been revolver champion of the British Isles, a Metropolitan policeman with a wife and two children. Walpole placed him at the very top of what he called his "First Fifteen" of friends — and, in the end, left him Brackenburn.

## 48 — The Man Whose Family Burned the Evidence
*Plas Newydd · Anglesey, Wales · 1875–1905*

### BOOK VERSION

His car had a modified exhaust pipe, so that instead of fumes it sprayed perfume in its wake as he drove. This is the detail people remember about Henry Cyril Paget, and it is a good one, because it tells you at once that here was a man with more money than sense and a genius for spectacle — but it is also, in a way, the least of him, and it is worth getting past the perfume to the person, because the person is a good deal more interesting, and a good deal sadder, than the anecdotes.

He was born in 1875, in Paris, into one of the great landed families of Wales and England — the Pagets, Marquesses of Anglesey, whose first marquess had famously lost a leg at Waterloo. There was gossip from the start that Henry's real father was a French actor, and the boy was fostered for a time in Paris by an actor's sister, which may explain the theatricality that ran through everything he later did. When his father died in 1898 Henry, aged twenty-three, became the fifth Marquess, and with the title came some thirty thousand acres and an income of around £110,000 a year — a colossal fortune, worth many millions today.

He spent it, more or less all of it, in about six years, and he spent it on beauty.

The centre of the spending was the theatre. Henry took the chapel at Plas Newydd, the family seat on Anglesey overlooking the Menai Strait, and converted it — a consecrated family chapel — into a 150-seat theatre modelled on the opera house at Dresden, fitted by 1901 with electric stage lighting, and christened the Gaiety. He hired professional actors from London, luring them off their engagements at nearby Llandudno with wages nobody could refuse, and staged lavish productions — pantomime, comedy, and, tellingly, Oscar Wilde's *An Ideal Husband*, put on within a few years of Wilde's imprisonment, which was either oblivious or brave and was quite possibly both. He took the company on tour around Britain and Europe, travelling with its own orchestra and sets.

And in the intervals he danced. His signature turn was the "Butterfly Dance," borrowed from the American dancer Loïe Fuller, in which he moved under coloured light in a vast robe of transparent silk, waved like wings — sinuous, snakelike, sensual. He sewed real diamonds and other precious stones onto his costumes; his jewels alone would later realise some £80,000 at auction. He was known to appear aglow with diamonds from head to foot. A platoon of dressers was needed to get him in and out of the silk. For this he was dubbed, half in wonder and half in mockery, the Dancing Marquess.

He married, in 1898, his cousin Lilian Chetwynd. The marriage was annulled for non-consummation. There is a much-repeated story that he made her cover herself in the family jewels and did nothing else, which is unverifiable and belongs to legend rather than record, so treat it as such; what is documented is that the marriage produced nothing, was declared never to have been a marriage in the physical sense, and freed him to live as he wished.

The money ran out. On 11 June 1904 he was declared bankrupt, with debts of £544,000 — an almost unimaginable sum. The response was the Great Anglesey Sale, a bankruptcy auction of his effects; the sale of his wardrobe and personal effects ran, astonishingly, for some seventeen days, part of a liquidation that comprised many thousands of lots, in which everything went: the costumes, the jewels, the motor cars, the furs, even, it is said, his little dogs in their embroidered silk coats. He retreated to Monte Carlo and died there on 14 March 1905, of tuberculosis, aged just twenty-nine. The newspapers were merciless; one called his a "wasted life."

Then came the burning. The title passed to his cousin, who became the sixth Marquess, and who set about the memory of Henry Cyril with a thoroughness that is the whole reason we must be careful. He destroyed the fifth Marquess's papers — all of them. He turned the Gaiety Theatre back into a chapel. The family, for the better part of a century, kept Henry as an embarrassment; the present Marquess has said his ancestor's photographs ended up stuck in a bathroom while the war heroes got columns and statues.

This matters enormously when we come to the question everyone wants answered, which is whether Henry Cyril Paget was gay. The honest answer is that we cannot know, and the reason we cannot know is that the people best placed to tell us made sure of it. The direct evidence — his letters, his diaries, whatever he confided to paper — was deliberately destroyed by his own family. What survives is circumstantial: the cross-dressing performances, the jewels, the transparent silk, the annulled and unconsummated marriage. On that basis he is claimed today, reasonably and warmly, as a queer and gender-nonconforming icon. But the claim rests on inference, because the family burned the proof, and it is more honest — and more damning of them — to say so than to pretend to a certainty the record cannot bear.

The reclamation has been real and rather joyful. The National Trust, which has held Plas Newydd since 1976, now displays the very photographs the family once hid. Seiriol Davies made a celebrated musical about him, *How to Win Against History* — a title that says everything about a man whose history was taken from him. And in 2024 came a film, *Madfabulous*. The current, eighth Marquess speaks of him now with affection. Henry Cyril Paget has, in the end, won the long game.

So Chapter Six closes where its whole argument has been leading: on a man whose truth survives only as inference, because the family destroyed the rest. The queer past, again and again in these pages, comes down to us as private code, or hostile gossip, or a locked bag, or an unnamed mourner — and here, most starkly of all, as a bonfire. What the sixth Marquess could not burn was the photographs, and the memory, and the perfume still hanging faintly in the air of a story too good to be lost.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The seventeen-day sale.** When bankruptcy came in 1904, the "Great Anglesey Sale" disposed of the Dancing Marquess's belongings; the auction of his wardrobe and effects alone ran for roughly seventeen days: costumes crusted with jewels, furs, a fleet of motor cars, and — the detail that breaks the heart a little — his pet dogs in their little silk coats embroidered with his coat of arms. Everything went to the creditors.

**A leg at Waterloo.** Plas Newydd's grandeur owed much to Henry Cyril's great-great-grandfather, the first Marquess, who lost a leg at Waterloo commanding the cavalry — "By God, sir, I've lost my leg!" runs the possibly apocryphal exchange with Wellington. The bloodied trousers are still shown at the house. The family's military heroes were remembered in bronze; Henry Cyril, for decades, was remembered in a bathroom.

**The detective and the jewels.** When some of the Marquess's jewels were stolen, the press gleefully noted that he had been at a Sherlock Holmes play while the theft took place. Arthur Conan Doyle himself is said to have taken an interest. The jewels were as central to Henry Cyril's legend as the dancing — sewn onto costumes, worn head to foot, and, in the end, auctioned off to pay his debts.

## 49 — The Village Eight Miles Over the Border
*Gretna Green & the road north · Dumfries and Galloway / Cumbria · 1754–present*

### BOOK VERSION

Picture the scene, because it happened thousands of times. A carriage, driven hard through the night up the Great North Road, the horses lathered, a young couple inside who are not yet married and mean to be by morning. Somewhere behind them, another carriage, driven harder — a father, or a guardian, or a lawyer, purple with fury, trying to close the gap before the border. And ahead, over a small river called the Sark, a nondescript Scottish village where a blacksmith is very possibly still up, because business is good. The whole of British erotic history is, in one sense, a long argument between desire and regulation, and there is no purer illustration of it than this: a law passed in London to control who could marry whom, and a village eight miles over the border that turned the evasion of that law into an industry lasting a century.

The law came first. In 1753 the Lord Chancellor, Lord Hardwicke, pushed through an Act "for the Better Preventing of Clandestine Marriage," in force from 25 March 1754. Before it, England was awash with irregular weddings — hurried, unregistered, sometimes drunken affairs, most notoriously in the precincts of the Fleet Prison in London, where disreputable clergymen married anyone for a fee. Hardwicke's Act shut all that down. From 1754, a valid marriage in England and Wales required banns read in church on three Sundays or a licence; it required a ceremony in an Anglican church before witnesses; and, crucially, it required the consent of a parent or guardian for anyone under twenty-one. A clergyman who broke the rules faced fourteen years' transportation. It was, in effect, the state and the Church seizing control of the marriage bed.

There was one enormous hole in it. The Act did not apply in Scotland.

Scots law had never accepted that marriage needed a church, a priest, banns, or anybody's permission. Under Scottish "irregular marriage," a couple were validly and bindingly married if they simply declared themselves so before two witnesses. That was the whole ceremony. No waiting, no residence, no parental veto; a boy could marry at fourteen and a girl at twelve. So the moment the new English requirements took effect, a couple thwarted in England had an obvious remedy: get to Scotland, declare yourselves married in front of two people, and it was done — as binding as any wedding in Canterbury Cathedral, and a good deal quicker.

Which brings us to the blacksmith, and to why he, of all people, became the emblem of runaway love. Gretna Green was the first village of any size across the border on the main coaching road up from London through Carlisle — the last English staging post. In the 1770s a new toll road funnelled the traffic straight through it, and the village never looked back. And at Gretna, at a junction where five roads met, stood the blacksmith's shop. Almost anyone could conduct a Scots irregular marriage, and in practice fishermen, weavers, toll-keepers and innkeepers all did a trade in it; but the blacksmith, standing at the crossroads, was often the first respectable-looking man an arriving couple clapped eyes on, and English couples in particular seem to have wanted some figure of standing to give the thing the feel of a ceremony. So the smith downed his hammer and became a "priest." The nickname stuck: the anvil priests. The tradition grew up that he would seal the marriage by bringing his hammer down on the anvil — joining two people, went the patter, as he joined two pieces of hot metal, in the heat of the moment but bound for good.

The mechanics were brisk and the price was whatever the market would bear. Two questions — are you of age, are you free to marry — a declaration, a bang of the hammer, a certificate, and it was over in minutes. The fee slid according to what the smith reckoned the couple could pay. Ordinary folk might marry for a few shillings or a dram of whisky; a fleeing aristocrat could be relieved of a great deal more. The last of the anvil priests, Richard Rennison, worked from 1926: he generally asked a pound but could take twenty for a good catch, and by the time irregular marriage was abolished he had recorded 5,147 ceremonies.

The pursuing fathers were real, and so, occasionally, was the violence. In a debate in the House of Commons in 1939, a member recalled that Lord Westmorland, marrying at Gretna, escaped the bride's pursuing father only by firing a pistol at one of the horses of the pursuing carriage. Lord Erskine, a future Lord Chief Justice, married there and paid £20; grander clients could pay a hundred guineas. And there is at least one documented tragedy in the chase, a death among a pursuing party, which is a reminder that behind the comedy of the runaway wedding there were real families torn and real stakes — money, land, reputation, and the ancient question of who gets to say no.

The state, predictably, tried to close the hole. Lord Brougham's Act of 1856 — the "cooling-off act", effective from the following year — required that at least one of the parties reside in Scotland for twenty-one days before the marriage. It slowed the trade sharply. It did not kill it, because the good people of Gretna simply started taking in lodgers, and farmers rented out their barns, and the twenty-one days were served. What finally ended the old way was the Marriage (Scotland) Act 1939, which from 1 July 1940 abolished irregular marriage by declaration altogether; from then on only a registrar or minister could tie the knot. The anvil priests hung up their hammers.

And here is the joke the whole chapter has been building towards: none of it stopped Gretna Green. The romance had outgrown the loophole that created it. In 2020 the BBC reported that the Gretna Green area hosted about twelve per cent of Scotland's weddings, while the local operator reports about five thousand couples marrying in the area each year. Many still choose to be "married over the anvil" in a ceremony that has, since 1940, no legal force whatsoever and enormous sentimental power. People who could marry anywhere on earth drive to a small village in Dumfries and Galloway to do it, because of what it has always meant.

Which is the point, and it echoes backwards through this book. British erotic history is a history of rules and the ducking of them. When George IV wanted Maria Fitzherbert he married her in secret and the Royal Marriages Act made the marriage void — regulation defeating desire. When Welsh couples wanted a wishing-well blessing there was Ffynnon Fair, the "Welsh Gretna Green" that never quite became one. Gretna is the same story with the ending reversed: here, for once, desire got round the regulation, wholesale, for a hundred years, and built an industry on it, and is building on it still. The law was made in London. The evasion was perfected on the far side of the border, and the sound of it was a hammer coming down on an anvil.

Carlisle was the last English staging post on the long road north — the place the pursuing father changed horses too. ### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**Five thousand weddings, one priest.** Richard Rennison, the last of the Gretna "anvil priests," worked the Old Blacksmith's Shop from 1926. He generally charged a pound, but could command twenty for a wealthy couple. By the time irregular marriage was abolished in 1940, he had recorded 5,147 ceremonies — a personal tally that gives some sense of the sheer scale of the runaway trade at its height.

**The cooling-off dodge.** When Lord Brougham's 1856 Act demanded a 21-day residence before marriage, Gretna simply adapted. Locals took in the runaways as lodgers; farmers let their barns as makeshift lodgings; the three weeks were served on the spot. The law meant to cool ardour merely created a new local trade in bed and board. There is a nice irony in the record that Brougham himself is said to have eloped to Scotland in his own youth.

**Austen's shorthand for ruin.** By Jane Austen's day "Gretna Green" was a byword the whole reading public understood. In *Pride and Prejudice*, Lydia Bennet's flight is assumed to be headed there with Wickham — and the horror of the family is precisely that it turns out not to be, because a man who won't even take you to Gretna has no intention of marrying you at all. The village had become cultural code for both romance and disgrace.

## 50 — Nineteen
*Murray's Cabaret Club, Cliveden & Wimpole Mews · London and Buckinghamshire · 1959–1963*

### BOOK VERSION

She was nineteen years old, working-class, and without a shred of power, and the men were middle-aged and running the country. Everything else about the Profumo affair — the spies, the swimming pool, the lies in the Commons, the government that fell — flows from that single imbalance, and any honest telling has to begin there, because for sixty years the telling did not. Christine Keeler was cast as the scarlet woman, the good-time girl, the tart who brought down a minister. She was, in fact, a young woman used and discarded by a series of powerful men, and this chapter of the book opens on her not because she was scandalous but because she was wronged.

Begin at Murray's. Murray's Cabaret Club stood at 16–18 Beak Street in Soho, a members' club that had been running since 1913, and it worked on a nicely hypocritical formula. The showgirls appeared topless — but under the rules of the Lord Chamberlain, who censored the London stage, a nude on stage was not permitted to move. If she stood still, in a "tableau," she was Art; if she so much as shifted her weight, she was Obscenity, and the club could be closed. It was the identical rule that governed the more famous Windmill Theatre round the corner, summed up there in a phrase that could stand as an epigraph for the whole of British attitudes to sex: "If it moves, it's rude." So the girls of Murray's walked bare-breasted to their positions and then froze, and afterwards, dressed, they sat with the wealthy clientele as hostesses for a fee of five pounds a time. Keeler reckoned she made about thirty pounds a week that way, in a club where, as she drily put it, the customers could look but could not touch.

It was at Murray's, in 1959, that Christine Keeler — then seventeen — met Stephen Ward. Ward was a fashionable osteopath with a gift for friendship and a client list that ran from film stars to royalty; he sketched portraits on the side, including one of Prince Philip. He took Keeler up, in a relationship both always described as like brother and sister rather than lovers, and she moved into his flat. It is worth being clear-eyed about what this was. Keeler's childhood had been one of real deprivation and abuse; she had left school at fifteen, and at seventeen had already borne and lost a baby. Ward, twenty-six years older and superbly connected, took a vulnerable teenager and drew her into a world far above her own — and if that reads today less like kindness than like a man building his own social capital out of a girl who had none, that is the more honest reading. At Murray's, Keeler also met a younger dancer, Mandy Rice-Davies, who became her friend and shared the ride down.

Then Cliveden. Cliveden was the vast Italianate house of the Astor family, above the Thames in Buckinghamshire, and Stephen Ward rented a cottage on its grounds. On the weekend of 8–9 July 1961, Lord Astor was hosting a grand dinner up at the house; down at the walled swimming pool, Ward had a small party of his own, Keeler — now nineteen — among them. She was in the pool. John Profumo, Secretary of State for War, aged forty-six, married to the actress Valerie Hobson, was among the dinner guests who wandered down from the house when they heard the laughter. What happened next has been embroidered for sixty years into a scene of deliberate seduction — the snatched swimming costume, the naked girl rising from the water. Strip the embroidery away and the documented core is simpler and stranger: a middle-aged cabinet minister came upon a nineteen-year-old showgirl at a pool on a summer night, and found her, in the plain sense of the word, entrancing. He asked Ward for her telephone number. A brief affair followed, over within a few months by the autumn of 1961.

There was a second man at Cliveden that weekend, and he is the reason the affair became history rather than gossip. Yevgeny Ivanov was a Soviet naval attaché — an intelligence officer — and a friend of Ward's, and Keeler was, around this same period, also involved with him. A cabinet minister and a Soviet spy sharing the bed of the same young woman, at the frozen height of the Cold War, months before the Cuban Missile Crisis: that is the fact that would later turn a private indiscretion into a matter of state. It should be said at once, because it is the single most important corrective in the whole story, that when Lord Denning came to investigate — his report published as a Command Paper in September 1963 — he concluded there had been no leak of security, no breach at all arising from the Ivanov connection. The danger to the nation, so luridly imagined, was never shown to have existed.

What actually cracked the thing open was not espionage but a gun. By late 1962 Keeler had become entangled with two other men, and on 14 December 1962 one of them, Johnny Edgecombe, turned up at Ward's flat at 17 Wimpole Mews in Marylebone, where Keeler had been staying, and — refused entry — fired shots at the door. The shooting put Keeler in front of the press and the courts, and once journalists began pulling at the thread, the whole tapestry came away: the minister, the showgirl, the Russian, the osteopath.

The political end came fast. On 22 March 1963 Profumo stood up in the House of Commons and told it flatly that there had been no impropriety in his acquaintance with Miss Keeler. It was a lie, told to Parliament, and it was the lie, far more than the sex, that destroyed him. On 5 June 1963 he confessed and resigned. In October Harold Macmillan, the Prime Minister, resigned too, his health and his authority broken; the Conservatives lost the general election the following year. A government had, in the end, been felled — not by a Soviet plot, but by a minister who could not tell the truth about his own bed.

And then they needed someone to punish, and they chose the man least able to fight back. Stephen Ward was arrested and put on trial for living on the immoral earnings of Keeler and Rice-Davies — for pimping, in effect. It is the settled view of most modern historians that this was a scapegoating: a prosecution got up, on thin and pressured evidence, to give the establishment a villain and to punish the man who had introduced a minister to a showgirl. His grand friends, who had used his flat and his hospitality for years, abandoned him almost to a man. Midway through the trial, on the night of 31 July 1963, facing a hostile judge and certain conviction, Ward took an overdose of sleeping pills. He was found guilty as he lay in a coma, and died on 3 August without regaining consciousness. Barristers and writers have argued ever since — Ludovic Kennedy at the time, Geoffrey Robertson QC decades later, who titled his book *Stephen Ward Was Innocent, OK* — that the conviction was unsafe and the trial rigged; the papers that might settle it remain, extraordinarily, sealed.

That leaves Christine Keeler, which is where a humane account has to end, because she is the one the story used worst. In December 1963 she was sent to Holloway prison for nine months, not for anything to do with Profumo, but for perjury in a separate case — a case, note, in which she had herself been the victim of an assault. She was twenty-one, without money or protectors, and she was made to serve time while the powerful men around the affair walked away: Profumo rehabilitated himself through decades of charity work and died honoured in 2006; the Astors kept their house. Keeler was hounded by the press for the rest of her life, cast forever as the woman in Lewis Morley's famous photograph astride a chair, and died in 2017, aged seventy-five, still trying to outrun a notoriety she had never deserved. Since her death her son has campaigned to clear her name, and reassessments — a Criminal Cases Review Commission referral, a petition for a posthumous pardon — have gathered pace, arguing what should have been obvious all along: that she was a young woman wronged, not a villain.

The state that scapegoated him is still, sixty years on, keeping its own counsel.
