## 56 — The Bishop of Winchester's Geese
*Bankside stews & Cross Bones Graveyard · Southwark, London · c.1161–1853 (graveyard closed), reclaimed from 1996*

### BOOK VERSION
The women were called Winchester Geese, after the bishop who licensed their brothels and drew rent from the Bankside stews. The founding document of that arrangement, drawn up in the fifteenth century, tells you almost everything about the English attitude to sex, money and God, and it does so with a straight face. It is titled, with the sonorous pomposity of the very holy, the "Ordinances Touching the Government of the Stewholders in Southwark under the Direction of the Bishop of Winchester". It sets out the rules for running brothels. It was issued in the name of the Bishop of Winchester. And its author claimed, dishonestly, that it dated back to 1161 and to a parliament held under Henry II — a parliament that could not have existed, there being no such thing at the time. The Wellcome Collection, which has examined the thing carefully, dates the surviving text to the fifteenth century and notes drily that the bishop was gilding his own paperwork. Even the founding charter of England's licensed sex trade, in other words, is a forgery of sorts. It is a fitting beginning.

The setting was Bankside, a strip of the south bank of the Thames directly opposite the City of London, and its whole character depended on a legal accident. This was the Liberty of the Clink: a manor that lay technically in Surrey but answered to neither the sheriff of Surrey nor the aldermen of the City. It answered instead to the Bishop of Winchester, whose London palace stood on what is now Clink Street. Because the City's writ stopped at the river, the things the City forbade within its walls — the playhouses, the bear-baiting rings, the brothels — simply crossed the water and set up shop where the bishop, rather than the mayor, took the rents. It is the founding principle of this entire chapter: London's vices were not banished but relocated, licensed and taxed, usually by the very authorities that condemned them.

The brothels were called "stews", a word with two possible parentages. It may descend from the fish stew-ponds that bred carp on the bishop's land; it may equally come from *estuves*, the Norman-French for the heated bath-houses where, then as now, a great deal more than bathing tended to go on. Either way the brothel-keeper became a "stewholder" even where his premises offered no bath at all. The nickname given to the women proved just as durable. To be "bitten by a Winchester goose" was to catch a venereal disease; the phrase "goose bumps" is sometimes traced, plausibly if not certainly, to the same unhappy source.

What the ordinances actually contain is the surprising part. A good number of the rules read less like the fantasies of a lecherous bishop than like early, imperfect labour law. A stewholder who detained a woman against her will faced a fine. A woman who "lives by her body" was not to be forced to board on the premises but must be free to lodge where she chose and to come and go as she pleased — the clause about coming and going freely is the one modern writers quote most, and rightly, because it is genuinely striking. Married women were barred, and so were nuns. Women with a "burning disease" were not to work. The house was forbidden to hold any man prisoner over a debt. And crucially, the stews were not supposed to sell food and drink, precisely so that a woman could not be trapped in a spiral of debt to her own employer for her supper and her ale — though the surviving court records show stewholders flogging illicit ale anyway, and repeat offenders treating the fines as an operating cost. There were fines, too, for a woman who dragged a man in off the street by his clothing.

It would be pretty to imagine the medieval Church running a model of ethical sex work. It would also be wrong. The regulations protected the women partly because protected women were a more reliable revenue stream, and the bishop's income was made up substantially of the rents and the fines. The point is not that the bishops were kind. The point is that they were businessmen in mitres, and that the trade they profited from was, by the later fifteenth century, a serious enterprise — eighteen licensed houses along the bank, according to records of the period, though the number rose and fell with plague, war and royal temper.

The whole arrangement lasted, astonishingly, for centuries, and it was killed not by a sudden attack of conscience but by disease. There had been earlier attempts — Henry VII closed the stews around 1505 after an outbreak of the pox, and they mostly reopened within the year. It was Henry VIII, in 1546, who finally put them down. John Stow, writing his great *Survey of London* in 1598, records that the ban was "proclaimed by the sound of trumpet" — one imagines the trumpeter doing his best to look solemn — and the driving fear was syphilis rather than sin. It did not, of course, end the trade. It merely scattered it. The geese packed up and dispersed across London, to Cock Lane and Petticoat Lane and streets whose names are too frank to have survived on the maps.

A short walk from the river, on Redcross Way, is the coda to the story, and it is one that has to be told carefully, because affection has a way of hardening folklore into fact. In his 1598 *Survey*, Stow mentions "a plot of ground called the Single Woman's churchyard", where the Bankside prostitutes, denied Christian burial for their trade, were laid in unhallowed ground. Local tradition has long identified this churchyard with Cross Bones, the disused burial ground on Redcross Way. It is a powerful identification and it may well be true. It is not, however, proven; historians who have looked at it, including those quoted by the Smithsonian, note that the link rests essentially on Stow's line and on tradition, not on documentary certainty. That caveat matters, because it protects the dead from being conscripted into a better story than the one they actually lived.

What the ground itself says is grimmer and harder to romanticise. When the Jubilee Line was extended in the 1990s, an electricity substation was to be built on the site, and Museum of London archaeologists were given a few weeks to excavate a corner of it. In the 1999 report by Brickley, Miles and Stainer they removed 148 skeletons, all dated to the graveyard's final decades, roughly 1800 to 1853, and estimated that this represented "less than 1% of the total number of burials" — pointing to something like 15,000 in all. The demographics are the part that stops you: 104 of the 148, over 70 per cent, were children, at least 98 of them aged six or under. The bones carried the marks of rickets, scurvy and syphilis; one skeleton, later shown on television, was a girl of sixteen to nineteen, four feet seven inches tall. By 1853 the ground was closed as "completely overcharged with dead". This was, by then, not the geese's graveyard at all but a paupers' pit for the poorest parishioners of St Saviour's. Wherever the comedy of this chapter belongs — and there is comedy, a great deal of it, in a bishop taxing brothels — it does not belong here.

The site was rescued, in the end, by the living. From 1996 the writer John Constable, who also wrote as "John Crow", took up its cause and wove its history into *The Southwark Mysteries*; a vigil has been held at the gates on the 23rd of every month since 2004, and in 2021 the Bankside Open Spaces Trust secured a thirty-year lease. Today the iron gates on Redcross Way are thick with ribbons, tokens and hand-written names, tied there for the outcast dead. Bankside was always London's licensed elsewhere — the same stretch that later gave us the Tabard from which Chaucer's pilgrims set out, and that sat a river's width from the molly houses of the eighteenth century. It made the Church rich for four hundred years. Its women got a nickname, a disease named after a bird, and a corner of unconsecrated ground that the city forgot and then, slowly, remembered.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)
From at least the later Middle Ages, the brothels of Bankside — the "stews" — were licensed, regulated and taxed by the Bishop of Winchester, whose Liberty of the Clink lay outside the City of London's jurisdiction. Things forbidden across the river were permitted here, provided the bishop took his cut.

The rules survive in the "Ordinances Touching the Government of the Stewholders in Southwark", a document that claims to date from 1161 and the reign of Henry II but which the Wellcome Collection dates to the fifteenth century — the bishop backdating his own authority. Many of its clauses read like rough labour protections: a woman "who lives by her body" could come and go as she pleased and lodge where she chose; keepers could not detain her; married women and nuns were barred; and the houses were not to sell food and drink, so a woman could not be run into debt to her employer.

The women were nicknamed "Winchester Geese". To be "bitten by a Winchester goose" meant to catch a venereal disease. Henry VIII suppressed the licensed stews in 1546, "by the sound of trumpet" in John Stow's phrase, driven by fear of syphilis. The trade simply scattered.

Nearby on Redcross Way is Cross Bones, long identified by tradition — though not by firm proof — with the "Single Woman's churchyard" Stow mentions in 1598. Museum of London archaeologists excavated 148 skeletons dating 1800–1853; 104 of them (over 70 per cent) were children, at least 98 aged six or under, and this was estimated to be under 1% of perhaps 15,000 burials before the ground closed in 1853, "completely overcharged with dead". By then it was a paupers' cemetery, not a prostitutes' one.

Rediscovered during 1990s Jubilee Line works, Cross Bones was championed from 1996 by the writer John Constable. A vigil has been held at the gates on the 23rd of each month since 2004, and Bankside Open Spaces Trust secured a 30-year lease in 2021. The gates are hung with ribbons for "the outcast dead". The rose window of Winchester Palace still survives on Clink Street — the last fragment of the power that grew rich on the trade.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**The rose window on Clink Street** — The most legible relic of the bishops' power is the great fourteenth-century rose window in the west gable of Winchester Palace's Great Hall, added under Bishop William of Wykeham around 1370. The palace was gutted by fire in 1814, which ironically exposed the medieval hall behind later warehousing. English Heritage cares for the ruin today, a stone's throw from the Clink Prison Museum.

**The Clink** — The bishop's own gaol, in the grounds of his palace, gave its name to every prison in the English language. Originally for those who brawled or broke the peace "on the banke, or in the Brothell houses", it later held debtors and, in the Tudor period, religious prisoners, before being burned down in the Gordon Riots of 1780 and never rebuilt.

**The white-walled houses** — In 1479 Edward IV, no puritan, ordered that the licensed Bankside stews identify themselves by painting their river-facing walls entirely white, each with its own painted sign like a pub. The Cardinal's Cap was among the best known — and among those trading again within a few years of Henry VIII's supposedly final suppression.

## 57 — Harris's List
*Covent Garden bagnios · London · 1757–1795*

### BOOK VERSION
It was a small thing, this book — a pocket-sized annual, cheaply printed, sold for two shillings and sixpence, the sort of object a gentleman could slip inside his coat and produce in a coffee house with a raised eyebrow. It was called *Harris's List of Covent Garden Ladies*, and for the best part of forty years, from 1757 to 1795, it was one of the reliable bestsellers of Georgian London. The German visitor Johann von Archenholz, in his *A Picture of England* of 1791, recorded that the lists were printed by a tavern-keeper in Drury Lane and that "eight thousand copies are sold annually" — a genuinely startling figure in an age before mass literacy. Each edition described between roughly 120 and 190 women working in and around Covent Garden: where to find them, what they looked like, what they charged, and what, in the coy and heavily italicised euphemism of the day, they specialised in.

The "Harris" of the title was real, more or less. Jack Harris was the working name of a man born John Harrison, head waiter at the Shakespear's Head Tavern on the Covent Garden piazza, who had styled himself, with magnificent cheek, "Pimp-General of All England". He kept a handwritten ledger of some four hundred women, and it was probably his notoriety that sold the printed version, since his name appeared on the title page — written, as it happens, in someone else's hand. The someone else was Samuel Derrick, a debt-ridden Irish poet and Grub Street hack who, according to the historian Hallie Rubenhold, whose *The Covent Garden Ladies* (2005) is the definitive modern account, wrote the thing in an act of near-desperation to keep himself out of debtors' prison. Derrick knew Samuel Johnson and James Boswell; he ended his days, improbably, as Master of Ceremonies at Bath. The little book of harlots was a poet's survival strategy.

The trade it served had its own architecture. The bagnios of Covent Garden — the word comes from the Italian *bagno*, a bath — had begun life as Turkish-style bath-houses and slid, by an entirely predictable gravity, into brothels and houses of assignation where a room, a meal and company could be had discreetly for the night. Around them clustered the whole apparatus of the Georgian sex market: the taverns, the coffee houses with a woman's arm holding a coffee-pot painted on the sign as a knowing signal, the theatres of Drury Lane and Covent Garden disgorging their audiences into the piazza.

And here, because there is no more sensible place for it in the whole book, we must talk about contraception, or rather about "armour". The Georgian condom was a real and thriving article of commerce, and it was made, unappetisingly, from the caecum — the blind gut — of a sheep or lamb, soaked, turned, scraped, treated and dried, then tied at the open end with a length of coloured ribbon to hold it on. It was sold, and it was reused. The two great names in the London trade were both women and both rivals: Mrs Phillips, who traded at the Sign of the Green Canister on Half Moon Street off the Strand, and Mrs Perkins. A surviving trade card for Mrs Phillips, held at Duke University, boasts of thirty-five years in the business, of exports to France, Spain, Portugal and Italy, and offers, with a poet's touch, the couplet: "To guard yourself from shame or fear, / Votaries to Venus, hasten here." The most famous testers of the product left their own record. Casanova is described inflating them like balloons to check for holes before use — an early and sensible piece of quality control. And James Boswell, whose *London Journal* of 1762–63 is the frankest sexual diary of the century, recorded picking up "a strong, jolly young damsel" and conducting her to Westminster Bridge, where "in armour complete did I engage her upon this noble edifice." Boswell being Boswell, he also recorded, repeatedly, the times he went without, and the poxes that followed.

What did the *List* actually say? At its best the prose is a peculiar hybrid of estate-agent's puffery and mock-classical verse, all "Cyprian nymphs" and "dairy hills of delight". Prices ran, roughly, from five shillings to five pounds, and occasionally higher for the celebrated. Most entries flatter. A few are startlingly, needlessly cruel: the 1773 notice for one Miss Berry pronounces her "almost rotten, and her breath cadaverous". Others carry, buried in the smut, a flash of real biography and even sympathy — the woman "led astray" by a designing man, the country girl come to London and undone. It is worth holding both of those facts in the head at once. The *List* was, as Rubenhold has argued, essentially a work of erotica, written by men, for the private pleasure of men, and its women are specimens arranged for the male gaze. And yet buried in its pages are real people, with addresses and ages and, occasionally, the outline of a life.

That is the note on which to end, because it is the honest one. These were not characters. Kitty Fisher, who appears in the *List*, was a genuine celebrity courtesan, painted by Reynolds. Charlotte Hayes, who features briefly in the 1761 edition, went on to run one of the grandest brothels in London and to amass, with her gambler partner, a small fortune. Most of the others left no such trail, and it is the achievement of Rubenhold's book to have gone into the parish records and the Grub Street journalism and reassembled a few of them into something like biography. The *List* itself was killed in 1795, when public opinion had turned and its publishers were fined and imprisoned. But for four decades it did a brisk trade, entirely in the open, licensed by nothing more than the Georgian state's happy willingness to look the other way — the same tolerance that lit the Dark Walks at Vauxhall and let the pleasure-seekers of London find each other in the shrubbery. Nobody prosecuted a bestseller about prostitutes. That would come later, and it would come, as we shall see, only when the trade got cheap.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)
*Harris's List of Covent Garden Ladies* was an annual pocketbook directory of London prostitutes, published from 1757 to 1795 and selling, by Johann von Archenholz's 1791 estimate, "eight thousand copies" a year at two shillings and sixpence. Each edition described between roughly 120 and 190 women working around Covent Garden: their appearance, their prices — from about five shillings to five pounds — and their "specialities", in heavily coded, heavily italicised prose.

It was named for Jack Harris, real name John Harrison, head waiter at the Shakespear's Head Tavern, who called himself "Pimp-General of All England" and kept a ledger of some 400 women. But the prose was largely the work of Samuel Derrick, a debt-ridden Irish poet who, the historian Hallie Rubenhold argues, wrote it to escape debtors' prison. Derrick later became Master of Ceremonies at Bath.

The bagnios of Covent Garden — from the Italian for "bath" — had begun as bath-houses and become brothels and houses of assignation. Around them ran the whole Georgian sex trade, including a brisk market in contraception. Condoms were made from sheep or lamb gut, soaked and tied at the base with a ribbon, and reused. The two leading London suppliers were both women: Mrs Phillips of the Green Canister, Half Moon Street, and her rival Mrs Perkins. Casanova reportedly inflated his to test for holes; James Boswell's *London Journal* records engaging a woman "in armour complete" on Westminster Bridge.

The *List* itself ranged from flattery to cruelty — the 1773 entry for a Miss Berry called her "almost rotten, and her breath cadaverous" — but occasionally showed real sympathy for women "led astray". These were real people, described for the male gaze: Kitty Fisher and Charlotte Hayes both appear in its pages. Rubenhold's *The Covent Garden Ladies* (2005) reconstructs several of their actual lives.

Public opinion turned in the 1790s; the publishers were fined and imprisoned, and the 1795 edition was the last. For nearly forty years, though, London tolerated an open bestseller about its prostitutes — the same easy tolerance it extended to the pleasure gardens.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**The Green Canister** — Mrs Phillips's condom warehouse off the Strand was a genuine business with genuine advertising. Her trade card claimed thirty-five years' experience and a customer base across Catholic Europe, and defied anyone "to equal her goods in England or any other country whatsoever." Her rival Mrs Perkins is said to have taken over the market on Phillips's retirement — two women running the earliest documented commercial contraceptive trade in Europe.

**Boswell's armour** — James Boswell's *London Journal* is the frankest sexual record of the age. He tried a condom "for the first time" in St James's Park in 1763 and found it "but a dull satisfaction"; he then abandoned the practice with his mistress "Louisa" and caught gonorrhoea for his trouble. His candour, recovered from a trunk at Malahide Castle in the 1920s, tells us more about Georgian sex than any brothel guide.

**Charlotte Hayes and the nunnery** — One name in the 1761 *List*, Charlotte Hayes, rose to run a luxurious "nunnery" at King's Place, St James's, where the women were "nuns" and she the "abbess". The model — a vetted, elegant, expensive house — had been imported from Paris by Mrs Goadby of Marlborough Street, who reportedly employed a surgeon to inspect her staff. Prostitution's high end was a business run largely by women.

## 58 — The White House, Soho Square
*Manor House, 21 Soho Square · London · c.1778–1801*

### BOOK VERSION
A cupboard opens and a human skeleton lurches into the room on hidden machinery, grinning at the gentleman who has just been shown into what looked like an ordinary chamber. This was the "Skeleton Room", and it was, we are told, one of the attractions of the White House at 21 Soho Square, the most theatrical brothel in Georgian London. It is a wonderful image. Before we get too attached to it, though, we should be honest about where it comes from, because the White House is a case study in how much of sexual history is reliable record and how much is a good story told long after the doors have closed.

Start with what is solid. The house at 21 Soho Square — the building now called Manor House — is documented in the *Survey of London*, published by the London County Council and available through British History Online, which is about as sober an authority as this subject possesses. It records that the house was kept as a hotel by one Thomas Hooper, that it "became notorious in the annals of Fashion as the infamous White House", and that "it is likely that the house was used as a brothel". It describes the reception rooms as "garishly decorated": three of them, known from their fittings as the Gold, Silver and Bronze rooms, had walls "inlaid with mirrored panels". There was a Painted Chamber, a Grotto, a Coal Hole, and the Skeleton Room, "where, for the delectation of the patrons, a skeleton could be made to step out of a closet with the aid of machinery". That the house had themed rooms and a mechanical skeleton, in other words, is attested by a serious topographical survey. So far, so gloriously peculiar.

Now the softer ground. The fuller, more lurid version — the one where whole rooms transform, where innocent-looking furniture springs traps, where the place becomes something close to a mechanised pleasure-dungeon — rests mainly on Henry Mayhew, writing in 1861. Mayhew, the great chronicler of *London Labour and the London Poor*, called the White House "a notorious place of ill-fame" and wrote that some of its apartments "were furnished in a style of costly luxury; while others were fitted up with springs, traps, and other contrivances, so as to present no appearance other than that of an ordinary room, until the machinery was set in motion." It is vivid stuff. But Mayhew was writing sixty years after the house had closed, from reputation and hearsay rather than from any inventory, and the detail has grown in the retelling ever since, until modern accounts cheerfully describe a "transforming sex dungeon". The mirrored rooms and the skeleton have a documentary spine. The rest is memory, and memory of this kind tends to improve with age.

What is not in doubt is the milieu. Soho and neighbouring St James's were, by the later eighteenth century, the luxury end of the London sex trade, and the White House belonged to a recognisable species of establishment: the theatrical, expensive, discreet house of assignation for men who could pay. The genre had a founding mother. Mrs Goadby — Jane, or Margaret, Goadby — is credited with importing the French model to London, fitting up a house in Marlborough Street "in an elegant stile", engaging "some of the first-rate filles de joye", and, tellingly, employing a surgeon to inspect the women and turn away any of doubtful health. It was, for its clientele, a considerable selling point. Her success bred imitators, the most famous of whom was Charlotte Hayes, who established herself at King's Place, off Pall Mall, and ran what the trade wittily called a "nunnery": the women were her "nuns", she the "abbess", and the price of admission cheerfully excluded anyone below the very rich. King's Place became a small colony of these convents-that-were-nothing-of-the-sort, and the joke — pious language for profane trade — is exactly the joke the Bishop of Winchester had been making, unintentionally, five hundred years before.

The White House sits in this company as the show-off, the one with the special effects. It kept its clientele deliberately mysterious, and the tradition — recorded a little breathlessly in the Victorian *Old and New London* — has it frequented by the Prince of Wales, the old Duke of Queensberry and the Marquis of Hertford, which may be true and is certainly the sort of thing that gets said about a fashionable brothel whether it is true or not. It seems to have operated from around 1778 to 1801 under Hooper, though some accounts stretch its notoriety to 1836.

And then, as these things do, it stopped. The building at 21 Soho Square was altered or rebuilt in 1838 for Edmund Crosse and Thomas Blackwell, and there is a certain magnificent flatness to what came next. The house that had allegedly boasted a mechanical skeleton and a Grotto and rooms lined with mirrors became the headquarters of Crosse & Blackwell, the pickle and preserve firm — a place of chutney, piccalilli and potted meat. It is Grade II listed now, an office block, its Georgian scandals invisible behind a Victorian façade. The Hellfire crowd staged their theatrical vice out at West Wycombe in caves and mock-abbeys; the White House staged it in a townhouse on a Soho square, and did it, if the *Survey of London* is to be believed, with a working skeleton. Of all the ways for a brothel to end, becoming the home of England's favourite pickle is not the one anyone would have scripted.

Her most famous imitator, Charlotte Hayes, ran a "nunnery" at King's Place, St James's, where the women were "nuns" and she the "abbess", and the prices kept out all but the very rich.

By tradition the White House drew royal and aristocratic clients, though this is reputation, not record. The building was rebuilt in 1838 for Edmund Crosse and Thomas Blackwell, and the notorious brothel became — with magnificent bathos — the headquarters of Crosse & Blackwell, the pickle and preserve company. It is Grade II listed today, an office block, its Georgian scandals hidden behind a Victorian front.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**Mrs Goadby's French innovation** — In 1773 the *Covent Garden Magazine* announced that "Mrs Goadby, that celebrated Abbess, having fitted up an elegant Nunnery in Marlborough Street, is now laying in a stock of Virgins for the ensuing season." Goadby modelled her house on the vetted, medically inspected bordellos of Paris, complete with liveried carriages that doubled as advertising. **From skeleton to piccalilli** — When the house was rebuilt in 1838 it became the head office of Crosse & Blackwell, whose intervention saw the weathered statue of Charles II removed from the centre of Soho Square during their tenure. The firm's pickles and preserves were shipped worldwide from the address that had once, allegedly, alarmed its patrons with a mechanical skeleton.

## 59 — Mrs Berkley's Machine
*Site of 28 Charlotte Street, now 84–94 Hallam Street (Weymouth House) · Marylebone, London · c.1828–1836*

### BOOK VERSION
The most precise fact attached to Theresa Berkley is an address, and even that sends visitors to the wrong street. Her supposed establishment stood at 28 Charlotte Street in Marylebone, not the restaurant-lined Charlotte Street in Fitzrovia. The road was renamed Hallam Street in 1905, and Weymouth House now covers the site. There is no plaque. More awkwardly, there is no secure independent record yet identified in the sources used for this book that places Berkley there at all.

The standard story calls her the queen of London's flagellation trade and gives September 1836 as the month of her death. In the spring of 1828, it says, she invented the Berkley Horse: an adjustable padded frame, something between a ladder and a piece of gymnasium equipment, to which a client could be strapped face-down and birched from above while being pleasured from below. The same account gives her an armoury of implements, a rich and titled clientele, and a fortune made from the flogged backsides of the English upper classes. It is a magnificent story, in the sense that it has grown grander with handling.

Nearly all its load-bearing details come from Henry Spencer Ashbee, the Victorian collector who catalogued his enormous library of erotica under the name Pisanus Fraxi. Ashbee quotes an unnamed source describing birches kept wet, needle-pointed cats, leather straps, holly, furze and green nettles. He also supplies the memoirs, the executor called Dr Vance, the rejected inheritance and the claim that the original Horse was presented to the Society of Arts. No independent court case, will, rate-book entry, parish record or accession record has yet been produced to support that biography. This does not prove Berkley was invented. It means that the woman described with such confidence by later reference works is resting largely on a pornographer's bibliography and the repetitions that followed it.

The wider trade is on firmer ground. Flagellation houses, usually called houses of discipline, existed in Georgian and Victorian London. Continental writers called the appetite *le vice anglais*, the English vice, and historians have linked it to a society in which schoolboys and sailors encountered the birch as a routine instrument of authority. Rictor Norton reads the dominant fantasy as passive: grown men returning, in imagination, to the position of the punished schoolboy. The publishing history is equally real. The English edition of *A Treatise of the Use of Flogging in Venereal Affairs* appeared in 1718, and flagellation erotica remained a profitable trade long afterwards. There was a golden age of the governesses. It is this particular governess who will not stand still for the historian.

The Horse may have existed. Berkley may have existed. What does not yet exist is an independent biography separating her from the men who found her commercially useful as a fantasy. That is the distinction the section needs to preserve. Stand outside Weymouth House and the standard reference works can still give you a death date, a profession and an address accurate to the yard. The documentary woman remains much harder to find.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)
The standard account remembers Theresa Berkley as the "queen" of London's flagellation trade — a professional "governess" credited with inventing, around 1828, the "Berkley Horse", an adjustable padded frame to which willing gentlemen were strapped to be birched. It places her establishment at 28 Charlotte Street — not the famous Fitzrovia street of that name, but a different Charlotte Street in Marylebone, renamed Hallam Street in 1905 after the historian Henry Hallam. The supposed site is now Weymouth House, 84–94 Hallam Street. Nothing survives; there is no plaque.

The crucial thing to know is how little is independently documented. Almost the entire Berkley story comes from a single source: Henry Spencer Ashbee, the Victorian pornography collector who wrote as "Pisanus Fraxi" and compiled the first English bibliography of erotica (1877–85). It is Ashbee who describes her armoury of birches, needle-tipped cats-o'-nine-tails and green nettles, and Ashbee who reports that the original Horse was left to the Society of Arts by a "Doctor Vance". No independent court case, will, rate-book entry, parish record or accession record has yet been produced to support that biography. This does not prove Berkley was invented; it means the confident later account rests largely on a pornographer's bibliography and the repetitions that followed it.

What is solidly attested is the wider trade. The English enthusiasm for flagellation was notorious enough to earn a continental nickname, *le vice anglais* — rooted, historians suggest, in the routine use of the birch in English schools and the Navy. Flagellation brothels, or "houses of discipline", genuinely operated in Georgian and Victorian London, and flagellation erotica was a real publishing industry. The scholar Rictor Norton, working from documentary records, describes the English vice as essentially a fantasy of grown men about being caned as schoolboys, the desire overwhelmingly passive.

The Horse may have existed, and Berkley may have existed. What has not yet emerged is an independent biography separating her from the men who found her commercially useful as a fantasy.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**The two Charlotte Streets** — The confusion is understandable: Georgian London had several Charlotte Streets, all named for Queen Charlotte. The Fitzrovia one still bears the name; the Marylebone one, where Berkley supposedly worked, became Hallam Street in 1905, and a third became part of Bloomsbury Street. Berkley's alleged address, 28 Charlotte Street, lay under what is now Weymouth House — meaning the "sex-dungeon of Fitzrovia" of popular retelling was never in Fitzrovia at all.

**Pisanus Fraxi's library** — Henry Spencer Ashbee bequeathed his vast collection to the British Museum on condition it accept the pornography along with his valuable Cervantes library. The trustees, wanting the Cervantes, took the lot — then quietly destroyed six boxes of "offensive matter". The remainder formed the core of the British Library's "Private Case", kept from readers for decades. Much of what we think we know about Georgian sex passed through this one obsessive collector's hands.

**The English vice** — Flagellation was so associated with England that Europeans called it *le vice anglais*. Its likely origin is grimly ordinary: the birch was the routine instrument of punishment in English public schools and the Royal Navy, applied to the bare buttocks, so that discipline, shame and the body were fused early. The erotic afterlife of the schoolroom rod ran right through Victorian pornography.

## 60 — Holywell Street
*Holywell Street (site now under Aldwych/Australia House) · London · c.1820s–1903*

### BOOK VERSION
Gladstone still stands at the eastern end of the Strand, looking towards a street respectable London removed from the map. Holywell Street ran behind him: narrow, overhung and packed with booksellers whose windows made Victorian newspapers lose their composure. One called it "the most vile street in the civilised world". The exact wording and attribution have shifted in the retelling. The disgust has not. Long before Soho acquired the reputation, this short, crooked lane was London's principal market for dirty books and obscene prints.

Holywell Street was a narrow, crooked lane running parallel to the Strand, its timber-framed houses leaning drunkenly over the roadway and blocking out the light, as though a piece of medieval London had been left behind by mistake. It had been known as "Rag Street", for the second-hand clothes dealers, and as "Booksellers' Row", for the print shops, and it was the second trade that made its name. By 1834, one investigation counted fifty-seven shops in this one short street selling indecent material, their windows crammed with suggestive title pages and obscene engravings designed to stop a passer-by in his tracks.

The street's transformation was mostly economics. Holywell Street first attracted radical pamphleteers, freethinkers selling dangerous material on politics, religion and sex after the French Revolution. When prosecutions made sedition expensive, some discovered that erotica was safer and considerably more profitable. The presses stayed where they were. The product changed.

Its presiding genius was William Dugdale, a former radical whom the critic Steven Marcus later called the Napoleon of the trade. Dugdale reissued old pornography under new titles, advertised common books as rare ones, borrowed other men's chapters and wrote admiring notices for books he had produced himself. He traded through aliases and shifting addresses, keeping the explicit stock upstairs or behind the counter for customers he trusted. Photography and the stereoscope joined the inventory as soon as the technology allowed.

For much of the century obscenity was policed less by the state than by private moral reformers. The Society for the Suppression of Vice bought incriminating material and prosecuted the people who sold it. In 1857 Lord Campbell's Obscene Publications Act gave magistrates statutory powers to seize and destroy obscene stock. Campbell spoke of protecting the morals of youth. Later historians have noticed the class line running underneath the concern. A gentleman could lock an expensive collection in a cabinet. Dugdale sold to anyone who had the coins.

Repeated prosecutions finally caught up with Dugdale. He died in 1868 in the Clerkenwell House of Correction while serving hard labour. Holywell Street followed between 1900 and 1903, demolished for the widening of the Strand and the construction of Aldwych and Kingsway. The shops disappeared. The stock moved west, first towards Charing Cross Road and then into Soho. Local lore says the holy well itself stayed behind, somewhere in a basement near Australia House, which would be a suitably stubborn survival.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)
Holywell Street was a narrow lane running parallel to the Strand, known earlier as "Rag Street" and "Booksellers' Row", which by the 1830s had become the centre of London's pornography trade — an 1834 investigation counted 57 shops selling indecent material. It was damned in the Victorian press as "the vilest street in the civilised world" (a phrase usually attributed to *The Times*, though the best-documented denunciation is the *Daily Telegraph*'s) and as "a place where dirt and darkness meet and make mortal compact".

The trade grew out of radical politics. After government crackdowns on sedition in the 1810s and 1820s, freethinking pamphleteers redirected their presses from dangerous political tracts into the safer, more lucrative business of pornography. The presiding figure was William Dugdale (1800–1868), whom Steven Marcus called the "Napoleon" of the trade. Dugdale reprinted old pornography under new titles, split books, added racy subtitles to innocent novels, wrote his own advertising copy, and traded under aliases — Turner, Smith, Young, Brown. His stock included *The Lustful Turk* and *The Autobiography of a Flea*, with photographic and stereoscopic pornography sold upstairs.

For most of the century obscenity was prosecuted not by the state but by the private Society for the Suppression of Vice (founded 1802), which brought 154 successful prosecutions between 1845 and 1868. In 1857 Lord Campbell drove through the Obscene Publications Act, giving magistrates statutory power to seize and destroy obscene material; he described the trade as "a sale of poison more deadly than prussic acid, strychnine, or arsenic" and pressed his Bill as protection for the morals of youth.

The pattern is telling. Historians argue that prosecution intensified as pornography became cheap enough to reach working-class and youthful buyers — an interpretation consistent with Campbell's stated fears, though not with any explicit words of his about price. British obscenity law — from Holywell Street to the *Lady Chatterley* trial — was always, at bottom, about class. Dugdale was repeatedly raided, once brandished a knife at a jury, and died in 1868 in the Clerkenwell House of Correction under hard labour.

Holywell Street was demolished between 1900 and 1903 for the Strand widening and the Aldwych scheme. Australia House stands over it. The "holy well" that named it reputedly survives in a basement nearby.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**The window trade** — Holywell Street's shops advertised through their windows: suggestive title pages and obscene engravings signalled what lay within. Inside was a dark, musty interior watched over by a suspicious shopkeeper, forever alert for undercover police. The rarest items — like the privately printed, obscenely illustrated *Story of a Dildoe!*, limited to 150 copies — were kept behind the counter or passed hand to hand, and the choicest photographs were sold only upstairs to trusted buyers.

**The Eton mail-order scandal** — In 1880 a police inspector went undercover to trace a family-run enterprise posting indecent photographs to Eton pupils in envelopes marked "art studies", in the hope of drumming up orders. The trail led to a Holywell Street bookshop, where, after earning the keeper's trust, he was led upstairs to "a dirty little place, part bed-room, part workshop". The shop was raided, 5,000 photographs seized, and the man given two years.

**Ashbee's forbidden index** — The Holywell Street trade was catalogued by Henry Spencer Ashbee, writing as "Pisanus Fraxi", whose three-volume bibliography of pornography (1877–85) mockingly echoed the Catholic Church's *Index Librorum Prohibitorum*. Part review, part critical analysis with absurd scholarly footnotes, it is the single richest record of what Victorian London's dirty bookshops actually sold — filth catalogued with the diligence of a monk.
