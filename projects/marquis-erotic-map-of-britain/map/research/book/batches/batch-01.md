## 1 — A Machine for Making and Unmaking Wives
*Hampton Court Palace · East Molesey, Surrey (historically Middlesex) · c.1514–1547*

### BOOK VERSION
At its Tudor height Hampton Court produced over 800 meals a day, served up by a staff of some 200 cooks, sergeants, grooms and pages working across a range of kitchens that Henry VIII had enlarged until they sprawled through more than fifty rooms and burned through well over a million logs a year. Beef arrived by the carcass. Wine came up the Thames by the barrel. This was not a house. It was a small, ravenous city devoted to the appetite of one man, and appetite is rather the point of the place.

Hampton Court was not Henry's idea. It was Cardinal Thomas Wolsey's. By an indenture of 11 January 1514 Wolsey took a ninety-nine-year lease on a manor from the Knights Hospitaller, at £50 a year, and spent the following years and a genuinely startling amount of money — the traditional figure is 200,000 gold crowns — turning it into the grandest house in England. He built so well that he rather fatally out-built his king. When Wolsey failed to extract from the Pope the annulment Henry wanted from Catherine of Aragon, he handed Hampton Court over in a doomed attempt to keep the royal favour. It did not work. Wolsey died in 1530 at Leicester Abbey, on his way south to almost certain trial. The palace passed to Henry, who set about making it his own.

What Henry built there tells you exactly how a Tudor monarch thought about privacy, power and sex, because at Hampton Court those three things were the same thing wearing different hats. A visitor moved through a deliberate sequence of rooms, each one harder to enter than the last: the Great Hall, then the Great Watching Chamber where the guard stood, then the Presence Chamber, then the Privy Chamber, and finally the innermost secret lodgings and the bedchamber. The architectural historian Simon Thurley has shown how this worked as a filtering system: the closer you got to the King's bed, the more important you were. Access was the currency. The bedchamber was the treasury.

Henry pushed the private rooms further and further from the crowds, and to guarantee genuine seclusion he raised the Bayne Tower — the name comes from the French *bain*, a bath — a three-storey block beside his bedchamber containing his study, his library, his jewels, and a bathroom with hot and cold running water piped from cisterns on the roof. Here was a king who could lock the world out and soak in warm water while, a few rooms away, several hundred courtiers jostled for the right to hand him his shirt.

All of this magnificence had one blunt biological purpose. Henry needed a son. The whole convulsion of the 1530s — the break with Rome, the founding of the Church of England, the deaths — flowed from a dynastic anxiety about a male heir, and the marriage bed at Hampton Court was where that anxiety was supposed to be resolved. A royal marriage was not private life. Consummation was a matter of state, witnessed in its consequences by ambassadors and recorded in dispatches. A queen's body was a public instrument, and the palace was the machine built around it.

Anne Boleyn is written faintly into the fabric. She and Henry stayed here in the early 1530s while grand new lodgings were built for her overlooking the park; those rooms were demolished later in the seventeenth century. After her execution in 1536 her badges were stripped out — though the palace's gatehouse is still called the Anne Boleyn Gateway, and high in the Great Hall ceiling, missed by the workmen, the entwined initials H and A survive: one of the more poignant clerical oversights in English history. She had connected this place to Hever Castle in Kent, where Henry had courted her for years; she left it as a ghost in the plasterwork.

It was Jane Seymour, Henry's third wife, who gave him what he wanted. In October 1537, at Hampton Court, she bore a son, the future Edward VI. The court erupted in relief. Twelve days later Jane was dead, of complications following the birth. Henry, by all accounts genuinely grief-stricken, had at last got his heir and lost the wife who produced him in a fortnight — the transaction of Tudor queenship laid bare in a single terrible week.

Then there is Catherine Howard, and here we must be careful, because history and the tourist trade have not been. Catherine was a girl — probably about seventeen when Henry married her in July 1540, weeks after annulling his marriage to Anne of Cleves. Henry was forty-nine, obese, and increasingly frightening. Catherine had been placed at court by ambitious Howard relatives who saw in her a route to influence, exactly as a previous generation had used her cousin Anne Boleyn. In November 1541 evidence of her earlier relationships reached the King. She was confined to her rooms, then taken away, and executed at the Tower in February 1542. She was a young woman used by the adults around her and destroyed by a system that treated her body as property; that is the true story, and it is grim enough without embroidery.

The embroidery is the Haunted Gallery. The legend holds that Catherine broke free of her guards, ran screaming along a gallery towards the chapel where Henry was at prayer, and was dragged back — and that her ghost re-enacts the dash to this day. Historic Royal Palaces, which runs the place, is admirably honest about this: it labels the tale a legend. So should we. Lucy Worsley, who was a curator at the palace for twenty-one years, has pointed out that the famous cold spot in the gallery is caused by a draught from a concealed staircase, and adds, drily, that she does not believe in ghosts. There is no solid contemporary evidence for the screaming run at all. What we have instead is a real, documented cruelty — a terrified teenager under arrest — which the Victorians, who understood that terror sells tickets, reupholstered as a spook story. The debunking is not a killjoy footnote. It is the whole moral of the room: a real girl's suffering turned into a visitor attraction.

Anne of Cleves supplies the comic counterpoint, and the comedy is entirely at Henry's expense. His fourth marriage, in January 1540, was a diplomatic arrangement with a German duchy; on meeting Anne he decided he did not fancy her and never consummated the match. Within six months it was annulled. Anne, no fool, agreed at once, was rechristened the King's "beloved sister", handsomely housed, and quietly outlived every one of them — later holding Hever Castle itself. She is the one wife who read the machine correctly and stepped off it before it could close.

Over the inner gateway hangs the astronomical clock, installed in 1540, designed by the King's astronomer Nicholas Kratzer and made by the clockmaker Nicholas Oursian. Fifteen feet across, gloriously pre-Copernican, it puts the earth at the centre of the universe and tells you the hour, the month, the phase of the moon and — most usefully for courtiers arriving by barge — the time of high water at London Bridge. It is, in a sense, the perfect emblem of the place: a beautiful, expensive machine with the King's world firmly at its centre, everything else revolving obediently around it. Much of its mechanism has in fact been renewed over the centuries, the Victorians doing a good deal of the work now taken for Tudor, but the theatre of it is authentic enough.

Stand in Clock Court today and the argument makes itself. This was the most sophisticated pleasure palace in England, wired for warm baths and wine fountains, and it was also a device for producing and disposing of women according to their dynastic usefulness. Two of Henry's six wives died as a direct result of their duty to him — one in childbed, one on the block — and both were connected to these rooms. The bedchamber was a room of state. The women who entered it were instruments of policy. The wonder of Hampton Court is not that it is beautiful. It is that we can still walk through the exact machinery and feel how it worked.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)
Hampton Court was built to feed one man's appetites — literally. At its Tudor height the kitchens turned out over 800 meals a day, staffed by some 200 people, and the whole vast complex existed to serve the private life of Henry VIII.

The palace wasn't his idea. Cardinal Wolsey took a ninety-nine-year lease in 1514 and spent a fortune making it the finest house in England — so fine that he out-built his king. When Wolsey failed to win Henry his annulment from Catherine of Aragon, he surrendered Hampton Court to keep the royal favour. It didn't save him.

Henry rebuilt on a colossal scale, and the way he arranged the rooms tells you how Tudor power worked. To reach the King you passed through a sequence — Great Hall, Great Watching Chamber, Presence Chamber, Privy Chamber, and finally the secret lodgings and bedchamber. The closer you got to his bed, the more powerful you were. Access was everything. He even raised the Bayne Tower, with a bathroom fed by hot and cold running water, so he could shut the world out entirely.

All this served one purpose: a male heir. Jane Seymour delivered it in October 1537, bearing the future Edward VI here — and died twelve days later. Catherine Howard, married at around seventeen to a man of forty-nine, was arrested here in November 1541 and executed months afterwards; she was a girl used by ambitious relatives and destroyed by a dynastic system, not the giggling villainess of legend.

That legend is the "Haunted Gallery," where Catherine's ghost supposedly re-runs a screaming dash to beg Henry for mercy. Historic Royal Palaces itself calls it a legend, and a former curator notes the famous "cold spot" is simply a draught. The Victorians, who knew terror sold tickets, turned a real cruelty into a ghost tour.

Over the inner gate, the 1540 astronomical clock still turns — pre-Copernican, with the earth at its centre and everything revolving around it. A fair emblem for a palace that was, in the end, a machine built around the body of whichever woman Henry currently needed.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**The oversight in the ceiling.** After Anne Boleyn's execution in 1536, Henry ordered her badges and initials scrubbed from the palace. The workmen were thorough — but not perfect. High in the hammerbeam roof of the Great Hall, the entwined initials "H" and "A" still survive, missed in the purge. It is the smallest and most human trace of a marriage that broke a church.

**The King's private plumbing.** The Bayne Tower took its name from the French *bain*, bath. Inside, Henry had a bathroom supplied with hot and cold running water piped from lead cisterns on the roof — an almost science-fictional luxury in the 1530s, when most of his subjects washed, if at all, in cold water from a bucket.

**The wife who won by losing.** Anne of Cleves is remembered as the wife Henry found unattractive. She was also the cleverest. Offered an annulment in 1540, she agreed instantly, was made the King's honoured "sister," given houses and income — and outlived all five of the others, at one point holding Anne Boleyn's own Hever Castle.

## 2 — Seventeen Letters and a Seven-Year No
*Hever Castle · Hever, Edenbridge, Kent · c.1526–1533*

### BOOK VERSION
"Having been for above a whole year stricken with the dart of love," Henry VIII wrote, "and not yet sure whether I shall fail of finding a place in your heart and affection." A king who by his own admission loathed writing letters had picked up a pen, and gone on picking it up, because a young woman from Kent kept not quite saying yes.

The woman was Anne Boleyn, and the place that made her was Hever Castle, a moated manor near Edenbridge that her family had held since her father inherited it in 1505. It is worth being honest about how little we know of her beginnings, because the uncertainty starts with the most basic fact of all. Anne's birth year is genuinely disputed: most modern historians settle on around 1501, but two seventeenth-century sources point to 1507, and the six-year gap changes how we read everything she did. A woman of thirty holding out for a crown is a different story from a girl of twenty-four. The record simply does not tell us which we are looking at.

What we do know is her education, and it was extraordinary for an English gentlewoman. In 1513 her father sent her abroad, first to the sophisticated court of Margaret of Austria at Mechelen in the Low Countries, then to France, where she served Mary Tudor (briefly queen to the ageing Louis XII) and afterwards Queen Claude, wife of Francis I. She stayed in France for the better part of a decade. She came home around 1521 speaking flawless French, versed in music, dancing, fashion and theology, and carrying a continental polish that made the daughters of the English gentry look provincial. She had, in effect, been finished at the two most cultured courts in Europe. Henry never stood a chance.

The seventeen surviving love letters he wrote her are among the most remarkable documents in English royal history, and not only for their content. Eight are in English, nine in French — the language of courtly love — and none is dated, which is why scholars still argue over their order and place them loosely between 1527 and 1528, with Henry's pursuit beginning perhaps a year earlier. They are, frankly, the letters of a besotted and slightly desperate man. He signs some of them by drawing a heart around Anne's initials, like a lovestruck adolescent with the powers of a tyrant. In one he closes wishing himself "in my sweetheart's arms," in language warm enough to make a modern reader look away and check nobody is reading over their shoulder. And here is the strange, sad asymmetry at the heart of the archive: we have every one of Henry's letters and not a single one of Anne's replies. We hear only half the conversation. We must infer her from the shape of the silence — from what Henry answers, complains about, and pleads against.

What she was doing, across those years, was refusing. Anne's sister, Mary Boleyn, had already been Henry's mistress, and the arrangement had left her precisely nowhere; when it ended, so did the King's interest. Anne appears to have drawn the obvious conclusion. She would not be a mistress. She held out — for years — for marriage and a crown, and in doing so turned a private infatuation into a constitutional emergency. Because the only way Henry could marry Anne was to be rid of Catherine of Aragon, and the only way to be rid of Catherine was to break with the Pope who would not permit it. The English Reformation has many causes, but one of them, undeniably, is a woman at Hever saying no and meaning it.

A word about Mary Boleyn, because a great deal is said about her and much of it is later gossip dressed as fact. That Mary was Henry's mistress is reasonably well evidenced; Henry himself later obtained a dispensation that only makes sense if he had slept with Anne's sister. That she had earlier been a mistress of Francis I of France is repeated everywhere and rests on far thinner ground — a couple of hostile continental remarks, hardened by retelling into "the great prostitute" of Tudor legend. The careful position is to say that the relationship with Henry is documented and the rest is rumour, and to resist the centuries of eager embroidery.

In the summer of 1528 the story nearly ended in a different way entirely. The sweating sickness — a terrifying disease that could kill within hours — swept through England, and Anne caught it at Hever. Henry, who was frightened of illness to the point of cowardice and fled from it whenever he could, did not come. But he sent his own physician to attend her, and wrote anxiously. She recovered. Had she not, there would have been no Queen Anne, no Elizabeth I, and quite possibly no Church of England — a reminder of how much of history hangs on the constitution of a single immune system.

Hever survives, and survives handsomely, largely because an American gave it a second life: William Waldorf Astor bought and restored it in the early twentieth century, laying out the gardens and filling the rooms. You can stand in the house where Anne grew up, and where those letters arrived by messenger, and where she formed the resolve that would remake the country. There is a neat, almost novelistic coda in the deeds, too: after Anne's fall, Hever eventually passed to Anne of Cleves — the wife who survived Henry by knowing when to yield, inheriting the childhood home of the wife who never yielded at all.

As for the letters, they took an even stranger journey than their author. They are not in England. They sit in the Vatican Library, in Rome, catalogued as Vat. lat. 3731, and how they got there was for centuries a mystery answered only with a shrug and the word "stolen" — probably, people said, to be used against Henry in the annulment fight. Recent scholarship tells a better story. In a 2023 essay in the *Times Literary Supplement*, the historians Peter Lake, Michael Questier, Kendra Packham and Estelle Paranque traced the letters still sitting in England as late as 1601, in the hands of an English Jesuit who meant to send them to a colleague in Rome writing a history of the Reformation. The most intimate words the King of England ever wrote were carried to the heart of the church he had defied — not by a thief in the 1530s, but by a Catholic exile, half a century later, as evidence in the long argument over what desire had cost.

### WEB VERSION (~350 words)
"Having been for above a whole year stricken with the dart of love." A king who hated writing letters wrote them anyway, because a young woman from Kent kept not quite saying yes.

Anne Boleyn grew up at Hever, but she was made abroad. From 1513 her father sent her to the courts of Margaret of Austria in the Low Countries and then France, where she served two queens. She came home around 1521 fluent in French and polished in music, dance and theology — dazzlingly sophisticated by the standards of the English gentry.

Henry's seventeen surviving love letters to her are astonishing: eight in English, nine in French, undated, written roughly 1527–28. He draws hearts around her initials. He wishes himself "in my sweetheart's arms." And yet not one of Anne's replies survives — we hear only his half of the conversation and must guess at hers from what he answers.

She held out for marriage and a crown — and in doing so forced a constitutional crisis, because the only route to marrying her ran through breaking with Rome. A woman at Hever saying "no" is one of the genuine causes of the English Reformation.

(On Mary Boleyn: her affair with Henry is well evidenced; the claim that she was also mistress to Francis I of France rests on much thinner, gossipy ground — worth separating fact from centuries of embroidery.)

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**The disputed birthday.** We do not know when Anne Boleyn was born. Most historians say around 1501; two seventeenth-century sources say 1507. It sounds like pedantry until you realise the gap changes the whole story: a woman of thirty gambling on a crown is a very different figure from a girl of twenty-four.

**The doctor Henry sent instead of himself.** When the sweating sickness reached Hever in 1528, Henry VIII — who fled illness whenever he could — did not visit the woman he was pursuing. He sent his personal physician and a stream of anxious letters. Anne recovered. Had she not, there would have been no Elizabeth I.

**Hever's second American life.** The castle you visit today owes its survival to William Waldorf Astor, who bought it in the early twentieth century and poured a fortune into restoring the house and creating its famous gardens. After Anne's fall, the estate had earlier passed — with tidy historical irony — to Anne of Cleves.

## 3 — The Properest Long Man She Ever Saw
*Wemyss Castle, Fife, and the Palace of Holyroodhouse, Edinburgh · 1565–1566*

### BOOK VERSION
On the evening of 9 March 1566, in a small supper room off her bedchamber at Holyroodhouse, Mary, Queen of Scots was six months pregnant and having dinner with a few friends when armed men came up the private stair. What happened next would end her marriage, endanger her crown and echo down Scottish history. But to understand why it happened, you have to go back thirteen months and a little way up the coast, to the moment she first laid eyes on the man who caused it.

The place was Wemyss Castle, perched on the sea cliffs of Fife above the Firth of Forth, and the date was 17 February 1565. Mary was twenty-two: tall, clever, and — as reigning Queen of Scots, dowager Queen of France and a serious claimant to the English throne — very possibly the most eligible woman in Europe. She had been widowed for four years and needed a husband, an heir, and, one suspects, a bit of pleasure in a life that had so far been mostly duty. Into this walked Henry Stuart, Lord Darnley: nineteen, over six feet tall, elegant, a decent hand with a lute, and — this mattered, because Mary herself stood about six feet — someone she did not tower over on the dance floor.

The one contemporary voice we have for the meeting is Sir James Melville, whose memoirs record that Mary reckoned Darnley "the properest and best proportioned long man that ever she had seen." It is worth attributing that carefully — it is Melville's recollection, written later, not a transcript — but it has the ring of truth, and it did the damage. Within weeks the courtship was a love affair. Mary nursed Darnley through an illness. On 29 July 1565 they married in the Chapel Royal at Holyroodhouse, she twenty-two and he nineteen, and for a very short while it seemed a triumph.

It curdled almost at once. Darnley was vain, idle, given to drink and to sulking, and above all he wanted power he had not earned. He demanded the Crown Matrimonial — the right to rule as king in his own name and, crucially, to keep the throne if Mary died before him. It was an enormous thing to ask, and Mary, sensibly, would not grant it. A marriage that had begun in physical infatuation became a cold war over the constitution, which is a very Scottish sort of tragedy.

Into the gap between them stepped David Rizzio, an Italian musician who had risen to become Mary's private secretary and trusted confidant. He and the Queen shared a love of music and talk; he had her ear; and Darnley, encouraged by nobles with their own reasons for wanting Rizzio gone, convinced himself — or allowed himself to be convinced — that the friendship was something more. It almost certainly was not. But sexual jealousy rarely troubles itself with evidence, and a jealous consort with a grievance about power is a dangerous combination.

Which brings us back to that supper room. On 9 March 1566 Darnley and a large body of armed men, led by Lord Ruthven, made their way to the Queen's private chambers and seized Rizzio. That the Queen was six months pregnant with the child who would become James VI did not stay their hands; a loaded pistol was reportedly held to her. Rizzio was dragged from the room and killed, and Darnley's own dagger was left in the body to mark his complicity. It was a political murder staged deliberately in front of a pregnant queen — an act designed as much to terrorise and control Mary as to remove her secretary. I will not linger on the killing itself, which was as brutal as these things are. The point that matters is what it revealed: that Mary's husband would spill blood in her own rooms, and that her marriage was now a threat to her life and her throne.

She responded with extraordinary nerve. Within days she had detached the weak-willed Darnley from his fellow conspirators, and under cover of darkness the royal couple slipped out of Holyrood to safety. In June 1566 she was delivered of a healthy son. The marriage, though, was finished in all but name, and its final chapter belongs to another kind of book: in February 1567 Darnley himself was killed, at Kirk o' Field, in circumstances that would help bring Mary down. That is a consequence, and I leave it as one line.

What survives of all this is worth knowing. Wemyss Castle still stands above the Forth, the ancient seat of the Wemyss family, a Category A listed building and very much a private home — its walled gardens are opened to visitors on a limited basis, but the castle itself is not on show. It is, appropriately, a place you glimpse rather than tour: the first meeting happened somewhere the public cannot quite reach.

Holyroodhouse is the opposite. Now the King's official residence in Scotland and cared for by the Royal Collection Trust, it shows visitors the oldest part of the palace — the James V tower — including Mary's bedchamber and the little supper room where it all went wrong. And here we must deal with the tourist fiction, because it is a good one. For generations, visitors have been shown a dark stain on the floorboards and told it is Rizzio's blood, indelible after four and a half centuries. It is nothing of the sort. The Royal Collection Trust notes that the fascination with Mary's tragedy sent visitors flocking from the later eighteenth century onward, and the "bloodstain" grew up to meet the demand; the floorboards themselves were largely replaced long after 1566, and the stain has, over the years, been helpfully refreshed. Chip away the legend and you are left with something more interesting than a magic bloodstain: a real room, a real terror, and a queen who kept her head when men were losing theirs around her.

The long shadow of that meeting at Wemyss stretches further than anyone in 1565 could have known. The child Mary carried through the supper-room ordeal became James VI of Scotland, and in 1603 he became James I of England too, uniting the crowns and founding the Stuart line that would, two generations on, give England its most sexually candid king of all — Charles II, whose court we shall visit shortly. A tall young man with a lute, glimpsed across a hall in Fife, set in motion a dynasty.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT
**The dagger left behind.** When Rizzio was killed, Darnley's own dagger was reportedly left in the body. It was a signature. In the vicious factional politics of the Scottish court, the point was not to hide the crime but to advertise exactly who had sanctioned it — and to bind the conspirators together in guilt.

**The escape in the dark.** Days after the murder, the six-months-pregnant Mary talked her weak husband out of his alliance with the killers and slipped out of Holyrood by night to the safety of Dunbar. It was one of the coolest pieces of nerve in her whole eventful life, and it saved both her and the son she was carrying.

**The child in the middle of it.** The baby Mary carried through the supper-room ordeal was born in June 1566 and became James VI of Scots. In 1603 he inherited England's throne too, uniting the crowns — which means the whole later Stuart dynasty, Charles II and all, traces back to a marriage that began at Wemyss and unravelled at Holyrood.

## 4 — Pretty, Witty, and Freehold
*Banqueting House, Whitehall, and 79 Pall Mall · City of Westminster, London · 1660–1687*

### BOOK VERSION
Everybody knows the building at 79 Pall Mall is Nell Gwyn's house. Everybody is wrong. The handsome Italianate pile standing there today went up in 1866–68, designed by David Brandon for the Eagle Insurance Company, and Nell had been dead for nearly two centuries before the first brick was laid. What is true is that on this plot of ground once stood the house of the most beloved royal mistress in English history — and that the ground itself carries a genuine constitutional peculiarity, which we will come to, because it is the best thing about her.

First, the world she rose into. When Charles II came back from exile in 1660, he brought with him an appetite for pleasure that eleven years of Puritan rule had only sharpened, and he installed it at Whitehall Palace — by common consent the most sexually frank court in English history. The diarist John Evelyn, no prude but easily shocked, described the King as "addicted to women," and the King spent his reign proving him right. Of that sprawling palace almost nothing survives; it burned in 1698. The one great fragment left standing is the Banqueting House, built by Inigo Jones in 1622 and ceilinged with Rubens's vast painted apotheosis of the Stuarts — a fitting relic, since it was also the building outside which Charles's father was executed in 1649. The son grew up knowing exactly what the family business cost.

Charles conducted his love life at a scale that beggars the modern imagination. His mistresses were famous in their own right: Barbara Villiers, Lady Castlemaine and later Duchess of Cleveland, imperious and grasping; Louise de Kérouaille, Duchess of Portsmouth, the French Catholic widely assumed to be a spy for Louis XIV; the actress Moll Davis; the flamboyant Hortense Mancini; and Nell. He publicly acknowledged fourteen illegitimate children by seven mistresses — six of them borne by Barbara Villiers alone — and produced, with his patient and childless queen, Catherine of Braganza, not one legitimate heir. The whole gaudy edifice of Restoration royal fertility rested on a barren marriage, which is the kind of irony the age's poets could not resist. The Earl of Rochester, the court's laureate of libertinism, skewered his monarch in a couplet — "Restless he rolls from whore to whore, / A merry monarch, scandalous and poor" — and, remarkably, kept his head.

Nell Gwyn came up from nothing, and never pretended otherwise. Born around 1650 into real poverty — her mother kept a brothel — she sold oranges at the King's Theatre before the actor Charles Hart took her in hand and turned her into the finest comic actress of the age. Samuel Pepys, who saw everything, adored her and called her "pretty, witty Nell." By about 1668 she had caught the King, and she held him, on and off, until his death, bearing him two sons — the elder of whom became Duke of St Albans, an English dukedom that descends from an orange-seller from Drury Lane.

In February 1671 Charles installed her in a townhouse at 79 Pall Mall. Evelyn, walking with the King through St James's Park on 2 March that year, was scandalised to overhear "a very familiar discourse between the King and Mrs Nelly," she leaning over her garden wall on a raised terrace, he standing on the green walk below. Evelyn confided that he was "heartily sorry" at the scene, which tells you rather more about Evelyn than about Nell.

Here is the constitutional oddity. The Pall Mall house was Crown property, granted to Nell on a lease. She objected. According to a tradition recorded in the eighteenth century and preserved by the *Survey of London*, she declared that "she had always conveyed free under the Crown, and always would," and refused to accept the house until the freehold was conveyed to her outright by Act of Parliament. In 1676 she got her way. The freehold has been in private hands ever since — making the site of 79 Pall Mall, as the *Survey of London* confirmed, the only ground on the south side of the street not owned by the Crown. It is a small, permanent joke lodged in the London land registry: a king's mistress, born in a bawdy-house, holds the one scrap of Pall Mall the Crown could not keep. The exact wording of her retort is traditional rather than documented from her own lips, but the freehold itself is solid fact, and it is the perfect monument to her — earthy, shrewd, and unbudgeable.

The other thing everyone knows about Nell is the "Protestant whore" story: that her coach was mobbed at Oxford (or London — the versions differ) by a crowd who mistook her for the Catholic Louise de Kérouaille, whereupon she leaned out and cried, "Good people, you are mistaken; I am the Protestant whore," and won them over. It is a wonderful line and it belongs to her in spirit, but honesty compels a note: the anecdote is not contemporaneously documented, comes down through later retellings, and varies in its details. Treat it as the truest sort of legend — the kind that is right about a person even when it cannot be proved about an event. It survives because it captures something real: unlike the aloof foreign duchesses, Nell was a Londoner the London mob claimed as their own.

She stayed loyal to Charles to the end. On his deathbed in 1685 he is said to have asked his brother to "let not poor Nelly starve," and James II, to his credit, paid her debts. She did not long outlive her king. After a stroke she died on 14 November 1687, aged about thirty-seven, and was buried at St Martin-in-the-Fields; her funeral sermon was preached, at her own request, by Thomas Tenison, the future Archbishop of Canterbury, who "said much to her praise" and took some criticism for it. A vicar who would rise to the top of the English church, standing up to eulogise an orange-seller and royal mistress — it is a very Nell Gwyn kind of ending.

And the house? Gone, of course, replaced by that Victorian insurance office, which now bears a blue plaque noting the association. You can stand outside it. There is not a great deal to see. But somewhere under the Portland stone and the nineteenth-century pretension lies the only freehold on the south side of Pall Mall — the one thing she made sure the Crown would never get back.

## 5 — The Wife Who Was and Wasn't
*Royal Pavilion and St John the Baptist Church · Brighton, East Sussex · 1785–1837*

### BOOK VERSION
On a memorial in a Catholic church on the Bristol Road in Brighton, the effigy of a woman wears three wedding rings. She had three husbands, which would be unremarkable were it not that the third was the King of England, and that the marriage which mattered most to her was, in the eyes of English law, no marriage at all. The woman is Maria Fitzherbert, and her three rings are the neatest possible summary of one of the strangest romantic tangles in British royal history.

Maria was born Maria Anne Smythe in 1756, a member of the Catholic gentry, and by her late twenties she had already buried two husbands — Edward Weld, who died within months of their wedding, and Thomas Fitzherbert, who left her a wealthy and self-possessed widow. She entered London society and there, in the spring of 1784, met George, Prince of Wales, heir to the throne and six years her junior. He fell for her with the headlong, theatrical intensity that would characterise his whole life; on one occasion he is said to have staged a suicidal gesture to force her hand. Maria, devout and sensible, would not become his mistress. So George did the one thing guaranteed to cause maximum constitutional trouble. He married her.

The ceremony took place on 15 December 1785, in the drawing room of her house in Park Street, Mayfair, before a tiny company: her uncle and her brother as witnesses, and a clergyman, the Reverend Robert Burt, sprung from the Fleet debtors' prison for the occasion by the payment of his £500 debts (perhaps £80,000 today). It was, in the sincerity of the moment, a real wedding. In law it was doubly, comprehensively void. The Royal Marriages Act of 1772 made any marriage by a member of the royal family invalid without the sovereign's consent, and George III had most certainly not been asked. And the Act of Settlement of 1701 provided that any heir who married a Roman Catholic forfeited his place in the succession. So the marriage that George had contracted could not be legal — and had it somehow been legal, it would have cost him the throne. He had found a way to lose either way, which was a kind of genius.

The secret could not hold. By 1787 rumours of the marriage were loud enough to threaten the Prince's finances, which depended on Parliament clearing his monumental debts. His ally Charles James Fox rose in the House of Commons and denied the marriage outright, calling the rumour a malicious falsehood — on what he believed was the Prince's own authority. Maria, who had gone through a ceremony she regarded as sacred, found herself publicly declared never to have been a wife at all, by the Prince's own spokesman. She was humiliated and furious. That she eventually forgave him tells you something about the peculiar gravity he exerted.

Brighton is where they went to be, as far as possible, themselves. The Prince had first come to the little seaside town in 1783; in 1786 he took a farmhouse, and in 1787 he had the architect Henry Holland transform it into a neat neoclassical villa, the Marine Pavilion. Maria was installed nearby. Later, in 1804, William Porden built her Steine House a short walk away — she was the not-quite-wife kept in comfortable, deniable proximity. And then, between 1815 and 1823, John Nash wrapped Holland's tidy villa in the astonishing confection we see today: the Royal Pavilion, all Indian domes and minarets outside and delirious chinoiserie within, a pleasure-dome raised on cast iron and royal debt. It is one of the maddest, happiest buildings in England, and it exists because a prince wanted a private world by the sea.

The private world did not last, because the public duties of a prince intervened in the crudest way. By the mid-1790s George was, once again, catastrophically in debt, and Parliament would only relieve him if he made a proper, legal, Protestant marriage. In 1795 he married his cousin, Caroline of Brunswick, a union so instantly and mutually loathsome that the couple separated almost immediately after producing a daughter, Princess Charlotte. George reportedly needed to be drunk to get through the wedding. The contrast could hardly be sharper: the marriage the law recognised was a disaster, and the marriage the law denied was the one he actually wanted. In the years that followed he returned to Maria more than once — there were separations and reconciliations — and Rome, characteristically, took her side: around 1800 the papacy ruled that in the eyes of the Catholic Church she was, and had always been, George's lawful wife.

Maria outlived him. She spent her later years quietly in Brighton and died at Steine House in 1837, and was buried at the church of St John the Baptist on the Bristol Road, where her memorial shows those three rings. George had died seven years earlier, in 1830, King at last and long estranged from her — and yet, when they laid him out, he was found to be wearing a miniature portrait of Maria Fitzherbert around his neck, as he had asked to be. Whatever the law said, and it said a great deal, he went into his coffin married to her in the only court that finally mattered to him.

There is a pattern here worth naming, because it runs right through this book. British erotic history is, over and over, a history of desire colliding with regulation — of laws written to control who may marry whom, and of the endless human ingenuity applied to getting around them. The Royal Marriages Act that voided George and Maria's wedding is the same impulse that sent runaway couples bolting to Gretna Green, that filled the Fleet prison with clandestine weddings, that drew the desperate to a holy well in Wales; we will meet all of them later. And the rhyme carries forward, too. A century and a half after George stood in a Mayfair drawing room and married a twice-widowed Catholic he was forbidden to marry, another Prince of Wales would face the same collision between the crown and an unacceptable twice-married woman — and Edward VIII, unlike his ancestor, would choose the woman over the throne. George never had to make that choice. He kept the crown, and kept Maria's face against his chest, and let the law say what it liked.
