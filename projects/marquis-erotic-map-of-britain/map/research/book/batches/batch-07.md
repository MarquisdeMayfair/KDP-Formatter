## 31 — Medieval Bawdy: Chaucer's Tabard and Gwerful Mechain
*Tabard Inn, Southwark, London & Llanfechain, Powys, Wales · c.1387–1502*

### BOOK VERSION

The best-known pub in English literature does not exist. It stopped existing in 1873, when the demolition men pulled down a dilapidated galleried coaching inn on the east side of Borough High Street in Southwark, a building that had by then spent two centuries being called the Talbot because a signwriter, at some point after the great Southwark fire of 1676, had confused a tabard (a herald's sleeveless coat) with a talbot (a breed of hunting dog) and nobody had ever quite got round to fixing it.

The original Tabard was old even in Chaucer's day. It had been built for the Abbot of Hyde around 1307 as a place for him and his fellow churchmen to stay when business brought them to London, and by 1381 the tax rolls record an innkeeper living there — one Henry Bailly, with his wife Christian. That name matters. When Geoffrey Chaucer sat down around 1387 to write the frame for *The Canterbury Tales*, he set his pilgrims' departure at the Tabard and made their genial, bullying master of ceremonies a man called Harry Bailly. He was using the name of the real landlord of the real pub. It is as if a modern novelist set a story in a named Wetherspoon's and gave the manager his actual name.

Chaucer was not a full-time poet. Almost nobody was. He was a civil servant, and from 1374 he held one of the more thankless jobs in the medieval bureaucracy: Controller of Customs for the port of London, checking that the correct duty was levied on the wool, sheepskins and leather leaving the country. This was no minor sideline of the exchequer. The historian Mark Ormrod called Edward III's wool taxes "the single most important weapon in the financial armoury of the late medieval state," the custom-plus-subsidy on a sack of wool exceeding thirty per cent of its value — which made Chaucer's a serious, numerate, document-heavy post. He held it for twelve years while living rent-free in an apartment over Aldgate. The *Tales* were written in the margins of a working life.

And what he wrote, in those margins, is the filthiest great poem in English before the modern era. Not all of it — there are saints' lives and sermons and a knightly romance — but Chaucer understood that a pilgrimage is a cross-section of society and that a cross-section of society, given a few drinks, tells dirty jokes. The Miller's Tale is the purest of these: a randy Oxford lodger named Nicholas seduces his landlord's teenage wife Alison, persuades the gullible old carpenter that a second Noah's flood is coming so he'll sleep in a tub hung from the rafters, and — the detail every schoolchild remembers — a rejected suitor, Absolon, comes to the window in the dark begging a kiss, and Alison offers him not her lips. The Reeve's Tale answers it with two Cambridge students taking their revenge on a thieving miller by bedding his wife and daughter in a single dark night of misdirected beds and moved cradles. These are *fabliaux* — short, obscene comic tales — and Chaucer tells them with a technical brilliance that has kept them on syllabuses precisely because teachers cannot pretend they are about anything other than what they are about.

But the truly startling voice belongs to a woman. The Wife of Bath — Alison of Bath, a prosperous cloth-maker — is given the longest prologue of any pilgrim, and she uses it to talk, with tremendous relish and not a shred of shame, about sex, marriage and power. She has buried five husbands. She married the first three, who were old and rich, for their money, and cheerfully describes withholding sex until they signed over their land; she loved the fifth, who was young and hit her, best of all. She discusses her own appetite as a simple fact of life. Chaucer even lets her make a joke that turns on the word "queynte" — a pun hovering between "a curious thing" and the plainest available Anglo-Saxon word for the female anatomy — and she deploys it more than once. The astonishing thing is not the smut. The astonishing thing is the argument underneath it: that what women most desire is "sovereynetee," mastery, sovereignty over their own husbands and their own lives. A fourteenth-century male poet put that thesis in the mouth of a woman and gave her the wit to defend it. It would be centuries before English literature was that frank about a woman's desires again — and when D. H. Lawrence tried, they took him to the Old Bailey.

Southwark, it should be said, was the right postcode for all this. It lay outside the City of London's jurisdiction, under the more relaxed authority of the Bishop of Winchester, whose Bankside "stews" employed the Winchester Geese, whose licensed trade this book returns to in Chapter 10. Chaucer's pilgrims set out for Canterbury from the exact edge of medieval London's red-light district.

Two centuries and the better part of two hundred miles away, in the Powys parish of Llanfechain in mid-Wales, a woman was writing something even blunter. Gwerful Mechain (flourished c.1460–1502) is the only medieval Welsh woman poet from whom a substantial body of work survives — the daughter of Hywel Fychan of Mechain and a woman named Gwenhwyfar, married to John ap Llywelyn Fychan, mother of a daughter called Mawd. She wrote devotional verse of real seriousness ("Christ's Passion," "Death and Judgement") in the fiendishly demanding strict metres of Welsh bardic poetry, all internal rhyme and consonantal chiming, called *cynghanedd*. And she wrote, in exactly the same exacting metre, a poem called "Cywydd y Cedor" — "Ode to the Vulva."

It is not a euphemism-laden thing. It opens by scolding male poets — the great professionals — for lavishing praise on a woman's eyes, her hair, her hands, her elegant throat, and then stopping, cowards that they are, at exactly the point that matters, ignoring "the best bit." And then Gwerful does what they would not: she praises the vulva directly, at length, ingeniously, in the high poetic manner usually reserved for God or a patron. Her modern translator, Professor Katie Gramich of Cardiff University, whose bilingual edition *The Works of Gwerful Mechain* (2018) finally brought her to a wide readership, has admitted to blushing when reading the Welsh aloud. The poem was a riposte, roughly a century late, to Dafydd ap Gwilym's earlier "Cywydd y Gal," "Ode to the Penis." Fair, you might think, is fair.

The manuscripts prove she was no isolated eccentric. According to Gramich's edition, "There are 13 manuscript copies of this cywydd, located in the University of Bangor, the British Library, Cardiff Central Library, and the National Library of Wales." She exchanged poems — jousting, flirtatious, sometimes obscene — with the leading male poets of her day, Dafydd Llwyd of Mathafarn (whom scholars think was probably her lover) and Llywelyn ap Gutyn. She answered a misogynist, Ieuan Dyfi, with a defence of women. Her range was total: she also wrote a short, cold, unforgettable poem addressed to her husband after he beat her, a curse wishing a dagger into his chest. It is documented, it is attributed to her, and it is a reminder that a woman writing gleefully about sex in the fifteenth century was not necessarily a woman who was safe.

No house of Gwerful's survives. Llanfechain is a quiet mid-Wales village and there is nothing to visit but the churchyard and the manuscripts in their four libraries. But that is rather the point of this pairing. Before print, before the Reformation, before the Puritans and the Victorians got to work, English and Welsh literature contained women and men writing about sex with a directness that the next five centuries would systematically bury. Gwerful was suppressed, Gramich argues, by the very Welsh scholars of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries who should have been preserving her — omitted from the anthologies because she was indecent. The frankness of the Welsh material this book keeps finding — the bundling beds, the holy wells — was buried by the same hands. What survives of the boldest woman in medieval Welsh poetry is thirteen copies of a poem about a vulva, kept safe, unread, in the archives of a nation that had decided to be embarrassed by her.

### WEB VERSION — tabard-inn-southwark
The most famous inn in English literature is a scrap of office space now, marked by a plaque on Talbot Yard off Borough High Street. But the Tabard was real. Built for the Abbot of Hyde around 1307, it had a landlord named Henry Bailly by 1381 — and when Geoffrey Chaucer set the departure of his Canterbury pilgrims here around 1387, he named his fictional host Harry Bailly, after the actual man behind the actual bar. Chaucer wasn't a full-time poet; he was Controller of Customs for the port of London from 1374, taxing the wool trade — the single largest source of royal revenue in the period. *The Canterbury Tales* were written around a day job, and they are gloriously rude. The Miller's Tale (seduction, a fake flood, a kiss planted somewhere unexpected in the dark) and the Reeve's Tale (two students, one miller's household, a night of moved beds) are outright *fabliaux* — obscene comic stories told with genius. Most startling is the Wife of Bath, who talks with total frankness about her five husbands, her appetites, and her belief that women should have "sovereignty" in marriage — an argument no English writer would make so boldly again for centuries. The inn burned in the 1676 Southwark fire, was rebuilt and misnamed the Talbot, and was demolished in 1873. Southwark itself lay under the Bishop of Winchester, whose Bankside brothels feature elsewhere in this book.

### WEB VERSION — gwerful-mechain-llanfechain
In a quiet parish in Powys, the only medieval Welsh woman poet with a substantial surviving body of work wrote the frankest poem in the language. Gwerful Mechain (fl. c.1460–1502) composed "Cywydd y Cedor" — "Ode to the Vulva" — in the strictest, most technically demanding Welsh bardic metre, scolding male poets for praising a woman's eyes and hair while ignoring "the best bit," then praising the vulva itself at length. Thirteen manuscript copies survive, held at Bangor University, the British Library, Cardiff Central Library and the National Library of Wales. She traded verses with the leading male poets of her day, answered a misogynist with a defence of women, and wrote a chilling short curse against the husband who beat her. Professor Katie Gramich's 2018 bilingual edition rescued her from what Gramich calls deliberate scholarly suppression. No house survives — only the poems.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The Winchester Geese.** The prostitutes of Bankside were licensed by the Bishop of Winchester, who took a cut of their earnings — which is why medieval Londoners nicknamed them the "Winchester Geese," and why to be "bitten by a Winchester goose" meant to catch a venereal disease. They worked yards from where Chaucer's pilgrims gathered, in a liberty deliberately placed outside the City's moral jurisdiction.

**Dafydd ap Gwilym's answer poem.** Gwerful's "Ode to the Vulva" was a reply, roughly a century late, to Dafydd ap Gwilym's "Cywydd y Gal" — "Ode to the Penis" — a boastful medieval poem in which the poet's own member is compared, at some length and with no modesty whatever, to a variety of unlikely objects. The Welsh bardic tradition had room for both.

**The Cecily Chaumpaigne release.** In 1380 a woman named Cecily Chaumpaigne formally released Chaucer from a charge of "raptus" — a Latin word meaning either rape or abduction. Scholars have argued about it for a century. Recent archival work has suggested the document may relate to a labour dispute rather than an assault, but the record is genuinely ambiguous and the poet's biography carries the shadow.

## 32 — Lady Chatterley's Country
*Renishaw Hall, Derbyshire & Teversal, Nottinghamshire · 1926–1960*

### BOOK VERSION

"The word 'fuck' or 'fucking' appears no less than 30 times... 'cunt' 14 times; 'balls' 13 times; 'shit' and 'arse' six times apiece; 'cock' four times; 'piss' three times, and so on."

That is not a review. That is the prosecution. In October 1960, at the Old Bailey, Mervyn Griffith-Jones — Eton, Cambridge, a decorated war hero who had helped prosecute Nazis at Nuremberg — stood up on behalf of the Crown and read out, to a jury of nine men and three women, a tally of the rude words in a novel. He had counted them. Somewhere in the preparation of the case, a barrister had gone through *Lady Chatterley's Lover* line by line with a pencil, noting on page 204 alone one "fucking," one "shit," three "balls" and a phrase too robust to reproduce in a family history, and totting up the grand totals. It is one of the great images of British officialdom: the state, in a horsehair wig, doing sums about swearing.

The book they were prosecuting had been thirty-two years in making its way to a British bookshop. D. H. Lawrence, a Nottinghamshire miner's son from Eastwood, wrote it three times over between 1926 and 1928, each version franker than the last, at the Villa Mirenda near Florence. Unable to find any English or American publisher willing to touch it, he had the third and rudest version privately printed in Florence in 1928 by a bookseller named Giuseppe "Pino" Orioli, in an edition of a thousand signed copies. It made him more money than he had ever earned, chiefly because it was instantly, comprehensively pirated. Lawrence died in 1930. He never saw it published openly in his own country.

The novel is set in a landscape Lawrence knew in his bones: the coal-and-iron Midlands, all pit-heads and soot and blackened brick. Constance Chatterley is married to Sir Clifford, a baronet paralysed from the waist down by the war, master of Wragby Hall. Into the gap in her life steps Oliver Mellors, the estate's gamekeeper, and what follows is a love affair conducted in plain Anglo-Saxon words — a deliberate choice by Lawrence, who believed the euphemisms were the real obscenity and that calling the thing by its oldest name was a kind of honesty.

Where was Wragby? Two Midlands houses claim it, and the claims are worth separating carefully. Renishaw Hall in Derbyshire, seat of the Sitwell family for four hundred years, is the more glamorous candidate; it is widely said to have been Lawrence's model. The truth is more comic. Lawrence and his wife Frieda visited Renishaw only once, at Sir Osbert Sitwell's invitation, and by Edith Sitwell's own later account they were shown in the front door and straight out the back into the garden — so all Lawrence actually saw of the grand house was the entrance hall. Wragby in the novel is deliberately undistinguished, "a long, low old house... without much distinction," which the actual Renishaw emphatically is not. The other candidate, Teversal in Nottinghamshire — a real colliery village generally taken to be the fictional "Tevershall" — has its own Wragby claimant in the Grade II-listed Teversal Manor. Both identifications are traditional and neither is provable; the honest position is that Lawrence, walking the country around Eastwood, built Wragby out of several places and the inside of his own head.

Then came the trial. The Obscene Publications Act of 1959 had, for the first time, allowed literary merit as a defence, and Penguin Books deliberately printed an unexpurgated Chatterley to test it, handing copies to the police by arrangement. The case, *R v Penguin Books Ltd*, ran at the Old Bailey from 20 October to 2 November 1960, Mr Justice Byrne presiding. The defence called an extraordinary parade of thirty-five expert witnesses — the critic Richard Hoggart, the Bishop of Woolwich, academics, clergymen, a headmaster — to testify to the book's seriousness. The prosecution called one policeman.

And then Griffith-Jones asked his question. Warning the jury not to be priggish, he wondered aloud: "Would you approve of your young sons, young daughters — because girls can read as well as boys — reading this book? Is it a book that you would have lying around in your own house? Is it a book that you would even wish your wife or your servants to read?" The courtroom laughed. It is the single most catastrophic sentence in the history of English advocacy, and its beauty is that it revealed everything: that the prosecutor genuinely imagined a jury of 1960 Britons kept live-in servants, and that the whole apparatus of obscenity law rested on the idea that literature was safe for gentlemen but dangerous for women and the lower orders. Three of the jurors were women. Quite a few of them were exactly the sort of people the question assumed did not exist. On 2 November they acquitted Penguin in three hours. Per Sotheby's, Penguin then sold its entire 200,000-copy print run in a single day, and an estimated two million copies over the following two years; Foyle's shifted its 300 copies in fifteen minutes.

The larger point is the one Philip Larkin made, in a poem this book will gesture at but not quote, dating the start of something to the gap between the end of the Chatterley ban and a certain pop group's first LP. He was joking, and he was not. The most consequential erotic book in modern British history was set in a specific, sooty patch of the East Midlands — Byron's county, as it happens, Newstead lying just up the road — and the fight over whether Britons could read it was the moment the state finally lost the power to decide what the British were allowed to put in front of their own eyes.

### WEB VERSION — teversal-lady-chatterley
Teversal, a colliery village in Nottinghamshire, is generally taken to be the "Tevershall" of *Lady Chatterley's Lover*, and the Grade II-listed Teversal Manor is a rival candidate for the fictional Wragby Hall. D. H. Lawrence grew up a few miles away in Eastwood and walked this coal-scarred country obsessively; the "utter, soulless ugliness of the coal-and-iron Midlands" that horrifies Constance Chatterley is the landscape of his childhood. Neither the Renishaw nor the Teversal identification is provable — Lawrence built Wragby from several places. What is certain is that the novel's 1960 obscenity trial, in which prosecutor Mervyn Griffith-Jones asked the jury whether it was a book they would wish "your wife or your servants to read," ended in acquittal and sold roughly two million copies within two years, effectively ending literary censorship in Britain.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The judge's wife's copy.** The paperback Mr Justice Byrne used at the trial was annotated in the margins — not by him, but by his wife, Lady Dorothy Byrne, who marked the significant passages and carried it into court each day in a small damask bag she had sewn herself. It sold at auction in 2018 for £56,250, and the government placed an export bar to keep it in Britain.

**Lady Ottoline's stonemason.** Some critics trace part of the plot to Lady Ottoline Morrell — the Garsington hostess who appears elsewhere in this book — and her reported affair with "Tiger," a young stonemason brought in to carve plinths for her garden statues. Lawrence knew Morrell well, and she suspected, not without cause, that she had been put into his fiction.

**Thirty-five witnesses, nobody for the defence to fear.** Penguin lined up thirty-five experts to defend the book; the prosecution, unable to find respected intellectuals willing to condemn it, called a single detective inspector. Even Enid Blyton was asked to testify — she declined, explaining that her husband had said no.

## 33 — Burns
*Globe Inn, Dumfries & Highland Mary's Monument, Failford, South Ayrshire · 1786–1796*

### BOOK VERSION

Robert Burns fathered at least twelve children — most accounts say by four different mothers, though the full roll of women in his bed is longer — and he wrote some of the tenderest love songs in the language, and both of those facts are true at once, and the second does not excuse the first. Any honest account of Scotland's national poet has to hold the two in the same hand.

Start at the Globe Inn, down a close off the High Street in Dumfries, established in 1610 and Burns's self-described "favourite howff" — his word for a haunt — from 1789 until his death in 1796. He had come to Dumfries as an exciseman, a taxman, having given up on farming, and the Globe was where he drank, sang, updated his ledgers, and conducted at least one of his affairs. In an upstairs bedroom he scratched verses into the windowpanes with a diamond-tipped stylus, and two of those panes survive there still, along with the chair he sat in — you can, if you visit, be made to recite a line of Burns as the price of sitting in it.

The woman in that room was Anna Park, niece of the landlady, Mrs Hyslop, and a barmaid at the Globe. She was in her early twenties. Their affair produced a daughter, Elizabeth — "Betty" — born on 31 March 1791. Nine days later, Burns's wife Jean Armour bore him a son, William Nicol Burns. Sit with that arithmetic for a moment: the poet had a legitimate child and an illegitimate child born nine days apart. Anna Park's fate is uncertain — one strong tradition holds that she died young, possibly in or soon after childbirth — and the baby was brought to Dumfries and raised by Jean Armour as one of her own. Burns wrote a song for Anna, "The Gowden Locks of Anna," which opens "Yestreen I had a pint o' wine" and which he privately rated among his best. He was, throughout, quite capable of loving the song more reliably than the woman.

Jean Armour is the quiet heroine of the whole story. She had already borne Burns two sets of twins; her father had once torn up the paper by which the couple declared themselves married. And when her husband's love-child by another woman needed a home, she took the baby in and brought her up, reportedly remarking — the line is much quoted and comes down through family tradition rather than a contemporary document, so treat it as attributed folklore — that "our Robin should hae had twa wives." Betty grew up in the household and, after Jean's death, spoke of her adoptive mother with real love.

The other place on the map is a sandstone column on a hillside at Failford in South Ayrshire, marking the traditional spot where, in May 1786, Burns and Mary Campbell — "Highland Mary" — are said to have exchanged vows across a small stream, each holding a Bible, before parting. Here the folklore and the fact need pulling firmly apart. The Bible-and-stream tableau comes largely from R. H. Cromek's account of 1808, published after both parties were dead, and the twentieth-century biographer James Mackay was among those who doubted its embroidered details. What is better documented: Mary was a servant, a dairymaid and nursemaid; Burns wrote several poems to her ("Highland Mary," "To Mary in Heaven," "Will Ye Go to the Indies, My Mary?"); they seem to have planned to emigrate to Jamaica together; and she died at Greenock in October 1786, probably of typhus contracted nursing her brother. The two-volume Bible Burns gave her, inscribed in his hand with his mason's mark, survives and is held at the Robert Burns Birthplace Museum. And the timing is, again, unforgiving: while Burns was exchanging tender vows with Highland Mary, Jean Armour was pregnant with his twins.

There is a darker footnote, and it must be handled with care. When Mary's grave at Greenock was opened in 1920 — the shipyard next door needed the land — the diggers reportedly found, among the remains in the lair, the bottom board of an infant's coffin. This prompted immediate speculation that Mary had died not of fever but in childbirth, carrying Burns's child. It is a genuinely contested point. Later investigation held that the infant's board belonged to a much later burial in the same family lair, in 1827, and one careful modern account concludes flatly that Mary was not pregnant when she died. The truth is unrecoverable, and the speculation tells us at least as much about our appetite for the story as about Mary.

The kirk knew all about Robert Burns. Scotland's Presbyterian church discipline ran on the Kirk Session, a parish court that hauled fornicators before the congregation to be publicly rebuked from the "cutty stool," the stool of repentance — a system uncannily like the Welsh chapel *seiat* that appears elsewhere in this book, a whole nation's machinery for policing what people did in bed. Burns knew the cutty stool intimately. He was rebuked at Mauchline for his affair with the servant Elizabeth Paton, mother of his first child, "dear-bought Bess," born 1785; he did penance again over Jean Armour. And in his poem "The Fornicator" he cheerfully refuses to be shamed, describing standing at the stool beside "my handsome Betsey" with his eye already wandering back to her figure. The kirk session minutes survive; the poet is in the record, unrepentant.

Be careful, though, about calling any of it charming. The roll of women is a roll of servants with little power: Elizabeth Paton; Jean Armour, twice bearing twins; Anna Park; May Cameron, an Edinburgh servant girl left pregnant and effectively abandoned, whom Burns asked a friend to pay off and get "out to some country friends"; Jenny Clow, another servant, who bore him a son and died of tuberculosis, poor and largely forgotten, while the poet fretted in a letter about a silhouette. Burns is the great exception in this chapter — the one figure with no restraint whatever on his desires, a man who wrote about sex as freely as he lived it, which is precisely why the kirk kept dragging him to the stool. His frankness is the mirror image of every thwarted, censored poet in these pages. But the freedom was his. The consequences, mostly, were theirs.

A red sandstone column on a hillside at Failford marks the traditional spot where, in May 1786, Robert Burns and Mary Campbell — "Highland Mary" — are said to have exchanged vows across a stream, each holding a Bible, before parting forever. What is solid: Mary was a servant; Burns wrote several poems to her; they seem to have planned to emigrate to Jamaica; and she died at Greenock in October 1786, probably of typhus. The two-volume Bible Burns gave her, inscribed with his mason's mark, survives at the Robert Burns Birthplace Museum. All this while Jean Armour was pregnant with Burns's twins. When Mary's grave was moved in 1920, an infant's coffin-board was found nearby, sparking a still-contested theory that she died in childbirth — later evidence dated the board to a burial of 1827.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**The cutty stool.** Scotland's kirk sessions punished fornicators by making them sit on the "cutty stool" — the stool of repentance — before the whole congregation to be rebuked by name. Burns endured this at Mauchline. In "The Fornicator" he refuses to be humbled, describing standing at the stool beside his lover with his eye already back on her figure. The surviving session minutes record the national poet as a repeat offender.

**Clarinda and "Ae Fond Kiss."** During a passionate but reportedly unconsummated correspondence with Agnes McLehose — "Clarinda" to his "Sylvander" — Burns wrote one of the great songs of parting, "Ae Fond Kiss," in 1791. It was Clarinda's servant, Jenny Clow, whom Burns got pregnant; she bore his son and died young and poor.

**Verses cut in glass — and near-sedition.** At the Globe, Burns used his diamond stylus to scratch not just love verses but political ones — lines that, given the loyalist panic of the 1790s, could have seen an exciseman of the Crown dismissed or worse for sedition. He scratched his opinions on sex and war into windowpanes in a building where his own employers might read them.

## 34 — 50 Wimpole Street
*50 Wimpole Street, Marylebone, London · 1838–1846*

### BOOK VERSION

In January 1845 a letter arrived at 50 Wimpole Street for Miss Elizabeth Barrett. It was from a poet she had never met, and it began: "I love your verses with all my heart, dear Miss Barrett." Then, having praised the freshness and the music and the brave new thought, it went further and told her he loved her too. Robert Browning was thirty-two, ambitious, comparatively unknown. Elizabeth Barrett was thirty-eight, famous, and an invalid confined to a single second-floor back room in her father's house, where she had spent years on a sofa, dosed with laudanum for a lung complaint and a spinal pain that had never left her since her teens. She had once said her room's shadowy walls looked "so much like Newgate's turned inside out."

What that letter began is probably the most documented courtship in English literature: over the next twenty months the two poets exchanged 573 letters, held since 1930 at Wellesley College's Margaret Clapp Library in Massachusetts. And yet — this is the strange, constrained heart of it — they conducted almost all of it on paper while living a few miles apart, because for a long time they were barely allowed to meet. Browning first visited on 20 May 1845. By his own meticulous count he came to that room ninety-one times. Every visit required the permission of Elizabeth's father.

Edward Moulton-Barrett is one of the great domestic tyrants of the age, and his tyranny took a peculiar form: he had forbidden any of his eleven surviving children ever to marry. Not Elizabeth specifically — all of them. The prohibition was total and, as far as anyone could tell, motiveless beyond the will to keep his family intact and under his hand. So the courtship was carried out in secret, letter by letter, visit by supervised visit, with Elizabeth's maid Elizabeth Wilson as go-between and her spaniel Flush as witness.

The money that paid for that comfortable, oppressive house has to be named plainly, because Elizabeth named it herself. The Barrett fortune came from sugar plantations in Jamaica worked by enslaved people. Elizabeth knew exactly what that meant and was ashamed of it. She was a committed abolitionist; when the Slavery Abolition Act passed in 1833 she declared herself glad the enslaved were "virtually — free," even though her father thought the West Indies would be ruined by it. She wrote, later, to John Ruskin: "I belong to a family of West Indian slaveholders, and if I believed in curses, I should be afraid." She wrote one of the fiercest abolitionist poems in the language, "The Runaway Slave at Pilgrim's Point." Her liberation and her guilt were tangled together: it was partly money that ultimately came to her from the same slave-worked estates that gave her the independence to defy her father.

Defy him she did. On 12 September 1846, Elizabeth slipped out of Wimpole Street with only Wilson for company, walked to St Marylebone Parish Church, and married Robert Browning. Then — and this is the detail that catches the throat — she went home. She returned to her father's house and lived there another week as though nothing had happened, before slipping out a second time, this time for good, with Wilson and Flush, to flee to Italy.

Her father never forgave her. He returned her letters unopened. He disinherited her, as he disinherited every child of his who dared to marry. She never saw him again; when he died in 1857 she was still unpardoned. In Italy her health improved, astonishingly; in 1849, at the age of forty-three, she bore a son. The sonnets she had written in secret during the courtship — she showed them to Browning only in 1849, and he gave them the disguising title *Sonnets from the Portuguese* — contain the most famous opening line of any English love poem, four public-domain words about counting the ways, which everyone can finish and which she wrote about the man she was forbidden to marry. She died in Florence in 1861.

The house is gone. Number 50 Wimpole Street was demolished in the 1930s; a plaque records that Elizabeth Barrett lived there from 1838 to 1846, and the stonework of the building now on the spot is a 1930s addition. When the whole row was pulled down, an American admirer bought the actual front door — the door Robert Browning stood before ninety-one times, waiting to be let in — and shipped it across the Atlantic to Wellesley, which already held the 573 letters. The door and the letters are together. The house is not there at all. It is a very Wimpole Street ending: the love survived, and the prison was demolished.

### MORE STORIES AT THIS SPOT

**Flush, the dog who was kidnapped.** Elizabeth's cocker spaniel Flush, a gift from the writer Mary Russell Mitford, was stolen more than once by a London dog-stealing gang who knew a devoted owner would pay. Elizabeth paid the ransoms, over her family's objections, and took the dog to Italy. Virginia Woolf later wrote his biography, telling the love story from the spaniel's point of view.

**The invalid who may not have needed the sofa.** Elizabeth's decades of illness are real but their cause is disputed — possibly tuberculosis, possibly a spinal injury, possibly worsened by the laudanum prescribed for it. What is striking is how much her health improved once she escaped Wimpole Street for Italy, which has led some to suspect the sickroom was partly a cage.

**The play that made a monster.** In 1930 Rudolf Besier's hit play *The Barretts of Wimpole Street* turned Edward Moulton-Barrett into a byword for the domestic tyrant — so much so that the family's descendants protested at his portrayal. The play, and its film versions, fixed the story in the popular imagination as the great escape it was.

## 35 — Keats House
*Wentworth Place (Keats House), Hampstead, London · 1818–1821*

### BOOK VERSION

Keats knew he was dying the moment he saw the colour. On a night in February 1820, back at Wentworth Place after a cold coach journey home to Hampstead, he coughed, saw the blood on the sheet, and — because he had trained as an apothecary and surgeon and knew exactly what he was looking at — told his friend Charles Brown it was arterial blood. "That drop of blood is my death-warrant," he said. "I must die." He was twenty-four. He had rather less than a year of poetry, and of Fanny, left.

Wentworth Place, in what is now Keats Grove in Hampstead, was built around 1815 as a clever piece of Regency real estate: one handsome white villa that was secretly two houses, divided down the middle by an internal wall. Keats moved into one half in December 1818, sharing with Charles Brown, paying £5 a month and splitting the liquor bill. In April 1819 the other half was taken by a widow, Mrs Brawne, and her children — among them Fanny, eighteen years old. The girl next door was, quite literally, the girl next door: only a party wall between the poet and the young woman he would love to distraction.

He had been sniffy about her at first. In a letter to his brother he called her "beautiful and elegant, graceful, silly, fashionable and strange." But by the summer of 1819 he was writing her letters of an intensity that still stops the reader cold, and that autumn they became secretly engaged. He gave her a ring — a modest thing, gold set with an almandine garnet, possibly his late mother's — and she wore it. It is on display at Keats House today, in the room that was hers, one of the small band of relics through which we know this story.

The garden did its part. Brown recorded that in the spring of 1819 Keats carried a chair out onto the grass under a plum tree, where a nightingale had nested, and in a single morning wrote "Ode to a Nightingale." The plum tree is long gone; a replacement has been planted where it stood, and an ancient mulberry that Keats would have known as a sapling still grows there. When he was too ill to leave the house in early 1820, he lay on a sofa by the window and watched Fanny walk in the garden, telling her by note that he would follow her with his eyes.

Then the arterial blood, and the death sentence he read in it himself. His friends raised money to send him to the warmth of Italy; he left in September 1820, sailing away from Fanny and from Hampstead, and he never saw either again. He could not bring himself to write to her from Rome, and he could not bear to open her letters — the pain, he said, went through him like a spear. He died in Rome on 23 February 1821, aged twenty-five. And here is the fact I have kept back: when Keats was buried, at his own request, Fanny's letters to him went into the coffin, unopened, laid against him in the shroud. Whatever she had written to him in his last months, no one has ever read. He took her words with him into the ground.

Fanny mourned him for years. She cut her hair short, wore black, kept his ring, walked on Hampstead Heath where they had walked, and did not marry until 1833, twelve years after his death. History repaid her badly for it. When Keats's love letters to her — thirty-seven of them survive, among the most naked in the language — were published in 1878, Victorian readers were not moved but scandalised, and the scandal fell not on the dead poet but on the living woman. Matthew Arnold thought the letters should never have been printed; the love-letter of Keats, he wrote, was "the love-letter of a surgeon's apprentice," with "something underbred and ignoble" in it. The assumption, running through decades of male commentary, was that a flighty, shallow girl had made a fool of a genius. Fanny had no defence, because her side of the correspondence was in the coffin. Only in the twentieth century, when her warm, sensible letters to Keats's sister came to light, did her reputation recover.

Wentworth Place is now Keats House, run by the City of London Corporation, its two halves long since knocked into one. The ring is in Fanny's room; a lock of Keats's hair is in the collection; the plum tree stands again, or its descendant does, over the patch of grass where the nightingale sang. It is a small house that holds an enormous quantity of feeling, and the most powerful thing in it is an absence — the letters that were never read, sealed in the dark of a grave in Rome, the one part of this love story that was allowed to stay private.
